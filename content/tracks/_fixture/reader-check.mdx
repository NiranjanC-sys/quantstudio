Every quantum speedup in this curriculum is the same move performed with
different bookkeeping: spread amplitude across many basis states, arrange for the
wrong answers to cancel, and read out what survives. Phase kickback is the
mechanism that lets a controlled operation write into the control instead of the
target, and it is where that cancellation is set up.

<Margin label="Notation">
A relative phase is a phase difference between basis states. A global phase
multiplies every amplitude and is unobservable.
</Margin>

## The one line of algebra

Suppose $U|\psi\rangle = e^{i\varphi}|\psi\rangle$, so $|\psi\rangle$ is an
eigenvector of $U$ with eigenvalue $e^{i\varphi}$. Apply a controlled-$U$ with the
control in $|+\rangle$:

$$
\mathrm{C}U \, |+\rangle|\psi\rangle
= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle|\psi\rangle + |1\rangle U|\psi\rangle\right)
= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\right)|\psi\rangle .
$$

The target came out unchanged. The phase landed on the control. That is the whole
of phase estimation, compressed into one line.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The usual confusion">
Nothing was measured and nothing moved. The eigenvalue was already there. What
the controlled gate does is convert an unobservable global phase on the target
into an observable relative phase on the control.
</Callout>

## Seeing it

Take $U = Z$, whose eigenvector for eigenvalue $-1$ is $|1\rangle$. Put the
control in $|+\rangle$, the target in $|1\rangle$, and apply CZ. The control
should end up in $|-\rangle$.

Read the amplitude table. The two surviving basis states are $|01\rangle$ and
$|11\rangle$, with amplitudes $+1/\sqrt2$ and $-1/\sqrt2$. The control is in
$|-\rangle$ and the target is untouched in $|1\rangle$.

| control before | target | control after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lvert+\rangle$ | $\lvert0\rangle$ | $\lvert+\rangle$ |
| $\lvert+\rangle$ | $\lvert1\rangle$ | $\lvert-\rangle$ |

A Hadamard on the control now turns that phase difference into a measurable bit.
That is Deutsch's algorithm.

<Check question="Why does measuring the control in the computational basis, without the final Hadamard, tell you nothing?">
Because $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ have identical computational-basis
probabilities, both $1/2$. The information is in the relative phase, and the
Hadamard is what rotates phase information into amplitude information.
</Check>

<Solution>
Apply H to the control, then measure. Outcome 0 means the eigenvalue was $+1$,
outcome 1 means it was $-1$, with certainty and one query.
</Solution>
