The Born rule says what a measurement reports. It does not say what the qubits
are afterwards, and in a circuit with more gates to come that is the question
that matters. The answer is the second measurement postulate, and it is the only
non-unitary step in the entire model.

## Project, then renormalise

Measure qubit $q$ of a state $\ket{\psi}$ and suppose the outcome is $b$. Let
$P_b$ be the projector that keeps every basis state whose $q$-th bit is $b$ and
deletes the rest. The post-measurement state is

$$
\ket{\psi'} = \frac{P_b \ket{\psi}}{\left\| P_b \ket{\psi} \right\|},
\qquad \text{where} \quad
\left\| P_b \ket{\psi} \right\|^2 = \Pr[b] .
$$

Two moves, in order. Delete the branches the outcome ruled out. Divide by the
length of what survives, so that the result is a state again. The division is
not optional: the surviving amplitudes have squared sum $\Pr[b] < 1$, and
T1.M1.L5 was about what happens if you forget.

<Margin label="Definition">
The denominator is $\sqrt{\Pr[b]}$, which is why the branch of probability zero
has no post-measurement state at all. The rule is defined only on outcomes that
can occur.
</Margin>

## Worked, on the state from the previous lesson

Take the same preparation, $R_y(2\pi/3)$ on qubit 0 and $H$ on qubit 1:

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.353553 \\ 0.353553 \\ 0.612372 \\ 0.612372 \end{pmatrix}
\quad \text{in the order} \quad \ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11} .
$$

Measure qubit 0 only. The outcome is 1 with probability
$0.375 + 0.375 = 0.75$. Projecting keeps the last two entries:

$$
P_1 \ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.612372 \\ 0.612372 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \left\| P_1 \ket{\psi} \right\| = \sqrt{0.75} = 0.866025 .
$$

Divide:

$$
\ket{\psi'} = \frac{1}{0.866025}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.612372 \\ 0.612372 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0.707107 \\ 0.707107 \end{pmatrix}
= \ket{1} \otimes \ket{+} .
$$

Qubit 0 has become $\ket{1}$, which is what "the measurement said 1" means, and
qubit 1 is untouched at $\ket{+}$. The circuit below prepares that exact state
from scratch, and its amplitudes are the ones just computed.

The other branch is just as easy. Outcome 0 has probability
$0.125 + 0.125 = 0.25$, the surviving vector is $(0.353553, 0.353553, 0, 0)$
with length $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$, and dividing gives
$(0.707107, 0.707107, 0, 0) = \ket{0}\otimes\ket{+}$. In both branches the
renormalisation rescaled by more than 1, since the projection can only shrink.

## Measuring twice changes nothing

The projectors are idempotent, $P_b^2 = P_b$, because deleting the same branches
a second time deletes nothing new. So measuring the same qubit again, on the
state $\ket{\psi'}$, gives outcome $b$ with probability

$$
\left\| P_b \ket{\psi'} \right\|^2
= \left\| \frac{P_b^2 \ket{\psi}}{\sqrt{\Pr[b]}} \right\|^2
= \frac{\left\| P_b \ket{\psi} \right\|^2}{\Pr[b]}
= \frac{\Pr[b]}{\Pr[b]} = 1 ,
$$

and the state is unchanged. Repeated measurement in the same basis is
deterministic after the first shot. This is what makes the outcome usable as a
classical bit: once written down, it stays true, and a later gate conditioned on
it is acting on a fact rather than a guess.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Same basis only">
Repeatability holds for the same measurement, not for any measurement. Measure
$\ket{+}$ in the computational basis, get 0, and the state is $\ket{0}$. Measure
that in the $X$ basis and the outcome is again a coin flip, and the state is
back to $\ket{+}$ or $\ket{-}$. Nothing is stable except with respect to the
basis that was used, a point T1.M2.L3 makes operational.
</Callout>

## Why the circuit stops being reversible

Every gate is unitary, hence invertible: given the output state you can recover
the input by applying $U^{\dagger}$. Collapse is not like that, for two
independent reasons.

It is not linear. The denominator $\sqrt{\Pr[b]}$ depends on $\ket{\psi}$, so
the map $\ket{\psi} \mapsto \ket{\psi'}$ is not a linear map at all, and only
linear maps have matrices to invert.

It is not injective. Measure a single qubit and report outcome 0: the state
$\ket{+}$ becomes $\ket{0}$, and so does $0.6\ket{0} + 0.8\ket{1}$, and so does
every state with a non-zero amplitude on $\ket{0}$. Infinitely many inputs, one
output. No inverse exists, whatever the probabilities were.

The practical consequence is the shape of every circuit in this track:
unitary gates first, measurement last. Anything you wanted to do with the
amplitudes has to be done before the measurement, because afterwards there are
no amplitudes left to work with, only a bit. T1.M8 relaxes this with mid-circuit
measurement, at the cost of having to reason about the branches explicitly.

<Check question="A qubit in state $0.6\ket{0} + 0.8\ket{1}$ is measured and reports 1. It is measured again. What are the two outcome probabilities, and what is the state after the second measurement?">
The first measurement reports 1 with probability $0.64$. Projecting keeps
$0.8\ket{1}$, whose length is $0.8$, and dividing gives $\ket{1}$ exactly. The
second measurement therefore reports 1 with probability 1 and 0 with probability
0, and the state stays $\ket{1}$. The amplitude $0.6$ on $\ket{0}$ is gone for
good; no later gate can recover the original state, because the information that
distinguished it was destroyed rather than moved.
</Check>
