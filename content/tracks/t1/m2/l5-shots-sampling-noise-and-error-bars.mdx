Every figure in this track reports exact probabilities, because a simulator can
compute them. A quantum computer cannot. It returns one bitstring per run, and
the only way to learn a probability is to run the circuit many times and count.
That replaces a number with an estimate, and an estimate without an error bar is
not a result.

## What a shot is

A **shot** is one execution of the circuit: prepare, evolve, measure, record a
bitstring, discard the qubits. The device then starts over from $\ket{0\dots0}$.
Shots are independent and identically distributed, so $N$ shots of a circuit
with outcome probability $p$ produce a count $n \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(N, p)$
and the natural estimator

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{n}{N} .
$$

This estimator is unbiased, $\expect{\hat{p}} = p$, and its variance is the
binomial variance divided by $N^2$:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{N},
\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} .
$$

<Margin label="Rule of thumb">
$p(1-p) \le \tfrac14$ always, so
$\mathrm{SE} \le \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt N}$ for any circuit. At $N = 1024$ that ceiling
is $0.015625$; at $N = 10^6$ it is $0.0005$. Precision costs quadratically:
ten times finer needs one hundred times more shots.
</Margin>

## A worked pair

Consider a circuit whose true outcome probabilities are $0.49$ and $0.51$. It is
prepared by $R_y(1.5907977)$, since $\sin^2(\theta/2) = 0.51$ at that angle.

Run it 1024 times. The expected count of outcome 1 is
$1024 \times 0.51 = 522.24$, with standard deviation
$\sqrt{N p (1-p)} = \sqrt{1024 \times 0.51 \times 0.49} = 16.0$. So a typical
run reports something like $522 \pm 16$, and $\hat{p} = 0.51 \pm 0.0156$. The
error bar is wider than the effect you were trying to see. A run that returned
506 would be entirely consistent with this circuit and also entirely consistent
with a fair coin, and reporting "the device is biased towards 1" on the strength
of it would be wrong.

## How many shots do you need

Fix a difference $\delta$ that you must be able to resolve, and demand that it
is at least two standard errors wide, which is roughly a 95 percent
separation:

$$
2\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} \le \delta
\qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
N \ge \frac{4\,p(1-p)}{\delta^2} .
$$

With $p$ near $\tfrac12$ the numerator is 1 and the rule is simply
$N \ge 1/\delta^2$:

| $\delta$ | shots needed at $p \approx 0.5$ |
| --- | --- |
| $0.05$ | $400$ |
| $0.02$ | $2\,500$ |
| $0.01$ | $10\,000$ |
| $0.001$ | $1\,000\,000$ |

So separating $0.51$ from $0.50$ needs $\delta = 0.01$ and about ten thousand
shots, ten times the default 1024 that most tutorials use. Notice also that
extreme probabilities are cheap: at $p = 0.933013$, the tomography state from
the previous lesson, $4p(1-p)/\delta^2$ with $\delta = 0.02$ is only $625$
shots, because $p(1-p) = 0.0625$ rather than $0.25$.

<Callout tone="warning" title="A zero count is not a zero probability">
If 1024 shots return no instance of an outcome, the estimate is $\hat{p} = 0$
and the naive standard error is also 0, which would claim certainty. It is not
certain. The rule of three gives a 95 percent upper bound of $3/N$, here
$0.0029$, so the honest statement is "below about 0.3 percent", not "impossible".
This matters whenever a lesson claims an amplitude cancels exactly.
</Callout>

## Expectation values carry error bars too

Most of this curriculum reports $\expect{Z}$ rather than a probability. Since
$\expect{Z} = 2p - 1$, the estimator is $\hat{z} = 2\hat{p} - 1$ and the error
doubles:

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{z}) = 2\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \expect{Z}^2}{N}} .
$$

The second form is the useful one. It says an expectation value near $\pm 1$ is
cheap to measure and one near 0 is expensive, and it is why the CHSH estimate of
T1.M6.L2, which combines four correlators each near $\pm 0.707$, needs several
thousand shots per setting before the violation of the classical bound 2 is
larger than its own uncertainty.

## Why one run proves nothing

Three reasons, and they stack.

A single Bernoulli sample carries no information about its own variance. From
one shot you can say the outcome was possible, which you already knew.

Algorithms that are exact in theory are not exact on hardware. Deutsch's
algorithm in T1.M10 returns the right answer with probability 1 in the noiseless
model, and with perhaps $0.95$ on a real device. One shot cannot tell an exact
algorithm from a broken one, and repetition is what turns $0.95$ into a
confident answer.

Post-selection on a single result is how people fool themselves. Running until
the answer looks right and then reporting that run is not evidence; it is a
search over runs. Fix the shot count before you look, report the count and the
error bar with the number, and the claim becomes checkable.

<Check question="A circuit is claimed to output 0 with probability exactly $0.75$. You run 400 shots and see 279 zeros. Is the claim in trouble?">
$\hat{p} = 279/400 = 0.6975$. The standard error under the claim is
$\sqrt{0.75 \times 0.25/400} = \sqrt{0.000469} = 0.02165$. The discrepancy is
$0.75 - 0.6975 = 0.0525$, which is $2.42$ standard errors. That is suggestive
and not conclusive: a deviation this large or larger happens about 1.6 percent
of the time even when the claim is true. The correct response is to run more
shots, not to declare either victory or a bug. At 4000 shots the standard error
falls to $0.00685$ and the same discrepancy would be $7.7$ standard errors,
which would settle it.
</Check>
