A quantum computer's only output is a bitstring. Everything the state contains,
$2^n$ complex numbers of it, has to be squeezed through that one channel, and the
rule governing the squeeze is a single line of arithmetic. It is called the Born
rule, and it is the only place in the theory where probability enters.

## The rule

Let $\ket{\psi} = \sum_{i=0}^{2^n - 1} a_i \ket{i}$ be an $n$-qubit state, where
$\ket{i}$ runs over the $2^n$ computational basis states. Measuring every qubit
in the computational basis returns the bitstring $i$ with probability

$$
\Pr[i] = |a_i|^2 .
$$

That is the whole rule. It says nothing about which outcome you will get on a
given run, only about the distribution, and it is silent about why. The
experimental content of quantum mechanics is this equation plus the rule for how
states evolve.

<Margin label="Convention">
Qubit 0 is the **most significant** bit. For $n$ qubits, basis index $i$ gives
qubit $q$ the value `(i >> (n - 1 - q)) & 1`, so the two-qubit state
$\ket{q_0 q_1}$ reads left to right and $\ket{10}$ is index 2. This matches
Nielsen and Chuang and Cirq. Qiskit reverses it, and T0.M5.L3 covers the
translation.
</Margin>

## A two-qubit worked example

Prepare qubit 0 with $R_y(2\pi/3)$, which gives
$\cos(\pi/3)\ket{0} + \sin(\pi/3)\ket{1} = 0.5\ket{0} + 0.866025\ket{1}$, and
put qubit 1 into $\ket{+}$ with a Hadamard. The joint state is the tensor
product, computed entry by entry:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.866025 \end{pmatrix}
\otimes
\begin{pmatrix} 0.707107 \\ 0.707107 \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.5 \cdot 0.707107 \\
0.5 \cdot 0.707107 \\
0.866025 \cdot 0.707107 \\
0.866025 \cdot 0.707107
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} 0.353553 \\ 0.353553 \\ 0.612372 \\ 0.612372 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Square each entry:

$$
\Pr[00] = 0.125, \quad \Pr[01] = 0.125, \quad
\Pr[10] = 0.375, \quad \Pr[11] = 0.375 .
$$

Those are $\tfrac18, \tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38$, and they sum to 1.

## Marginals come from sums, not from squares

To ask about one qubit alone, add the probabilities of the basis states that
agree with the question. Qubit 0 reads 1 in the states $\ket{10}$ and
$\ket{11}$, so

$$
\Pr[q_0 = 1] = 0.375 + 0.375 = 0.75 ,
$$

which matches $\sin^2(\pi/3) = 0.75$ from the single-qubit picture. Similarly

$$
\Pr[q_1 = 1] = \Pr[01] + \Pr[11] = 0.125 + 0.375 = 0.5 .
$$

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Add probabilities, not amplitudes">
The marginal of qubit 0 is $|a_{10}|^2 + |a_{11}|^2$, not
$|a_{10} + a_{11}|^2$. Amplitudes are added only when the terms describe the
same final outcome of the same experiment. Here $\ket{10}$ and $\ket{11}$ are
different outcomes that merely agree on one bit, so their probabilities add and
their amplitudes do not. Getting this backwards manufactures interference that
is not there.
</Callout>

## The projector form, and why the total is always 1

There is a second way to write the same rule that generalises to the other
measurements of this module. Let $P_i = \ketbra{i}{i}$ be the projector onto
basis state $i$. Then

$$
\Pr[i] = \bra{\psi} P_i \ket{\psi} = \left\| P_i \ket{\psi} \right\|^2 ,
$$

which reduces to $|a_i|^2$ because $P_i \ket{\psi} = a_i \ket{i}$. The
projectors are complete, meaning $\sum_i P_i = I$, and that single fact settles
normalisation once and for all:

$$
\sum_i \Pr[i] = \sum_i \bra{\psi} P_i \ket{\psi}
= \bra{\psi} \left( \sum_i P_i \right) \ket{\psi}
= \braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1 .
$$

The last step is the normalisation constraint of T1.M1.L4. So the Born rule
produces a valid distribution for exactly the states the theory calls valid, and
for no others. Nothing extra had to be assumed.

The same argument shows why gates cannot break it. Every gate is unitary, and
$\braket{U\psi}{U\psi} = \bra{\psi} U^{\dagger} U \ket{\psi} = \braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1$,
so a state that starts normalised stays normalised through any circuit and its
outcome probabilities always sum to 1.

## What the rule does not say

Three clarifications that prevent most early confusion.

It is not a statement about ignorance. The distribution is not there because you
failed to look closely enough. The state $\ket{+}$ is a complete description,
and it still yields a coin flip.

It applies to the state at the moment of measurement, not to any earlier state.
The circuit above has probabilities $0.125$ and $0.375$ only after both gates;
before the Hadamard the distribution over two qubits was $(0.25, 0, 0.75, 0)$.

It says nothing about the state afterwards. That is a separate rule, and it is
the subject of the next lesson.

<Check question="A three-qubit state has amplitude $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ on $\ket{000}$, $\tfrac{1}{2}$ on $\ket{011}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ on $\ket{101}$. Is it normalised, and what is $\Pr[q_1 = 1]$?">
The squared moduli are $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.25$, which sum to 1, so yes. Qubit 1
is the middle bit, so among the three states with non-zero amplitude it reads 1
only in $\ket{011}$, giving $\Pr[q_1 = 1] = 0.25$. The other two marginals are
$\Pr[q_0 = 1] = 0.25$, from $\ket{101}$ alone, and $\Pr[q_2 = 1] = 0.5$, from
$\ket{011}$ and $\ket{101}$ together. Reading the bitstrings in the opposite
order would swap the first and last of those answers, which is why the
convention has to be stated before any number is quoted.
</Check>
