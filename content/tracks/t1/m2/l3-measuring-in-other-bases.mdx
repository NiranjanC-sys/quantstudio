Hardware measures in one basis. Every superconducting or trapped-ion device
reports which of two energy levels a qubit is in, and that is the computational
basis, full stop. Yet half the interesting questions in this track are about
other bases: T1.M1.L7 needed the $X$ basis to see a relative phase, and T1.M6
needs measurement axes at arbitrary angles. The reconciliation is a trick so
routine that it disappears from most write-ups, and it is worth making explicit
once.

## Rotate the question, not the apparatus

A measurement in the orthonormal basis $\{\ket{b_0}, \ket{b_1}\}$ assigns
outcome $k$ the probability $|\braket{b_k}{\psi}|^2$. Let $B$ be the unitary
whose *rows* are the bras $\bra{b_0}, \bra{b_1}$, equivalently the unitary with
$B\ket{b_k} = \ket{k}$. Then

$$
\braket{b_k}{\psi} = \bra{k} B \ket{\psi} ,
$$

so the probability of outcome $k$ in the $b$ basis for the state $\ket{\psi}$
equals the probability of outcome $k$ in the computational basis for the state
$B\ket{\psi}$. Applying $B$ and then measuring normally is not an approximation
of the measurement you wanted; it is that measurement, exactly.

<Margin label="Definition">
$B$ is the **basis-change unitary**, and it satisfies
$B = \sum_k \ketbra{k}{b_k}$. Because $B$ is unitary, the two states you might
have measured, $\ket{\psi}$ and $B\ket{\psi}$, are related reversibly: no
information is lost in the rotation, only relabelled.
</Margin>

## The X basis, and why it is a Hadamard

The $X$ basis is $\ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + \ket{1})$ and
$\ket{-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} - \ket{1})$. The unitary sending
$\ket{+} \mapsto \ket{0}$ and $\ket{-} \mapsto \ket{1}$ is

$$
B = \ketbra{0}{+} + \ketbra{1}{-}
= \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
= H ,
$$

which you can read off the definition or simply recall from T1.M1.L2 that
$H\ket{+} = \ket{0}$ and $H\ket{-} = \ket{1}$. So **H before a measurement is an
X-basis measurement**, and the reported bit 0 means $\ket{+}$ while 1 means
$\ket{-}$. That single sentence is used dozens of times in the rest of the
track.

The probabilities follow from the inner products:

$$
\Pr[+] = \left| \braket{+}{\psi} \right|^2 = \left| \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\sqrt2} \right|^2,
\qquad
\Pr[-] = \left| \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\sqrt2} \right|^2 .
$$

Take the state $R_y(\pi/3)\ket{0} = \cos(\pi/6)\ket{0} + \sin(\pi/6)\ket{1} = 0.866025\ket{0} + 0.5\ket{1}$, whose computational-basis probabilities are
$0.75$ and $0.25$. Then

$$
\Pr[+] = \left( \frac{0.866025 + 0.5}{\sqrt2} \right)^2
= 0.965926^2 = 0.933013 ,
\qquad \Pr[-] = 0.066987 .
$$

gate, two entirely different questions asked of one preparation.

## The Y basis needs two gates

The $Y$ basis is $\ket{+i} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + i\ket{1})$ and
$\ket{-i} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} - i\ket{1})$. Build the basis change in
two steps. First $S^{\dagger} = \mathrm{diag}(1, -i)$ removes the $i$:

$$
S^{\dagger}\ket{+i} = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \cdot i \end{pmatrix}
= \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \ket{+},
\qquad
S^{\dagger}\ket{-i} = \ket{-} .
$$

Then $H$ finishes the job, so $B = H S^{\dagger}$, applied in that order:
$S^{\dagger}$ first, $H$ second. A $Y$-basis measurement is $S^{\dagger}$, then
$H$, then measure.

The state $\ket{+i}$ is a fair coin in both the $Z$ and the $X$ bases and is
certain in the $Y$ basis. Three bases, three completely different verdicts on
one state, which is the operational meaning of the three axes of the Bloch
sphere.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The gate order reverses">
To measure in the basis $\{\ket{b_k}\}$ you apply $B$, and $B$ is built from the
*inverse* of whatever prepares that basis. $\ket{+i}$ is prepared by $H$ then
$S$, so the readout is $S^{\dagger}$ then $H$: the same gates, daggered and in
the opposite order, because $(HS)^{\dagger} = S^{\dagger}H^{\dagger}$. Writing
the preparation and then reversing it is the reliable way to get this right.
</Callout>

## An axis at any angle

Nothing restricts you to the three named axes. To measure along the axis in the
$xz$ plane at angle $\phi$ from $+z$, apply $R_y(-\phi)$ and then measure. The
rotation carries that axis onto $+z$, and $R_y(-\phi)^{\dagger} Z R_y(-\phi) = \cos\phi\, Z + \sin\phi\, X$ is the observable you have measured. Setting
$\phi = \pi/2$ recovers the $X$ basis and shows that $R_y(-\pi/2)$ is an
alternative to $H$, differing from it only by which outcome gets called 0.
T1.M6.L1 uses exactly this construction with four different angles to violate a
Bell inequality.

<Check question="A qubit is in $\ket{0}$. What are its outcome probabilities in the X basis and in the Y basis, and what is the state after an X-basis measurement reporting 1?">
$\braket{+}{0} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\braket{-}{0} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so
the $X$-basis outcomes are $0.5$ and $0.5$. The same holds in the $Y$ basis,
since $|\braket{\pm i}{0}|^2 = \tfrac12$. Outcome 1 in the $X$ basis means the
state was projected onto $\ket{-}$, so the qubit is now $\ket{-}$, not
$\ket{1}$. If you implemented the measurement as $H$ then a computational
measurement, the physical qubit is left in $\ket{1}$ and you must apply $H$
again to return it to the $\ket{-}$ the measurement actually prepared.
</Check>
