Alice and a middle station share a Bell pair. The middle station and Bob share a
different Bell pair. Alice and Bob have never met, never exchanged a photon, and
hold qubits that have never interacted with each other in any way. Can they end
up entangled? They can, and the middle station can make it happen without ever
learning anything about either qubit. This is entanglement swapping, and it is
the operation that makes long-distance quantum links possible at all.

## Four qubits, two pairs

Number the qubits 0 to 3. Qubits 0 and 1 form one Bell pair, qubits 2 and 3 the
other:

$$
\ket{\Phi^+}_{01} \otimes \ket{\Phi^+}_{23}
= \frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt2} \otimes \frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Alice holds qubit 0. Bob holds qubit 3. The middle station holds qubits 1 and 2,
one from each pair. Expanded, the joint state has four equal terms of amplitude
$\tfrac12$:

$$
\half\big(\ket{0000} + \ket{0011} + \ket{1100} + \ket{1111}\big),
$$

reading $\ket{q_0 q_1 q_2 q_3}$. Qubit 0 is perfectly correlated with qubit 1 and
qubit 2 with qubit 3, and there is no correlation at all between qubit 0 and
qubit 3. Their joint reduced state is the uniform mixture over all four outcomes.

<Margin label="Convention">
Qubit 0 is the most significant bit. Alice holds $q_0$, Bob holds $q_3$, and the
repeater station holds $q_1 q_2$ and measures them in the Bell basis.
</Margin>

## The measurement that does the work

The station measures its two qubits in the Bell basis. As in teleportation, that
means CNOT from qubit 1 to qubit 2, Hadamard on qubit 1, then a computational
measurement of both. Here is the state after the basis change and before the
measurement.

Eight basis states survive, each with amplitude $\pm\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2} = \pm 0.353553$ and probability $0.125$. Sort them by the middle two labels
$q_1 q_2$, which is what the station is about to read:

| $q_1 q_2$ | surviving strings | Alice and Bob hold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $00$ | $+\ket{0000}$, $+\ket{1001}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^+}$ |
| $01$ | $+\ket{0011}$, $+\ket{1010}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} + \ket{10}) = \ket{\Psi^+}$ |
| $10$ | $+\ket{0100}$, $-\ket{1101}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^-}$ |
| $11$ | $+\ket{0111}$, $-\ket{1110}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{10}) = \ket{\Psi^-}$ |

Read the third row against the figure. The strings with $q_1 q_2 = 10$ are
$\ket{0100}$ with amplitude $+0.353553$ and $\ket{1101}$ with amplitude
$-0.353553$. Stripping the middle two labels leaves $q_0 q_3 = 00$ with a plus
sign and $q_0 q_3 = 11$ with a minus sign, which is $\ket{\Phi^-}$ normalised by
the factor $\sqrt2$ that renormalisation supplies after the measurement collapses
the branch.

Two qubits that never interacted are now maximally entangled. Nothing acted on
qubit 0 or qubit 3 at any point in the circuit; check the operation list if you
doubt it.

## The correction table

Writing the outcome as $q_1 = a$ and $q_2 = b$, Alice and Bob are left holding
$(I \otimes X^{b} Z^{a})\ket{\Phi^+}$. To standardise on $\ket{\Phi^+}$, Bob
applies the inverse to his qubit: $X$ first if $b = 1$, then $Z$ if $a = 1$.

| $a b$ | resulting state | Bob applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $00$ | $\ket{\Phi^+}$ | nothing |
| $01$ | $\ket{\Psi^+}$ | $X$ |
| $10$ | $\ket{\Phi^-}$ | $Z$ |
| $11$ | $\ket{\Psi^-}$ | $X$ then $Z$ |

That is the same table as teleportation, which is not a coincidence: swapping
*is* teleportation, with the teleported state being half of an entangled pair
rather than an independent message. The station teleports qubit 2 onto qubit 0
using the pair it shares with Alice, and qubit 2 was already tied to Bob.

<Callout tone="note" title="Why the station learns nothing">
All four outcomes occur with probability $\tfrac14$ regardless of anything else in
the system, so the two bits the station publishes are uniformly random. They are
not a measurement *of* Alice's or Bob's qubit, and the station cannot use them to
reconstruct any property of the link it just created.
</Callout>

## Why repeaters need this

Photon loss in fibre is exponential in distance. Send a photon 100 km through
good fibre and only a small fraction arrives; send it 1000 km and essentially
none does. A classical repeater fixes this by measuring the signal and
retransmitting, which for a quantum state is exactly the operation no-cloning
forbids.

Swapping is the way out. Divide the 1000 km into segments short enough that
entanglement can be established across each one with a workable success
probability. Each segment is attempted independently and repeatedly until it
succeeds, and success is heralded, meaning the station knows when it happened.
Then swap adjacent segments together. Two segments become one link of double the
length, four become one of quadruple, and the number of swapping levels grows
logarithmically in the number of segments.

The catch, and it is the reason the quantum internet is hard, is that a qubit
waiting for its neighbouring segment to succeed has to *stay* coherent. That
turns the engineering problem into a quantum memory problem, which is why the
hardware track spends a whole module on memories and why T1.M11 starts measuring
coherence times four modules from now.

<Check question="The station measures and gets $a b = 11$, but never tells anybody. What do Alice and Bob share?">
Nothing usable. Conditioned on the outcome they hold $\ket{\Psi^-}$, which is
maximally entangled, but without the two classical bits they cannot know which of
the four Bell states they have. Averaged over the four equally likely branches
their joint state is the maximally mixed state on two qubits, which has no
entanglement at all. The entanglement is real only once the heralding message
arrives, which is again why swapping never signals faster than light.
</Check>
