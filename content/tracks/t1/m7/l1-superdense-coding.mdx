How many classical bits can one qubit carry? The honest answer is one. That is
the Holevo bound, and it is a theorem. So what is going on in a protocol whose
name promises two bits per qubit? The answer is that the accounting is done over
the wrong interval. Superdense coding is the first protocol where entanglement
stops being a curiosity and starts being a *resource you spend*, and the
bookkeeping is the lesson.

## The setup

Alice wants to send Bob two classical bits, $b_0 b_1 \in \{00, 01, 10, 11\}$. She
is allowed to send exactly one qubit. Before she knows what her message is,
somebody prepares the Bell state

$$
\ket{\Phi^+} = \frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

and gives qubit 0 to Alice and qubit 1 to Bob. That distribution step can happen
a year in advance, in a van, on a fibre, whenever. It carries no message,
because at that moment nobody knows the message.

<Margin label="Convention">
Qubit 0 is the most significant bit, so $\ket{q_0 q_1}$ reads left to right.
Alice always holds qubit 0 and Bob always holds qubit 1.
</Margin>

## Alice's four moves

Alice applies one of four operations to her own qubit only. She never touches
Bob's. Write the operation as $Z^{b_0}$ followed by $X^{b_1}$:

| message | Alice applies | shared state becomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $00$ | $I$ | $\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$ |
| $01$ | $X$ | $\ket{\Psi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} + \ket{10})$ |
| $10$ | $Z$ | $\ket{\Phi^-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{11})$ |
| $11$ | $XZ$ | $-\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-\ket{01} + \ket{10})$ |

Take the third row slowly, because it is where the trick lives. $Z$ acts only on
qubit 0, so it negates every term whose *first* label is 1:

$$
(Z \otimes I)\frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt2}
= \frac{\ket{00} - \ket{11}}{\sqrt2} = \ket{\Phi^-}.
$$

A gate applied to one half of an entangled pair has changed the global state, and
it has changed it into a state that is *perfectly distinguishable* from the one
before. That is not true of product states: acting on your half of
$\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}$ can never produce a state orthogonal to the original
unless you flip your own qubit, and then you have only two options, not four.
Entanglement is what turns one qubit of local freedom into four global outcomes.

The last row picks up a minus sign because $XZ = -ZX$, and the ordering matters
for the algebra even though it will not matter for the measurement. Keep the sign
and watch what happens to it.

## Bob's decoder

Bob receives Alice's qubit and now holds both. The four Bell states are
orthonormal, so a measurement in the Bell basis distinguishes them with
certainty. The circuit that rotates the Bell basis onto the computational basis
is the Bell-preparation circuit run backwards: a CNOT from qubit 0 to qubit 1,
then a Hadamard on qubit 0.

Message $11$ arrives as $11$ with probability 1. If you switch the figure to the
amplitude view you will see the amplitude of $\ket{11}$ reported as $-1$ rather
than $+1$. That is the minus sign from $XZ = -ZX$, and it is a global phase on a
one-term state, so it is unobservable. Four messages, four certain outcomes, one
qubit in transit.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The three-step reading">
Superdense coding is often drawn as one circuit, which hides the point. It is
three separated events: **distribute** the pair (no message exists yet),
**encode** locally (no communication), **send one qubit and decode**. Only the
third step is communication.
</Callout>

## Where the missing bit went

Now the accounting. Over the whole protocol, two qubits pass between the parties:
Bob's half of the Bell pair travelled to Bob at distribution time, and Alice's
half travelled to Bob at send time. Two qubits, two bits. Holevo is untouched.

What superdense coding actually buys is *scheduling*. It moves half the quantum
communication cost into the past, to a moment when the channel was idle and the
message did not exist. That is a real engineering win, and it is the same
resource pattern you will meet again in teleportation, where the trade runs the
other way.

<Margin label="Definition">
The **Holevo bound** says that a quantum system of dimension $2^n$ can carry at
most $n$ bits of accessible classical information. It bounds what a *receiver*
can extract, not what a state can encode.
</Margin>

## What breaks if you drop a piece

Two failure modes are worth checking against the algebra.

Suppose Alice and Bob share no entanglement and Alice simply prepares a fresh
qubit. Her four options are four states of one qubit in $\C^2$. Any four states
in a two-dimensional space include a pair with non-zero overlap, so Bob's
decoding cannot be certain. The best he can do is one bit.

Suppose instead the pair was prepared but Bob's half decohered into a classical
coin flip. Then the state Bob holds is a mixture, the CNOT and Hadamard have
nothing coherent to interfere, and the outcome distribution is uniform over all
four bitstrings. You will measure exactly that failure in T1.M11 when a noisy
Bell pair replaces a perfect one.

<Check question="Alice applies $Z$ to her qubit while Bob's half is a thousand kilometres away. Has she signalled to Bob?">
No. Bob's local state is unchanged by anything Alice does to her qubit alone. His
reduced state was the maximally mixed state $\tfrac{1}{2}I$ before her gate and it
is still $\tfrac{1}{2}I$ after. The four global states $\ket{\Phi^\pm}$ and
$\ket{\Psi^\pm}$ all have the same reduced state on qubit 1, which is precisely
why no measurement Bob performs alone can tell them apart. The information becomes
available only when Alice's physical qubit arrives, and that arrival is the
communication.
</Check>
