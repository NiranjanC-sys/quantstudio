Every anti-counterfeiting measure ever printed on a banknote has the same flaw:
the note is classical, so a good enough copier reproduces it. Holograms and
microprinting raise the cost of copying, they do not make it impossible. What
would a banknote look like if copying it were forbidden by physics rather than
made expensive by engineering? That question is older than quantum computing
itself, and the answer is the first quantum cryptographic protocol anybody wrote
down.

## The scheme

A banknote is a classical serial number $s$ together with $n$ qubits. For each
position $i$ the bank draws two secret random bits: a basis bit $\theta_i$ and a
value bit $v_i$. It prepares qubit $i$ in one of four states:

| $\theta_i$ | $v_i$ | prepared state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $\ket{0}$ |
| $0$ | $1$ | $\ket{1}$ |
| $1$ | $0$ | $\ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + \ket{1})$ |
| $1$ | $1$ | $\ket{-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} - \ket{1})$ |

The bank stores $(s, \theta, v)$ in a private table and hands the note over. The
holder cannot read $\theta$ or $v$ from the note, because the four states are not
mutually orthogonal: $\braket{0}{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so no measurement
distinguishes $\ket{0}$ from $\ket{+}$ with certainty.

<Margin label="Two bases">
$\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}\}$ is the computational or $Z$ basis.
$\{\ket{+}, \ket{-}\}$ is the $X$ basis. Measuring in the $X$ basis means
applying $H$ and then measuring in the computational basis, because
$H\ket{+} = \ket{0}$ and $H\ket{-} = \ket{1}$.
</Margin>

## Verification

The note comes back to the bank. The bank looks up $\theta$ and $v$ by serial
number, measures qubit $i$ in basis $\theta_i$, and checks that every outcome
equals $v_i$. A genuine note passes with probability 1, because measuring a state
in the basis it was prepared in returns the preparation with certainty.

## The forger

A forger holds one note and wants two. No-cloning already rules out the obvious
attack: there is no unitary that maps $\ket{\psi}\ket{0} \mapsto
\ket{\psi}\ket{\psi}$ for all $\ket{\psi}$. But no-cloning is a statement about
exactness, and a forger does not need exactness. He needs to pass verification.
So measure and guess.

The forger picks a basis at random for each qubit, measures, and prepares two
copies of whatever he saw. Follow one qubit through, in the case where the bank
prepared $\ket{+}$ and the forger guesses the computational basis:

A coin flip. The forger now holds $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$, prepares copies of it,
and the bank later measures one of those copies in the $X$ basis. Whichever of
$\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$ he re-prepared, the verification measurement is again a
coin flip:

| outcome | probability |
| --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0.500000$ |

Now assemble the per-qubit success probability. The forger guesses the basis
correctly with probability $\tfrac12$, in which case his measurement returns
$v_i$, his copies are exact, and verification passes with probability 1. He
guesses wrongly with probability $\tfrac12$, in which case verification passes
with probability $\tfrac12$. So

$$
\Pr[\text{one qubit passes}] = \half \cdot 1 + \half \cdot \half = \frac{3}{4}.
$$

The qubits are independent, so a forged note of $n$ qubits passes with
probability $(3/4)^n$. That number falls off a cliff:

| $n$ | $(3/4)^n$ |
| --- | --- |
| $4$ | $0.316406$ |
| $8$ | $0.100113$ |
| $20$ | $0.003171$ |
| $40$ | $0.000010$ |

Forty qubits per note and a forger who measures and guesses succeeds about once
in a hundred thousand attempts. Nothing about that argument depends on the
forger's computational power, his budget, or how long he waits. It depends only
on the overlap between two non-orthogonal states, and $\tfrac34$ is also the best
any attack can do on a single qubit of the scheme.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Measure-and-resend is not cloning">
The forger never violated no-cloning. He produced two states that are usually
wrong. No-cloning says he cannot produce two that are always right; the $3/4$
calculation says how wrong "usually wrong" is, and that quantitative version is
what a security proof actually needs.
</Callout>

## The assumption that does not survive contact with a shop

The scheme above requires the bank to hold $\theta$ and $v$ secret and to perform
verification itself. That is fine for a bank and useless for a cafe. Suppose we
try to make the note publicly verifiable by publishing $\theta$ so that anybody
can check it.

That destroys the scheme immediately. A forger who knows $\theta_i$ measures
qubit $i$ in the correct basis every time, learns $v_i$ with certainty, and
prepares as many perfect copies as he likes. The security came entirely from the
verifier knowing something the holder does not. Publish it and the note becomes
classical information, which copies freely.

So Wiesner money is *private-key* quantum money: verification requires the secret,
therefore it requires talking to the bank. Making money that anybody can verify
without a secret is a genuinely different problem, and every proposal for it
rests on a computational hardness assumption in addition to the physics. The
physics alone gets you unforgeability against an unbounded adversary, but only if
the verifier is trusted.

<Check question="A forger measures every qubit of a 20-qubit note in the $X$ basis, rather than in a randomly chosen basis. Does he do better or worse than the random strategy?">
Neither. His success probability is still $(3/4)^{20} \approx 0.003171$. The bank
drew each $\theta_i$ uniformly and independently, so from the forger's point of
view every position is equally likely to be a $Z$ position or an $X$ position. On
the $X$ positions he is exactly right and passes with probability 1; on the $Z$
positions he destroys the value and passes with probability $\tfrac12$. Averaging
gives $\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34$ per qubit, the same
as guessing. There is no clever basis to pick, because the secret he would need in
order to pick one is exactly the secret the scheme withholds.
</Check>
