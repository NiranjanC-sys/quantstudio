Quantum money made non-orthogonality into an unforgeability argument, but it
needed a bank holding a secret table. What if the two parties have no shared
secret at all and want to end up with one? BB84 answers that, and its security
argument is the same $\tfrac34$ calculation you did last lesson, read backwards:
the forger's advantage becomes the eavesdropper's fingerprint.

## The protocol, in the order you will code it

1. Alice draws two random bit strings, values $v$ and bases $\theta^A$, each of
   length $N$. She prepares qubit $i$ in $\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{+}$ or $\ket{-}$
   according to $(\theta^A_i, v_i)$, exactly as the bank did, and sends it.
2. Bob draws his own random basis string $\theta^B$, measures qubit $i$ in basis
   $\theta^B_i$, and records the outcome $w_i$.
3. Alice and Bob announce $\theta^A$ and $\theta^B$ over a public authenticated
   channel. They keep the positions where $\theta^A_i = \theta^B_i$ and discard
   the rest. This is **sifting**, and it keeps about half the positions.
4. On the kept positions, $w_i = v_i$ whenever the channel is undisturbed.
5. They disclose a random sample of the sifted positions and compare. The
   fraction that disagree is the **quantum bit error rate**, or QBER. If it is
   too high they abort. Disclosed positions are then discarded.

<Margin label="Why announcing bases is safe">
The basis string says which measurement was made, not what came out. An
eavesdropper who learns $\theta^A$ after the qubits have already been measured is
too late: her measurement choice was locked in before the announcement.
</Margin>

## Where the error rate comes from

When the bases match and nobody interferes, Bob's outcome is deterministic. Alice
prepares $\ket{+}$, Bob measures in the $X$ basis, and the outcome is 0 with
probability 1.

Now insert Eve, who measures every qubit in a basis she picks at random and
re-prepares what she saw. This is the identical attack from the money lesson.
Condition on a sifted position, so Alice and Bob agree on the basis.

- Eve guesses the same basis, probability $\tfrac12$. She learns $v_i$, her
  re-prepared qubit is exactly what Alice sent, and Bob's outcome is correct.
  Error probability 0.
- Eve guesses the other basis, probability $\tfrac12$. Her outcome is a fair coin,
  and the state she re-prepares is unbiased in Alice's basis, so Bob's outcome is
  a fair coin too. Error probability $\tfrac12$.

$$
\mathrm{QBER} = \half \cdot 0 + \half \cdot \half = \frac{1}{4}.
$$

Eve's measurement collapses $\ket{+}$ onto $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$ with equal
probability, and either of those is an equal superposition in the $X$ basis, so
Bob's bit is a fair coin. Over many runs Bob disagrees with Alice on a quarter of
the sifted positions.

A QBER of $0.25$ on a channel whose hardware error floor is a few percent is not
subtle. That is the entire security argument at this level: Eve cannot look
without leaving a mark, and the mark is quantitative.

<Callout tone="warning" title="What this argument does not prove">
Intercept-resend is one attack. A full BB84 security proof bounds Eve's
information for *every* attack allowed by quantum mechanics, and it does so by
relating the QBER to the amount of privacy amplification needed. The lab below
detects the textbook attack; it is not a proof of security.
</Callout>

## What to implement

Two pure functions, no quantum simulation required. The protocol reduces to
classical post-processing once the measurement outcomes exist.

```python
def sift(
    alice_bases: list[int],
    bob_bases: list[int],
    alice_bits: list[int],
    bob_bits: list[int],
) -> tuple[list[int], list[int]]:
    """Keep positions where the two bases agree."""


def qber(sample_a: list[int], sample_b: list[int]) -> float:
    """Fraction of disclosed positions whose bits disagree."""
```

`sift` returns the pair of kept bit lists, in the original order, keeping index
$i$ exactly when `alice_bases[i] == bob_bases[i]`. Both returned lists have the
same length, and on a clean channel they are equal.

`qber` counts disagreements over the disclosed sample and divides by its length.
An empty sample returns $0.0$ rather than raising, because a run that discloses
nothing has observed no errors, not an undefined error rate.

Worked cases the public tests use:

| input | expected |
| --- | --- |
| bases $[0,0,1,1]$ and $[0,1,1,0]$ | two positions kept, indices 0 and 2 |
| identical samples | QBER $0.0$ |
| one disagreement in four disclosed positions | QBER $0.25$ |

The hidden tests add a property check: every position your `sift` keeps must have
matching bases. Returning too much is a bigger failure than returning too little,
because a sifted key containing mismatched-basis positions is half noise and the
QBER estimate built on it is meaningless.

<Check question="Alice and Bob sift 1000 positions and measure a QBER of $0.02$. Should they abort?">
No, and the reason is that $0.02$ is far below the $0.25$ that full
intercept-resend produces and is consistent with ordinary hardware error. What
they must do instead is account for it: a non-zero QBER means Eve *may* hold some
partial information, so they run error correction to reconcile the two keys and
then privacy amplification to compress the reconciled key down to a length short
enough that Eve's residual knowledge is negligible. The threshold for aborting is
set by how much compression the protocol can afford, not by whether the QBER is
exactly zero, which on real hardware it never is.
</Check>
