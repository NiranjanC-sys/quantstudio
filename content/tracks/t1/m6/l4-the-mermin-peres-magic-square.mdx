The GHZ game needed three players. The magic square needs two, wins with
probability 1 against a classical ceiling of $8/9$, and its impossibility proof
is a puzzle you can hand to someone with no physics at all: fill a
$3 \times 3$ grid with $\pm 1$ so that every row multiplies to $+1$, the first
two columns multiply to $+1$, and the last column multiplies to $-1$.

## The puzzle has no solution

Suppose such a grid existed, with entries $v_{rc} \in \{+1, -1\}$. Multiply all
nine entries in two different orders.

By rows: the product is the product of the three row products,
$(+1)(+1)(+1) = +1$.

By columns: it is the product of the three column products,
$(+1)(+1)(-1) = -1$.

Both compute the product of the same nine numbers, so $+1 = -1$. No grid exists.
The obstruction is a single sign, and it does not depend on which cells you try:
any five of the six constraints can be met, and the sixth then fails.

<Margin label="The game">
A referee sends Alice a row index and Bob a column index, each uniform over
$\{1,2,3\}$. Alice returns three signs whose product satisfies her row's rule;
Bob returns three signs satisfying his column's rule. They win when the two
agree at the cell where the row meets the column. A deterministic classical
strategy is exactly a grid, so it must fail on at least one of the nine
question pairs: the classical ceiling is $8/9 \approx 0.888889$.
</Margin>

## Nine observables that do satisfy it

Now replace the numbers by two-qubit observables:

|  | column 1 | column 2 | column 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **row 1** | $Z \otimes I$ | $I \otimes Z$ | $Z \otimes Z$ |
| **row 2** | $I \otimes X$ | $X \otimes I$ | $X \otimes X$ |
| **row 3** | $Z \otimes X$ | $X \otimes Z$ | $Y \otimes Y$ |

Every entry is Hermitian with eigenvalues $\pm 1$, so each is a legitimate
$\pm 1$-valued measurement. Check the six products.

Rows 1 and 2 are immediate: $(Z \otimes I)(I \otimes Z) = Z \otimes Z$, and
squaring gives $I$. Row 3 needs the Pauli algebra of T1.M3.L1:

$$
(Z \otimes X)(X \otimes Z) = (ZX) \otimes (XZ) = (iY) \otimes (-iY) = Y \otimes Y ,
$$

so multiplying by the third entry gives $(Y \otimes Y)^2 = I$. Columns 1 and 2
work the same way and give $+I$. Column 3 is the interesting one:

$$
(Z \otimes Z)(X \otimes X) = (ZX) \otimes (ZX) = (iY) \otimes (iY) = -\, Y \otimes Y ,
$$

so multiplying by $Y \otimes Y$ gives $-I$. The operators reproduce the
impossible sign pattern that numbers cannot.

The reason there is no contradiction is that operators need not commute, and the
"product of all nine" is not well defined for them. Within a row or a column,
however, all three *do* commute: check row 3, where
$(Z \otimes X)(X \otimes Z) = Y \otimes Y = (X \otimes Z)(Z \otimes X)$, the two
anticommutations on the separate factors cancelling. Commuting observables have
simultaneous eigenvectors, so all three entries of a line have definite values
at once and their product is the product of those values.

<Callout tone="note" title="Contextuality, stated once">
Each cell belongs to a row and to a column. Classically its value is the same in
both. Quantum mechanically the value obtained depends on which *other*
observables are measured alongside it, that is, on the context. The magic square
is the cleanest demonstration that measurement outcomes cannot be
context-independent, and it needs no entanglement to state, only to play.
</Callout>

## Commuting observables have joint values

Before the strategy, see the mechanism on one Bell pair. $\ket{\Phi^+}$ is a
$+1$ eigenstate of $Z \otimes Z$ and simultaneously of $X \otimes X$, which is
possible because those two commute.

## The winning strategy

Alice and Bob share **two** Bell pairs. Alice holds one qubit of each, Bob holds
the other two. Given row $r$, Alice measures the three observables of that row on
her pair; they commute, so she can. Given column $c$, Bob measures the three
observables of that column on his pair. Each reports the three signs obtained.

Alice's row product is $+1$ by the row identity, and Bob's column product is the
required sign by the column identity, so both answers are always valid. The only
question is whether they agree at the intersection, and they do: on two Bell
pairs, Alice and Bob measuring the *same* real symmetric observable always get
the same outcome. All nine entries of the square are real symmetric matrices,
including $Y \otimes Y$, whose entries are real because the two factors of $i$
multiply out.

Here is the intersection check for row 1 and column 1, where the shared cell is
$Z \otimes I$. Alice measures both her qubits in the $Z$ basis; Bob measures his
first in $Z$ and his second in $X$, which yields all three of
$Z \otimes I$, $I \otimes X$ and $Z \otimes X$. Qubits 0 and 1 are Alice's,
qubits 2 and 3 are Bob's, and the pairs are 0 with 2 and 1 with 3.

Every listed outcome has bit 0 equal to bit 2. Alice's $Z \otimes I$ value and
Bob's $Z \otimes I$ value always match, which is the win condition for this
question pair. The same holds for all nine, so the strategy wins with
probability 1 against a classical ceiling of $8/9$.

<Check question="Why does the argument not also forbid the quantum strategy? Both Alice and Bob are, after all, producing signs for the same nine cells.">
Because there is no single grid. Alice fills only the row she was asked about and
Bob only his column, and the values Alice would have produced for a different
row are not defined. Cells sharing a row or a column commute and can be measured
together; cells in different rows *and* different columns generally cannot. Take
$Z \otimes I$ at row 1 column 1 and $X \otimes I$ at row 2 column 2: their
first factors anticommute, so the two observables anticommute and share no
eigenvector. The impossibility proof needs all nine values to exist at once, and
quantum mechanics never supplies them; it supplies three at a time,
consistently, whichever three you ask for.
</Check>
