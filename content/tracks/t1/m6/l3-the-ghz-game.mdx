CHSH beats the classical bound by a factor of $\sqrt2$, which means telling the
two apart requires counting. With three players there is a game whose quantum
strategy wins *every* round and whose best classical strategy loses one round in
four. A single loss then refutes the classical model outright, with no error
bars anywhere.

## The rules

Three players, Alice, Bob and Charlie, are sent bits $x$, $y$ and $z$ chosen
uniformly from the four strings with even parity:

$$
(x, y, z) \in \{ 000,\ 011,\ 101,\ 110 \} .
$$

They answer with bits $a$, $b$, $c$ and win when

$$
a \oplus b \oplus c = x \vee y \vee z ,
$$

which is 0 for the question $000$ and 1 for the other three. As before they may
agree on anything in advance and may not communicate once the questions are
sent.

<Margin label="Why even-parity questions">
The restriction is what makes the four constraints contradictory. Dropping it
would add questions whose constraints are consistent with the rest, and the
classical bound would rise. Every nonlocal game is a set of constraints chosen
so that the classical model over-commits.
</Margin>

## The classical bound is exactly three in four

A deterministic strategy is six bits: $f(0), f(1)$ for Alice, $g(0), g(1)$ for
Bob, $h(0), h(1)$ for Charlie. The four win conditions are

$$
f(0) \oplus g(0) \oplus h(0) = 0 ,
$$
$$
f(0) \oplus g(1) \oplus h(1) = 1 ,
$$
$$
f(1) \oplus g(0) \oplus h(1) = 1 ,
$$
$$
f(1) \oplus g(1) \oplus h(0) = 1 .
$$

Add all four modulo 2. On the left, each of the six values appears exactly
twice, so everything cancels and the sum is 0. On the right the sum is
$0 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 \equiv 1$. So $0 = 1$: the four conditions cannot all hold,
and any deterministic strategy loses at least one question. Since the four
questions are equally likely and a randomised strategy is an average of
deterministic ones,

$$
\Pr[\text{win}] \le \frac{3}{4} .
$$

The bound is achieved by, for instance, always answering $a = 1$, $b = 0$,
$c = 0$, which wins on the three odd-parity questions and loses on $000$.

## The quantum strategy wins always

The players share the GHZ state

$$
\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}} = \frac{\ket{000} + \ket{111}}{\sqrt2} ,
$$

prepared by a Hadamard on qubit 0 and CNOTs into qubits 1 and 2. On question bit
0 a player measures $X$; on question bit 1 a player measures $Y$. Each reports
the outcome bit, with 0 meaning the eigenvalue $+1$.

The strategy works because of four operator identities:

$$
X \otimes X \otimes X \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}} = + \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}} ,
$$
$$
X \otimes Y \otimes Y \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}
= Y \otimes X \otimes Y \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}
= Y \otimes Y \otimes X \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}
= - \ket{\mathrm{GHZ}} .
$$

Check the second by hand. $Y\ket{0} = i\ket{1}$ and $Y\ket{1} = -i\ket{0}$, so

$$
(X \otimes Y \otimes Y)\ket{000} = \ket{1} \otimes i\ket{1} \otimes i\ket{1} = -\ket{111} ,
$$
$$
(X \otimes Y \otimes Y)\ket{111} = \ket{0} \otimes (-i\ket{0}) \otimes (-i\ket{0}) = -\ket{000} ,
$$

and the two terms combine to $-\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}$.

Now translate. The GHZ state is a $+1$ eigenstate of $XXX$, so the product of the
three $\pm 1$ outcomes on question $000$ is $+1$ with certainty, which means
$a \oplus b \oplus c = 0$. It is a $-1$ eigenstate of $XYY$, $YXY$ and $YYX$, so
on each of the other three questions the product is $-1$ and the parity is 1.
All four win conditions hold with probability 1.

An $X$ measurement is a Hadamard before readout and a $Y$ measurement is
$S^{\dagger}$ then a Hadamard, both from T1.M2.L3.

The first figure lists $000$, $011$, $101$ and $110$, all with even parity. The
second lists $001$, $010$, $100$ and $111$, all with odd parity. Every
individual outcome is uniformly random, and the parity is deterministic. That is
the entire trick: local randomness, global certainty.

<Callout tone="note" title="Why this needs no statistics">
CHSH separates $0.75$ from $0.8535534$, so distinguishing them requires enough
shots that the difference exceeds the error bar. GHZ separates a strategy that
never loses from one that must lose a quarter of the time. Observe a classical
team and you will see a loss within a few rounds; observe the quantum team and
you will never see one. Mermin's original point was that this makes the
contradiction with local realism a matter of logic rather than of statistics.
</Callout>

## The same contradiction, in operators

There is a way to see the impossibility without mentioning a game. Suppose each
qubit has predetermined values $\pm 1$ for both $X$ and $Y$, call them
$X_1, Y_1, X_2, Y_2, X_3, Y_3$. The four identities demand

$$
X_1 X_2 X_3 = +1, \quad X_1 Y_2 Y_3 = -1, \quad Y_1 X_2 Y_3 = -1, \quad Y_1 Y_2 X_3 = -1 .
$$

Multiply all four. On the left every symbol appears exactly twice, and each
squares to $+1$, so the product is $+1$. On the right it is
$(+1)(-1)(-1)(-1) = -1$. The predetermined values cannot exist. This is the same
parity argument as the classical bound, and it shows the game is a repackaging
of an algebraic contradiction, which is exactly the structure T1.M6.L4 pushes
further.

<Check question="What happens to the quantum strategy if the players share $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{000} - \ket{111})$ instead?">
Every sign flips. That state is a $-1$ eigenstate of $XXX$ and a $+1$ eigenstate
of $XYY$, $YXY$ and $YYX$, so the parities come out exactly wrong on all four
questions and the strategy wins with probability 0. The fix is one $Z$ gate on
any single qubit before the measurements, which converts the state back to
$\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}$. The lesson generalises: nonlocal-game strategies are
sensitive to the relative phase of the shared state, not only to how entangled
it is.
</Check>
