The inequality of the previous lesson is a statement about correlators. Restated
as a game it becomes something you can win or lose, with a score you can compare
against a number no classical team can beat. That reframing is due to computer
scientists rather than physicists, and it is what turns a foundational result
into a benchmark you can run on hardware.

## The rules

A referee picks bits $x$ and $y$ independently and uniformly, sends $x$ to Alice
and $y$ to Bob, and receives bits $a$ and $b$ in return. Alice and Bob may agree
on any strategy in advance and may share any resource, but once the questions
are sent they cannot communicate. They win when

$$
a \oplus b = x \wedge y ,
$$

that is, their answers must differ when both questions are 1 and agree
otherwise.

<Margin label="Encoding">
Write $A = (-1)^a$ and $B = (-1)^b$. Then $a \oplus b = 0$ means $AB = +1$, so
the win condition is $AB = +1$ on three question pairs and $AB = -1$ on $(1,1)$.
The win probability is therefore
$\Pr[\text{win}] = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{S}{8}$ with $S$ the CHSH correlator of
T1.M6.L1.
</Margin>

## The classical ceiling is three in four

Any classical strategy is a distribution over deterministic ones, and a
deterministic strategy is four bits: $a_0, a_1$ for Alice and $b_0, b_1$ for
Bob. The four win conditions are

$$
a_0 \oplus b_0 = 0, \quad
a_0 \oplus b_1 = 0, \quad
a_1 \oplus b_0 = 0, \quad
a_1 \oplus b_1 = 1 .
$$

Add all four modulo 2. Each of $a_0, a_1, b_0, b_1$ appears exactly twice on the
left, so the left side is 0, while the right side is 1. The four conditions are
jointly contradictory, so at most three hold, and any deterministic strategy
wins at most 3 of the 4 equally likely question pairs. Randomising averages
strategies that each win at most $3/4$, so

$$
\Pr[\text{win}] \le \frac{3}{4} = 0.75 .
$$

The bound is achieved: both players always answer 0, winning every pair except
$(1,1)$.

## The quantum strategy

Alice and Bob share $\ket{\Phi^+}$. Alice measures at Bloch angle $0$ when
$x = 0$ and $\pi/2$ when $x = 1$; Bob measures at $\pi/4$ when $y = 0$ and
$-\pi/4$ when $y = 1$. Measuring at angle $\phi$ means applying $R_y(-\phi)$ and
reading the computational basis, and the reported bit is the outcome.

From T1.M6.L1 the correlator on $\ket{\Phi^+}$ is $\cos(\phi_A - \phi_B)$, so
each of the four settings has $|\cos| = \cos(\pi/4) = 0.707107$, three of them
positive and the $(1,1)$ pair negative. Then

$$
\Pr[\text{win}] = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2\sqrt2}{8}
= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{4} = 0.8535534 = \cos^2\frac{\pi}{8} .
$$

| $(x, y)$ | $\phi_A$ | $\phi_B$ | correlator | win probability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(0,0)$ | $0$ | $\pi/4$ | $+0.707107$ | $0.853553$ |
| $(0,1)$ | $0$ | $-\pi/4$ | $+0.707107$ | $0.853553$ |
| $(1,0)$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi/4$ | $+0.707107$ | $0.853553$ |
| $(1,1)$ | $\pi/2$ | $-\pi/4$ | $-0.707107$ | $0.853553$ |

Every setting wins with the same probability, which is what makes the strategy
optimal: there is no weak question pair to exploit.

Compute the win probability from simulated statevectors rather than returning a
literal. Build $\ket{\Phi^+}$, apply the two rotations for each of the four
question pairs, read the four outcome probabilities, form the correlator, and
average. The graded tests check `chsh_win_probability()` against
$0.8535533905932737$ to $10^{-10}$, check `chsh_correlator()` against
$2.8284271247461903$, check that `beats_classical()` is true, and check that
`chsh_product_state()` returns $0.75$, the best achievable when the shared state
is $\ket{00}$ instead of a Bell pair. A resource test confirms the shared state
uses exactly two qubits.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="A product state is not simply worse, it is classical">
Replace $\ket{\Phi^+}$ by $\ket{00}$ and keep the same four angles, and the
correlator falls to $\sqrt2 = 1.414214$, giving a win rate of $0.676777$: worse
than the classical strategy of always answering 0. Re-optimise the angles for
$\ket{00}$ and the best correlator is exactly 2 and the best win rate exactly
$0.75$. A product state can never exceed the classical bound, because it *is* a
local hidden-variable model with the hidden variable being nothing at all.
</Callout>

## Running it on hardware

The estimate has an error bar, and T1.M2.L5 gives it. Each correlator is an
expectation value near $\pm 0.707$, so its standard error at $N$ shots per
setting is $\sqrt{(1 - 0.5)/N}$. The four combine independently, so

$$
\mathrm{SE}(S) = \sqrt{4 \times \frac{0.5}{N}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{N}} .
$$

To claim a violation you need $S - 2$ to be several standard errors. At
$N = 1024$ shots per setting, $\mathrm{SE}(S) = 0.0442$ and the excess
$2.828427 - 2 = 0.828427$ is nearly 19 standard errors, which is comfortable.
At $N = 100$ it is $0.141$ and about 5.9 standard errors, which is still
convincing but leaves no room for the depolarising noise of T1.M11: a Bell pair
with fidelity below about $0.78$ stops violating the inequality at all.

<Check question="A team reports $S = 2.61$ from 1024 shots per setting on real hardware. Is that a Bell violation, and why is it below $2\sqrt2$?">
Yes. The standard error is $\sqrt{2/1024} = 0.0442$, so $S - 2 = 0.61$ is about
13.8 standard errors above the classical bound, which is decisive. It falls
short of $2.828427$ because the shared state is not a perfect Bell pair: gate
error, decoherence during the measurement rotations and readout error all pull
the correlators toward 0. Reporting the raw $S$ with its error bar is the honest
result; correcting it to $2\sqrt2$ by assuming the noise model would be
assuming the answer.
</Check>

<Solution>
Compute each correlator directly from the statevector. With the shared state
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ and measurement axes in the $xz$ plane, the correlator is
$\cos(\phi_A - \phi_B)$, but the graded version should evaluate it by
simulation:

```python
def _corr(a: float, b: float) -> float:
    """<A_a B_b> on (|00> + |11>)/sqrt(2) for Bloch angles a and b."""
    return float(np.cos(2 * (a - b)))


def chsh_correlator() -> float:
    a0, a1 = 0.0, np.pi / 4
    b0, b1 = np.pi / 8, -np.pi / 8
    return _corr(a0, b0) + _corr(a0, b1) + _corr(a1, b0) - _corr(a1, b1)


def chsh_win_probability() -> float:
    return 0.5 + chsh_correlator() / 8.0
```

Note the half-angle parameterisation here: the angles $0, \pi/4, \pi/8, -\pi/8$
are half the Bloch angles $0, \pi/2, \pi/4, -\pi/4$ used in the prose, which is
why the correlator carries a factor of 2 inside the cosine. Both conventions
give $S = 2.8284271247461903$.
</Solution>
