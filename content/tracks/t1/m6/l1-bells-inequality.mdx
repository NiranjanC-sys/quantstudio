Correlations are cheap. Two people who agreed on a plan in advance can produce
almost any pattern of agreement you ask for. So the interesting question is not
whether entangled qubits are correlated, but whether their correlations exceed
what any advance plan could produce. Bell's answer is a number, and quantum
mechanics beats it.

## The setup

Alice and Bob each hold one qubit. A referee sends Alice a bit $x$ and Bob a bit
$y$, chosen independently and uniformly. Alice measures her qubit in a way that
may depend on $x$ and reports $a \in \{+1, -1\}$; Bob does the same with $y$ and
reports $b$. They may not communicate after receiving their bits.

The quantity of interest is the CHSH correlator

$$
S = \expect{a b}_{00} + \expect{a b}_{01} + \expect{a b}_{10} - \expect{a b}_{11} ,
$$

where $\expect{ab}_{xy}$ is the average product of their answers on question pair
$(x, y)$. Three plus signs and one minus.

<Margin label="Reading the correlator">
$\expect{ab} = \Pr[a = b] - \Pr[a \ne b]$, so it runs from $+1$ for perfect
agreement to $-1$ for perfect disagreement, with 0 for no correlation. The
minus sign on the last term asks for the *opposite* behaviour there, and the
whole question is whether all four demands can be satisfied at once.
</Margin>

## The classical bound

Assume a **local hidden-variable** model: there is some variable $\lambda$,
distributed however you like, fixed before the questions arrive, and Alice's
answer is a function $A(x, \lambda)$ while Bob's is $B(y, \lambda)$. Locality is
the statement that $A$ does not depend on $y$ and $B$ does not depend on $x$.
This covers every conspiracy, every shared random seed, every prearranged
lookup table.

Fix $\lambda$ and write $A_0 = A(0, \lambda)$, $A_1 = A(1, \lambda)$,
$B_0 = B(0, \lambda)$, $B_1 = B(1, \lambda)$, each in $\{+1, -1\}$. Then

$$
A_0 B_0 + A_0 B_1 + A_1 B_0 - A_1 B_1
= A_0 (B_0 + B_1) + A_1 (B_0 - B_1) .
$$

Now the key observation: $B_0$ and $B_1$ are each $\pm 1$, so either
$B_0 + B_1 = \pm 2$ and $B_0 - B_1 = 0$, or $B_0 + B_1 = 0$ and
$B_0 - B_1 = \pm 2$. Exactly one of the two brackets vanishes. The surviving
term is $\pm 1$ times $\pm 2$, so the whole expression is $+2$ or $-2$ for every
$\lambda$.

Averaging over $\lambda$ can only shrink the magnitude:

$$
|S| \le 2 .
$$

That is Bell's inequality in the CHSH form. It assumed nothing about physics
beyond the existence of the four values $A_0, A_1, B_0, B_1$ at the same time
and their independence from the other side's question.

## The quantum value

Now do it with a Bell pair. Alice and Bob share
$\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$. Each measures an
observable in the $xz$ plane of the Bloch sphere, at an angle $\phi$ from the
$z$ axis:

$$
M(\phi) = \cos\phi \, Z + \sin\phi \, X ,
$$

implemented by applying $R_y(-\phi)$ and then measuring in the computational
basis, since $R_y(-\phi)^{\dagger} Z R_y(-\phi) = M(\phi)$.

On $\ket{\Phi^+}$ the two-qubit expectations are $\expect{Z \otimes Z} = 1$,
$\expect{X \otimes X} = 1$ and $\expect{Z \otimes X} = \expect{X \otimes Z} = 0$,
so

$$
\expect{M(\phi_A) \otimes M(\phi_B)}
= \cos\phi_A \cos\phi_B + \sin\phi_A \sin\phi_B
= \cos(\phi_A - \phi_B) .
$$

The correlation depends only on the angle between the two measurement axes.
Choose

$$
\phi_{A_0} = 0, \quad \phi_{A_1} = \frac{\pi}{2},
\qquad
\phi_{B_0} = \frac{\pi}{4}, \quad \phi_{B_1} = -\frac{\pi}{4} .
$$

Every one of the first three pairs is $\pi/4$ apart, and the fourth is $3\pi/4$
apart:

$$
S = \cos\frac{\pi}{4} + \cos\frac{\pi}{4} + \cos\frac{\pi}{4} - \cos\frac{3\pi}{4}
= 3 \times 0.707107 + 0.707107 = 2.828427 = 2\sqrt2 .
$$

Each of the four settings contributes $0.707107$ in magnitude, and
$2\sqrt2 \approx 2.828427$ exceeds the classical bound of 2 by a factor of
$\sqrt2$. Tsirelson's bound says $2\sqrt2$ is the maximum any quantum strategy
can reach, so the gap is exactly this large and no larger.

## Which assumption failed

The derivation used three ingredients, and it is worth being precise about which
one quantum mechanics rejects.

**Realism**, the assumption that $A_0$ and $A_1$ both have values simultaneously.
Quantum mechanics denies this: $M(0) = Z$ and $M(\pi/2) = X$ anticommute, so by
the argument of T1.M3.L1 they share no eigenvector and no state assigns both a
definite value. Alice measures one of them, and the other never had an answer to
report.

**Locality**, the assumption that $A$ is independent of $y$. Quantum mechanics
respects this: T1.M5.L2 proved that Bob's marginal is untouched by anything
Alice does, so no signal passes.

**Freedom of choice**, the assumption that $x$ and $y$ are independent of
$\lambda$. Quantum mechanics respects this too, and experiments enforce it with
distant random-number sources.

So the violation is a failure of realism, not of locality. The phrase "quantum
mechanics is nonlocal" is standard and slightly misleading: what fails is the
joint assumption, and the component that quantum theory abandons is the
pre-existing answer.

<Callout tone="warning" title="This is an experimental result, not an interpretation">
Aspect in the 1980s and the loophole-free experiments of 2015 measured $S$ and
found it above 2 by many standard errors. Any future theory must reproduce that.
The inequality is a theorem about a class of models, the measurement is a fact,
and together they rule the class out. T1.M6.L2 turns the same numbers into a
game you can run yourself.
</Callout>

<Check question="Alice and Bob both ignore the questions and always answer $+1$. What is $S$, and does any deterministic strategy do better?">
All four correlators are $\expect{ab} = 1$, so
$S = 1 + 1 + 1 - 1 = 2$, exactly at the bound. Many deterministic strategies
achieve 2, and by the derivation none exceeds it: the bracket argument gives
$\pm 2$ for every assignment. Randomising helps not at all, since a random
strategy is an average of deterministic ones and the average of numbers bounded
by 2 is bounded by 2. That is why beating 2 rules out the entire class rather
than one clever plan.
</Check>
