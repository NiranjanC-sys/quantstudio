"The qubit is in both states at once" is the sentence that does the most damage
in this subject. It suggests a qubit holds two answers and that a computation
could read them both. What a qubit actually holds is one vector, and the two
numbers in it are amplitudes, not answers. This lesson pins down what an
amplitude is, how to build a state with the probabilities you want, and why the
information that survives squaring is strictly less than the information in the
state.

## Superposition is a linear combination and nothing more

A **superposition** of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ is a linear combination

$$
\ket{\psi} = a_0 \ket{0} + a_1 \ket{1}
= \begin{pmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad a_0^2 + a_1^2 = 1 ,
$$

with $a_0$ and $a_1$ real for now. The coefficients are the **amplitudes**. The
word carries no metaphysics: it is the coefficient of a basis vector, in exactly
the sense that $3\hat{x} + 4\hat{y}$ has coefficients 3 and 4.

Amplitudes become probabilities only at measurement, and only through squaring:

$$
\Pr[0] = a_0^2, \qquad \Pr[1] = a_1^2 .
$$

<Margin label="Notation">
For complex amplitudes, which arrive in T1.M1.L6, the rule becomes
$\Pr[k] = |a_k|^2$, the squared modulus. The real case is the special case where
the modulus is the absolute value, so nothing said here will need revising.
</Margin>

Squaring is a lossy operation, and that is the whole point. It maps two numbers
to two numbers, but it cannot be inverted, because $a$ and $-a$ have the same
square. A state carries a sign that the probability distribution does not.

## Building a state to order

Suppose you want a qubit that reads 0 with probability $0.36$ and 1 with
probability $0.64$. Take square roots:

$$
|a_0| = \sqrt{0.36} = 0.6, \qquad |a_1| = \sqrt{0.64} = 0.8 .
$$

Check normalisation: $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$. Four sign choices give
four different states,

$$
(0.6,\ 0.8), \quad (0.6,\ -0.8), \quad (-0.6,\ 0.8), \quad (-0.6,\ -0.8),
$$

and all four are legitimate. The requested probabilities did not determine the
state; they determined it only up to signs. The gate $R_y(\theta)$ produces the
first of them, since $R_y(\theta)\ket{0} = \cos(\theta/2)\ket{0} + \sin(\theta/2)\ket{1}$ and $\theta = 2\arctan(0.8/0.6) = 1.8545904$ gives
$\cos(\theta/2) = 0.6$ exactly.

Negating the angle negates the second amplitude and leaves the first alone,
producing $(0.6, -0.8)$ with the same probabilities $0.36$ and $0.64$. Nothing
you measure in the computational basis will ever separate the two circuits.

## Same probabilities, different futures

Now put a Hadamard on the end of each. Recall that $H$ sends
$(a_0, a_1)$ to $\left(\tfrac{a_0 + a_1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{a_0 - a_1}{\sqrt2}\right)$.
For the first state,

$$
H \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1.4/\sqrt2 \\ -0.2/\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.989949 \\ -0.141421 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \Pr[0] = 0.98 .
$$

For the second, the sums and differences trade places:

$$
H \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ -0.8 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -0.2/\sqrt2 \\ 1.4/\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \Pr[0] = 0.02 .
$$

Two states with identical readout statistics, run through the identical gate,
give answers that disagree $98$ times in $100$. This is the operational content
of the claim that a state carries more than its distribution. A classical
description that recorded only $(0.36, 0.64)$ would have no way to produce these
two different predictions, because it never stored the difference.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Superposition is relative to a basis">
$\ket{0}$ is not a superposition in the computational basis, but written in the
$\{\ket{+}, \ket{-}\}$ basis it is
$\ket{0} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{+} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{-}$, a perfectly
even superposition. Asking whether a state "is in superposition" without naming
a basis is asking an incomplete question.
</Callout>

## How much information is in one qubit

It is tempting to conclude that a qubit stores a real number, and that a real
number holds infinitely many bits, so that a thousand qubits could hold a
library. The angle $\theta$ is indeed continuous. But
a single measurement returns one bit, and it destroys the state, so extracting
$\theta$ to $k$ bits of precision needs on the order of $4^k$ preparations and
measurements. Holevo's bound, which T1.M7 states properly, makes this exact: one
qubit transmits at most one classical bit. The extra structure is not storage
capacity. It is the ability to interfere, and interference is only visible when
many amplitudes meet.

<Check question="You need a state with $\Pr[0] = 0.09$. Give one, and give a second that no computational-basis measurement can distinguish from it but that a Hadamard separates.">
Take $\ket{\psi} = (0.3,\ 0.9539392)$, since $\sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$ and
$\sqrt{0.91} = 0.9539392$. The partner is $\ket{\phi} = (0.3,\ -0.9539392)$.
Both read 0 with probability $0.09$ and 1 with probability $0.91$. After $H$ the
first has amplitude $(0.3 + 0.9539392)/\sqrt2 = 0.8866689$ on $\ket{0}$, giving
$\Pr[0] = 0.7861818$, while the second has
$(0.3 - 0.9539392)/\sqrt2 = -0.4624048$, giving $\Pr[0] = 0.2138182$. The two
answers sum to 1, which is the algebraic trace of the cross term
$2 a_0 a_1 / 2 = 0.2861818$ being added in one case and subtracted in the
other.
</Check>
