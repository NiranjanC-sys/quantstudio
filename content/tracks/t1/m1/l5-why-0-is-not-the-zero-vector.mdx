Two objects in this subject are written with the character 0 and they are not
the same object. One is $\ket{0}$, the basis state that reads out as the bit 0
with certainty. The other is $\mathbf{0}$, the zero vector of $\R^2$, the
additive identity that every vector space has. Confusing them produces a
specific family of wrong answers, and the confusion is entirely the fault of the
notation.

## The two objects, side by side

$$
\ket{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Their lengths differ: $\lVert \ket{0} \rVert = 1$ and
$\lVert \mathbf{0} \rVert = 0$. By the constraint of the previous lesson, only
the first is a state. The second fails normalisation and cannot be repaired,
because repairing means dividing by the length and the length is zero.

<Margin label="Notation">
The symbol inside a ket is a **label**, not a number. Writing
$\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ is a convention inherited from bits; the states could as
honestly be called $\ket{\text{off}}$ and $\ket{\text{on}}$, and in T4 the same
two vectors are called $\ket{g}$ and $\ket{e}$ for ground and excited. Nothing
in $\ket{0}$ is being multiplied by zero.
</Margin>

## Why the zero vector cannot be a state

Three independent reasons, each sufficient on its own.

**Probabilities.** The squares of the entries of $\mathbf{0}$ are $0$ and $0$,
which sum to $0$. A measurement of that system would return nothing at all,
which is not a possible outcome of an experiment that certainly happens.

**Normalisation.** The rescaling that fixes every other unnormalised vector,
$v \mapsto v / \lVert v \rVert$, is undefined at $v = \mathbf{0}$. The zero
vector is the unique vector in $\R^2$ with no normalised representative, so it
is not "a state written badly". It is outside the set.

**Linearity.** Every linear map sends $\mathbf{0}$ to $\mathbf{0}$. If the zero
vector were a state, then it would be a state that no gate can ever leave, and
every gate would agree on it. Since gates are invertible and length preserving,
no unit vector is ever mapped to $\mathbf{0}$ either, so the zero vector is
unreachable as well as inescapable. It is disconnected from the physics in both
directions.

## Where the confusion produces real errors

The dangerous case is not writing $\ket{0} = \mathbf{0}$, which nobody does
twice. It is producing $\mathbf{0}$ at the end of a calculation and reading it
as a state.

**A zero amplitude is not a zero state.** Apply $X$ to $\ket{0}$. The result is
$\ket{1} = (0, 1)$, whose first entry is zero. The amplitude on outcome 0
vanished; the state did not. It has length 1 and reads out as 1 with certainty.

The figure lists one row. Rows whose probability is zero are omitted, and the
habit of reading a short table as "the missing states are gone" is precisely the
habit that leads to treating a vanished amplitude as a vanished system.
Interference produces exactly this situation: after $H$ then $H$, the amplitude
on $\ket{1}$ is $\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$, so the table shows a single row for
$\ket{0}$ with amplitude 1.

**A zero inner product is a number.** The expression $\braket{1}{0}$ evaluates
to the scalar $0$, meaning the two states are orthogonal. It does not produce a
state, and it is not $\ket{0}$ scaled by anything. The bracket eats two vectors
and returns a number, which is the reason the notation splits into bras and
kets at all.

**A projection can genuinely give the zero vector.** Measuring $\ket{0}$ and
asking for the outcome 1 means applying the projector
$P_1 = \ketbra{1}{1}$, and

$$
P_1 \ket{0} = \ket{1}\braket{1}{0} = \mathbf{0} .
$$

Here the zero vector is the correct answer, and its meaning is the probability
of that outcome, $\lVert P_1 \ket{0} \rVert^2 = 0$. The error is to continue,
divide by the norm, and report a post-measurement state. There is no
post-measurement state because the branch has probability zero and never occurs.
T1.M2.L2 uses this rule in the form that matters: renormalise after a
measurement, but only on branches that can happen.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Three ways to produce a non-state">
A vector that is not a state has arrived if any of these hold: its squared
entries do not sum to 1 and you have not renormalised; it has the wrong number
of entries for the number of qubits, so $2^n$ has been miscounted; or it is
$\mathbf{0}$, in which case you have projected onto an impossible outcome or
subtracted a state from itself.
</Callout>

## The superposition that is not a state

Beginners write "the qubit is in $\ket{0} + \ket{1}$". As written, that vector
is $(1, 1)$, with squared entries summing to 2. It is not a state; the state is
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + \ket{1})$. The habit of dropping the factor is
harmless in a proof where you renormalise at the end, and fatal in code, where
the missing $1/\sqrt2$ silently doubles every probability you compute from it.

Similarly $\ket{0} - \ket{0} = \mathbf{0}$, which is not a state that somehow
cancels itself out of existence. It is a sign that the two terms you subtracted
were describing the same branch and should have been combined earlier.

<Check question="A student computes $H\ket{+}$ and writes the result as $\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ 0\right) = (1, 0)$, then says the second entry being 0 means the qubit has collapsed. What is wrong?">
The arithmetic is right and the conclusion is wrong. $H\ket{+} = \ket{0}$, a unit
vector, and no measurement occurred. The 0 in the second entry is an amplitude,
the coefficient of $\ket{1}$, and it is zero because two contributions of
$+\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$ cancelled. Collapse is a change of state caused by
measurement; this is destructive interference caused by a gate. The state after
the gate is $\ket{0} = (1, 0)$, which has length 1, not the zero vector.
</Check>
