Two real parameters survived the counting in T1.M1.L6, and two real parameters
are what a point on a sphere has. This lesson is the sphere itself. Work it with
your hands: predict where a gate will send the arrow before you look, then look.

## The map

A canonical one-qubit state
$\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{0} + e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{1}$
becomes the unit vector

$$
\vec{r} = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta\cos\varphi \\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi \\ \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} ,
$$

with $\theta$ measured down from the north pole and $\varphi$ around the
equator. The halving of $\theta$ is what makes the map work: states that are
orthogonal as vectors, an angle of $90$ degrees apart in $\C^2$, come out
antipodal on the sphere, $180$ degrees apart. $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ are
opposite poles, not neighbours.

<Margin label="Definition">
Six named states sit on the axes. Along $z$: $\ket{0}$ at the north pole,
$\ket{1}$ at the south. Along $x$: $\ket{+}$ and $\ket{-}$. Along $y$:
$\ket{+i}$ and $\ket{-i}$. Each axis is the eigenbasis of the Pauli operator of
the same name, which is why the three measurement bases of T1.M2.L3 are
exactly these three axes.
</Margin>

Rotate about $y$ by $\pi/2$ and the arrow travels from $+z$ to $+x$. That is the
whole content of $R_y$: a right-handed rotation about the $y$ axis by the angle
in the parameter. The state vector picked up the angle $\theta/2$ and the arrow
picked up $\theta$.

## Predict, then check

Try $R_x(\pi/2)$ on $\ket{0}$ before reading on. Rotating $+z$ about the $x$
axis by a quarter turn, right-handed, sends it to $-y$. So the answer should be
$\ket{-i}$, with amplitudes $\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ -\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\right)$.

Now a partial turn. $R_x(\pi/3)$ takes $+z$ a third of the way round to $-y$, so
the arrow should be $(0,\, -\sin(\pi/3),\, \cos(\pi/3)) = (0,\, -0.866025,\, 0.5)$,
still on the surface since $0.866025^2 + 0.5^2 = 1$. The amplitudes are
$\cos(\pi/6) = 0.866025$ on $\ket{0}$ and $-i\sin(\pi/6) = -0.5i$ on $\ket{1}$,
so the outcome probabilities are $0.75$ and $0.25$. The height of the arrow and
the outcome probabilities are the same information: $z = \Pr[0] - \Pr[1]$.

## Reading a gate as an axis and an angle

Every one-qubit unitary is a rotation of this sphere, and stating it as an axis
and an angle is usually more useful than stating the matrix.

| gate | axis | angle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X$ | $x$ | $\pi$ |
| $Y$ | $y$ | $\pi$ |
| $Z$ | $z$ | $\pi$ |
| $S$ | $z$ | $\pi/2$ |
| $T$ | $z$ | $\pi/4$ |
| $H$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(x + z)$ | $\pi$ |

The last row is the one worth pausing on. $H$ is a half turn about the diagonal
axis midway between $x$ and $z$, which swaps those two axes and reverses $y$.
That is why $H\ket{0} = \ket{+}$ and $H\ket{+} = \ket{0}$: the half turn
exchanges the poles it sits between. It also explains T1.M1.L2 without
contradiction. Restricted to the real $xz$ great circle, a half turn about an
in-plane axis is a reflection of that circle in that axis, and the axis meets
the circle at $22.5$ degrees.

Two more predictions to make before looking. $T$ applied to $\ket{+}$ turns the
arrow one eighth of the way round the equator from $+x$ towards $+y$, landing at
$(\cos(\pi/4),\, \sin(\pi/4),\, 0) = (0.707107,\, 0.707107,\, 0)$. And $R_z$ on
$\ket{0}$ does nothing at all to the arrow, since the north pole is on the
rotation axis, which is the geometric statement that $R_z$ contributes only a
global phase to $\ket{0}$.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The inside of the ball is not empty">
Every state in this module has an arrow of length exactly 1, on the surface.
Shorter arrows are legal and they mean something: the state is mixed, either
because of noise or because the qubit is entangled with something you are not
looking at. T1.M5.L3 puts a Bell-pair qubit at the exact centre, arrow length 0.
The figures above report that length as the purity.
</Callout>

<Check question="Where does $Z$ send $\ket{+}$, and where does it send $\ket{0}$?">
$Z$ is a half turn about the $z$ axis. It sends $\ket{+}$ at $(1,0,0)$ to
$(-1,0,0)$, which is $\ket{-}$: the equator point travels half way round. It
sends $\ket{0}$ at $(0,0,1)$ to itself, because that point lies on the rotation
axis. In amplitudes, $Z\ket{0} = \ket{0}$ exactly, with not even a phase.
</Check>
