Why is a quantum gate a unitary matrix rather than any matrix at all? The
textbook answer, "because quantum evolution is unitary", explains nothing. The
honest answer is a one-line consequence of the normalisation constraint, and
seeing it derived is the difference between accepting the rule and being able to
recover it when you have forgotten it.

## The constraint, and the set it carves out

A real-valued qubit state satisfies

$$
a_0^2 + a_1^2 = 1 .
$$

That equation is the unit circle in $\R^2$, so the set of valid states is a
curve, not a region. There is no interior. The vector $(0.5, 0.5)$ is not a
weakly normalised state or a state with low confidence; it is not a state at all,
because $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5 \ne 1$. Neither is $(1, 1)$, whose squares sum to 2.

The reason is not aesthetic. The squares of the amplitudes are declared to be the
outcome probabilities, and a probability distribution over two outcomes has to
sum to 1. Normalisation is that requirement, transported through the squaring
rule and nothing more.

<Margin label="Definition">
$\lVert \psi \rVert_2 = \sqrt{a_0^2 + a_1^2}$ is the Euclidean length. A state is
a vector of length exactly 1. Two vectors of the same direction and different
length describe the same physics only after the longer one is scaled down; the
scaling is not optional bookkeeping, it is what makes probabilities add up.
</Margin>

## Renormalising

Vectors that fail the constraint arrive constantly. A hand-written combination,
a projection after a measurement, a state read off a matrix column: none of them
come out normalised. The repair is division by the length.

Take $v = (3, 4)$. Its length is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, so

$$
\ket{\psi} = \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad 0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1 .
$$

The probabilities are $0.36$ and $0.64$, which sum to 1 as required. Two more,
worth doing in your head: $(1, 1)$ has length $\sqrt2$ and renormalises to
$\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = \ket{+}$; and $(2, -1)$ has
length $\sqrt5$ and renormalises to $(0.894427, -0.447214)$, with probabilities
$0.8$ and $0.2$.

<Callout tone="note" title="Ratios survive, scale does not">
Renormalising never changes the relative sizes or the signs of the entries, so
it never changes any prediction. It only puts the vector where the rule for
turning amplitudes into probabilities is valid. This is why authors write
unnormalised states freely and attach the factor at the end.
</Callout>

## Why gates must preserve the circle

Now the derivation. Suppose $G$ is a $2 \times 2$ real matrix that a machine can
apply to any state. Feed it a valid state $\ket{\psi}$ and demand that the output
is a valid state too, for every input. That single demand says

$$
\lVert G \ket{\psi} \rVert = 1 \quad \text{whenever} \quad \lVert \psi \rVert = 1 ,
$$

which by linearity extends to $\lVert G v \rVert = \lVert v \rVert$ for all $v$:
scale any $v$ to unit length, apply the condition, scale back. A linear map that
preserves all lengths preserves all inner products as well, and writing that in
matrix form gives

$$
v^{\mathsf T} G^{\mathsf T} G\, w = v^{\mathsf T} w \quad \text{for all } v, w
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad G^{\mathsf T} G = I .
$$

So $G$ is orthogonal. Over the reals the orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrices are
exactly the rotations, with determinant $+1$, and the reflections, with
determinant $-1$. Nothing else can be a gate. Over the complex numbers the same
argument with the conjugate transpose gives $G^{\dagger} G = I$, the definition
of unitary, and T1.M3.L4 does that case in full.

The failure mode is easy to see. The shear $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$
sends $\ket{+}$ to $\left(\sqrt2, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$, whose squared
entries sum to $2.5$. A machine implementing $M$ would report outcome 0 with
probability 2, which is not a defect in the interpretation but a proof that no
such machine exists.

## Rotations of the circle, in a circuit

The rotation gate $R_y(\theta)$ has matrix
$\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) & -\sin(\theta/2) \\ \sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix}$,
which is the rotation of the plane by $\theta/2$. The half is a convention that
pays off on the Bloch sphere later; for now the useful consequence is that
angles add. Rotating by $0.3$ and then by $0.62729522$ should land in the same
place as rotating by $0.92729522$, which is the state $(0.6, 0.8)$.

Reflections are allowed too, and mixing them with rotations changes nothing
about normalisation. The sequence $R_y(0.7)$, $H$, $R_y(-1.3)$, $H$ contains two
reflections and two rotations, and because $H R_y(\theta) H = R_y(-\theta)$ it
collapses to $R_y(2.0)$, whose amplitudes are $\cos 1 = 0.540302$ and
$\sin 1 = 0.841471$.

Check the arithmetic: $0.540302^2 + 0.841471^2 = 0.291927 + 0.708073 = 1$. The
invariant held through every intermediate step, because it held for every factor.

<Check question="Is $(0.8, 0.6, 0)$ ever a legal state, and what is the state obtained by renormalising $(0, -5)$?">
$(0.8, 0.6, 0)$ has three entries, so it cannot describe one qubit, which needs
two. It is a legal state of a *qutrit*, a three-level system, since
$0.64 + 0.36 + 0 = 1$. Renormalising $(0, -5)$ divides by the length 5 and gives
$(0, -1)$, which is $-\ket{1}$: a perfectly good qubit state that reads 1 with
probability 1, and is distinguished from $\ket{1}$ by no measurement whatsoever.
</Check>
