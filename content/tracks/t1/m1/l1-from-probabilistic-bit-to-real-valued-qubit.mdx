A probabilistic bit is a point on a line segment. A qubit is a point on a
circle. Everything else in this track is bookkeeping around that one change, so
it is worth watching the segment become a circle in slow motion, with no complex
numbers anywhere, before the machinery arrives.

## One constraint, replaced

A probabilistic bit is a vector $p = (p_0, p_1)$ with

$$
p_0 \ge 0, \quad p_1 \ge 0, \qquad p_0 + p_1 = 1 .
$$

A *real-valued qubit* is a vector $\ket{\psi} = (a_0, a_1)$ in $\R^2$ with

$$
a_0^2 + a_1^2 = 1 ,
$$

and no sign condition at all. The numbers $a_0$ and $a_1$ are called
**amplitudes**. To get from a state to something you can bet on, square:

$$
\Pr[\text{outcome } 0] = a_0^2, \qquad \Pr[\text{outcome } 1] = a_1^2 .
$$

Normalisation is exactly the statement that those two probabilities sum to one,
so the rule is self-consistent by construction and never needs a separate axiom.

<Margin label="Notation">
$\lVert p \rVert_1 = |p_0| + |p_1|$ is the $\ell_1$ norm and
$\lVert \psi \rVert_2 = \sqrt{a_0^2 + a_1^2}$ is the $\ell_2$ norm. A
probabilistic bit is a non-negative vector of $\ell_1$ norm 1; a qubit is any
vector of $\ell_2$ norm 1.
</Margin>

Draw both sets in the plane. The probabilistic states are the segment from
$(1,0)$ to $(0,1)$: one dimension of freedom, two endpoints, nothing outside.
The qubit states are the full unit circle: still one dimension of freedom, but
now it closes on itself and it passes through four points on the axes rather
than two. The two extra axis points are $(-1, 0)$ and $(0, -1)$, and they are
the first genuinely new objects in the subject.

## Negative amplitudes are not negative probabilities

The vector $(-1, 0)$ has $a_0^2 = 1$, so it produces outcome 0 with certainty,
exactly as $(1,0)$ does. No single measurement can tell them apart. A reasonable
first reaction is that the minus sign is therefore meaningless. It is not. The
rule that squares amplitudes applies at the moment of measurement, and until
that moment the signs survive and are allowed to cancel.

Compare the two states

$$
\ket{+} = \begin{pmatrix} \isqrt \\[2pt] \isqrt \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ket{-} = \begin{pmatrix} \isqrt \\[2pt] -\isqrt \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both give outcome 0 with probability $\left(1/\sqrt2\right)^2 = 1/2$ and outcome
1 with probability $1/2$. As descriptions of what a measurement will do right
now, they are identical. As descriptions of what the bit will do after one more
gate, they are opposites, and the next section makes that concrete.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Amplitudes are not probabilities">
A negative probability has no operational meaning: there is no experiment whose
outcome occurs minus one third of the time. A negative amplitude is ordinary.
The squaring step is what forces non-negativity, and it happens at readout, not
at storage.
</Callout>

## What the extra room buys you

The Hadamard gate is the matrix

$$
H = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which sends $\ket{0} \mapsto \ket{+}$ and $\ket{1} \mapsto \ket{-}$. Because
$H$ has orthonormal columns it preserves length, so it maps the unit circle to
itself, which is the only thing a legal operation is allowed to do.

Apply $H$ twice to $\ket{0}$. The first application spreads the amplitude:
$\ket{0} \mapsto \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$. The second
recombines it:

$$
H \begin{pmatrix} \isqrt \\[2pt] \isqrt \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \\[2pt] \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The amplitude on outcome 1 is $\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$. Two contributions of
equal size and opposite sign met and cancelled. A probabilistic bit cannot do
this: a stochastic matrix applied to $(0.5, 0.5)$ can never sharpen it back to
$(1, 0)$, because mixing only ever destroys information.

Now insert a sign flip between the two Hadamards. The gate
$Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ leaves $a_0$ alone and
negates $a_1$, turning $\ket{+}$ into $\ket{-}$. Repeat the second Hadamard:

$$
H \begin{pmatrix} \isqrt \\[2pt] -\isqrt \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \\[2pt] \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The cancellation has moved. The outcome is now 1 with certainty.

Between the two circuits the intermediate probabilities are identical, both
$(0.5, 0.5)$, and the final probabilities are opposite. A classical simulator
that tracked only probabilities would have thrown away the sign at the halfway
point and would predict $(0.5, 0.5)$ at the end of both. That gap is the entire
resource, and it is called **interference**.

## Reading a point off the circle

Every real-valued qubit state can be written as

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \theta \in [0, 2\pi),
$$

since that is what $a_0^2 + a_1^2 = 1$ says. The probability of outcome 0 is
$\cos^2\theta$, and it depends only on the angle. Four familiar states sit at
four familiar angles: $\ket{0}$ at $\theta = 0$, $\ket{+}$ at $\pi/4$,
$\ket{1}$ at $\pi/2$, and $\ket{-}$ at $-\pi/4$, which is the same point as
$7\pi/4$.

The angle is the single real parameter of a real qubit. A probabilistic bit also
has one real parameter, $p_0 \in [0,1]$. The difference is not how much you can
store; it is that the qubit's parameter lives on a closed curve where paths can
come back and meet, and the probabilistic bit's lives on a segment where they
cannot.

<Check question="The vectors $(0.6, 0.8)$ and $(0.6, -0.8)$ are both valid real qubit states. Which measurement, if any, distinguishes them?">
No computational-basis measurement does: both give outcome 0 with probability
$0.36$ and outcome 1 with probability $0.64$. Apply $H$ first and they separate.
$H(0.6, 0.8) = \left(\tfrac{1.4}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{-0.2}{\sqrt2}\right)
\approx (0.98995, -0.14142)$, giving outcome 0 with probability $0.98$, while
$H(0.6, -0.8) = \left(\tfrac{-0.2}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1.4}{\sqrt2}\right)$, giving
outcome 0 with probability $0.02$. The sign is invisible until a gate converts
it into an amplitude difference.
</Check>
