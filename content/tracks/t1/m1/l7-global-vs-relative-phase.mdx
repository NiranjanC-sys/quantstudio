Complex amplitudes came with a free parameter that turns out to be pure
bookkeeping, and a second one that is the engine of every quantum algorithm.
They look nearly identical on the page. Telling them apart is the difference
between a state you can simplify and a state whose structure you must respect.

## Global phase: invisible, always

A **global phase** multiplies the whole vector:
$\ket{\psi} \mapsto e^{i\gamma}\ket{\psi}$ for a real $\gamma$. The claim is
that no experiment distinguishes the two. The proof is one line and it covers
every measurement, not just the computational one.

A measurement in any orthonormal basis $\ket{m_1}, \dots, \ket{m_d}$ assigns
outcome $k$ the probability $|\braket{m_k}{\psi}|^2$. Replace $\ket{\psi}$ by
$e^{i\gamma}\ket{\psi}$:

$$
\left| \bra{m_k} \left( e^{i\gamma} \ket{\psi} \right) \right|^2
= \left| e^{i\gamma} \right|^2 \left| \braket{m_k}{\psi} \right|^2
= \left| \braket{m_k}{\psi} \right|^2 ,
$$

because $|e^{i\gamma}| = 1$ for every real $\gamma$. Every outcome of every
measurement has an unchanged probability, so the two vectors are the same
physical state. They are the same state after any gate as well, since
$U(e^{i\gamma}\ket{\psi}) = e^{i\gamma}(U\ket{\psi})$: the factor commutes
through everything and can never be caught.

<Margin label="Notation">
Writing $\ket{\psi} \simeq \ket{\phi}$ for "equal up to global phase" saves a
great deal of ink. $-\ket{0} \simeq \ket{0}$, and
$i\ket{1} \simeq \ket{1}$. On a *sub*system, the same factor is not global and
the equivalence fails, which is the subject of T1.M9.
</Margin>

## Relative phase: visible in the right basis

A **relative phase** is a phase between components:
$\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1} \mapsto \alpha\ket{0} + e^{i\varphi}\beta\ket{1}$.
It leaves $|\beta|$ alone, so the computational-basis probabilities are
untouched, and beginners conclude it is invisible too. It is not. Measure in the
$X$ basis instead.

The $X$-basis probability of outcome $+$ is

$$
\left| \braket{+}{\psi} \right|^2
= \left| \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\sqrt2} \right|^2
= \frac{|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\alpha^{*}\beta)}{2}
= \frac{1}{2} + \mathrm{Re}(\alpha^{*}\beta) .
$$

The whole dependence sits in the cross term $\mathrm{Re}(\alpha^{*}\beta)$, and
that term is exactly what a relative phase moves. Take
$\alpha = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ throughout and vary $\beta$:

| state | $\beta$ | $\mathrm{Re}(\alpha^{*}\beta)$ | $\Pr[+]$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\ket{+}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |
| $\ket{+i}$ | $\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}$ | $0$ | $0.5$ |
| $\ket{-}$ | $-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ |

Three states with identical computational-basis statistics and $X$-basis
probabilities of $1$, $0.5$ and $0$. Applying $H$ before measuring turns the
$X$-basis question into a computational-basis one, so the circuits below read
those numbers off directly.

Replacing $S$ with $Z$, a relative phase of $\pi$, gives outcome 1 with
probability 1: the third row of the table. One gate between two Hadamards
sweeps the answer from certain-0 through even to certain-1, and nothing in the
computational-basis description of the middle state changes at all.

## Telling them apart mechanically

Given two vectors, they are the same state exactly when one is a scalar multiple
of the other by a unit-modulus number. The test: form the ratio componentwise
and see whether it is constant.

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 - 0.5i \\ 0.5 + 0.5i \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ket{\phi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707107 \\ 0.707107 i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Componentwise, $\tfrac{0.707107}{0.5 - 0.5i} = 0.707107 + 0.707107 i$ and
$\tfrac{0.707107 i}{0.5 + 0.5i} = 0.707107 + 0.707107 i$. The ratio is constant
and equals $e^{i\pi/4}$, so these are the same state up to global phase. The
first is what $R_z(\pi/2)$ produces from $\ket{+}$ and the second is what $S$
produces, which is why the two gates are interchangeable in a circuit whose
result is measured.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Rz and P differ by a global phase, until they do not">
$R_z(\lambda) = e^{-i\lambda/2}\,P(\lambda)$, so on a lone qubit they are the
same gate. Put either one under a control and the difference becomes a relative
phase on the control qubit, and the two circuits stop agreeing. Global phase is
only global while nothing else is watching.
</Callout>

## Canonical form

Because the global phase is free, every state has a representative whose first
non-zero amplitude is real and positive. Multiply by
$e^{-i\arg \alpha}$:

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1+i}{2} \\[2pt] \tfrac{1-i}{2} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \arg \alpha = \frac{\pi}{4},
\qquad
e^{-i\pi/4}\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707107 \\ -0.707107 i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Check the second entry by hand:
$\tfrac{1-i}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1-i}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac{(1-i)^2}{2\sqrt2}
= \tfrac{-2i}{2\sqrt2} = -\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}$. The canonical form is $\ket{-i}$,
which the circuit below prepares directly with $H$ then $S^{\dagger}$. Its
probabilities, $0.5$ and $0.5$, match those of the original, as they must.

In canonical form a one-qubit state is $\cos(\theta/2)\ket{0} +
e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{1}$ with two real parameters and no leftover
freedom, which is the parameterisation the Bloch sphere draws.

<Check question="Are $\left(\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2},\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$ and $\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\right)$ the same state?">
No. Componentwise ratios: $\tfrac{1/\sqrt2}{i/\sqrt2} = -i$ for the first entry
and $\tfrac{i/\sqrt2}{1/\sqrt2} = i$ for the second. The ratio is not constant,
so no global phase relates them. Put both in canonical form to be sure:
multiplying the first by $e^{-i\pi/2} = -i$ gives
$\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ -\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\right) = \ket{-i}$, while the
second is already canonical and is $\ket{+i}$. They are opposite poles, and an
$S^{\dagger}$ followed by $H$ separates them with certainty.
</Check>
