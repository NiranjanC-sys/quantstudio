The real circle has done a lot of work: superposition, interference,
normalisation, gates as reflections and rotations. So what is missing that
forces the amplitudes to be complex? The short answer is that the real theory is
not closed under the operations physics actually supplies. The longer answer
changes the state space from a circle to a sphere, and that sphere is where the
rest of the track lives.

## The rule, extended

A one-qubit state is

$$
\ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}
= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \alpha, \beta \in \C,
\qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 ,
$$

and the Born rule reads $\Pr[0] = |\alpha|^2$, $\Pr[1] = |\beta|^2$. The
modulus bars are doing real work now. For $z = x + iy$,

$$
|z|^2 = z^{*} z = x^2 + y^2 ,
$$

which is a non-negative real number even though $z^2$ generally is not. Squaring
without the bars would be a disaster: the amplitude $i$ would contribute
$i^2 = -1$ to a probability.

<Margin label="Notation">
$z^{*}$ is the complex conjugate, $(x + iy)^{*} = x - iy$. Writing
$|z|^2 = z^{*}z$ rather than $z^2$ is the single most common source of sign
errors in first calculations. When in doubt, split into real and imaginary parts
and add their squares.
</Margin>

Work one example all the way through. Let

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.433013 + 0.75 i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Then $|\alpha|^2 = 0.25$ and
$|\beta|^2 = 0.433013^2 + 0.75^2 = 0.1875 + 0.5625 = 0.75$, which sum to 1, so
this is a state, and it reads out as 1 three times in four. The gate
$U(\theta, \varphi, \lambda)$ prepares it from $\ket{0}$ with
$\theta = 2\pi/3$ and $\varphi = \pi/3$, because
$U(\theta,\varphi,\lambda)\ket{0} = \cos(\theta/2)\ket{0} + e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{1}$
and $e^{i\pi/3}\sin(\pi/3) = (0.5 + 0.866025i)(0.866025) = 0.433013 + 0.75i$.

## Why real amplitudes are not enough

Three reasons, in increasing order of force.

**Closure under square roots.** The gate $Z$ is real. Its square root, the gate
$S$ with $S^2 = Z$, is not: $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)$. Hardware supplies
continuous families of gates and therefore supplies square roots of things,
and the real $2\times 2$ orthogonal group is not closed under them in a way that
keeps the algebra one-qubit sized.

**Continuous time evolution.** A physical qubit evolves as
$\ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}$ for a Hermitian $H$. The $i$ in that
exponential is not decoration; it is what makes the evolution length preserving
rather than exponentially growing or decaying. T1.M3.L3 derives the rotation
gates from exactly this exponential.

**Counting states.** The real circle has one parameter, so real qubits form a
one-dimensional family. The physically distinguishable one-qubit states form a
two-dimensional family, and experiments confirm the second parameter exists: the
states $\ket{+i}$ and $\ket{-i}$ below are perfectly distinguishable from each
other and from every real state, and neither is on the circle.

## The sphere, by counting

Two complex amplitudes are four real numbers. Two constraints remove two of
them.

$$
\underbrace{4}_{\alpha, \beta \in \C}
\;-\; \underbrace{1}_{|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1}
\;-\; \underbrace{1}_{\text{global phase}}
\;=\; 2 .
$$

The normalisation constraint is one real equation. The global phase is the
observation, proved in the next lesson, that $\ket{\psi}$ and
$e^{i\gamma}\ket{\psi}$ give identical predictions for every experiment, so a
whole circle of vectors describes one state. What is left is a two-parameter
family, and writing it in the standard form

$$
\ket{\psi} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{0}
+ e^{i\varphi} \sin\frac{\theta}{2} \ket{1},
\qquad \theta \in [0, \pi], \ \varphi \in [0, 2\pi),
$$

makes $\theta$ a polar angle and $\varphi$ an azimuth. Those are spherical
coordinates, and the surface they sweep is the Bloch sphere of T1.M1.L8. The
real circle is the slice at $\varphi \in \{0, \pi\}$, which is the great circle
through $\ket{0}$, $\ket{+}$, $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{-}$.

## The two new poles

Setting $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\varphi = \pm\pi/2$ gives

$$
\ket{+i} = \frac{\ket{0} + i\ket{1}}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\ket{-i} = \frac{\ket{0} - i\ket{1}}{\sqrt2} .
$$

Normalisation: $\left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 + \left|\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\right|^2
= \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$, using $|i| = 1$. Both read 0 and 1 with probability
$\tfrac12$, so in the computational basis they are indistinguishable from
$\ket{+}$, from $\ket{-}$, and from each other. They are prepared by a Hadamard
followed by $S$ or by $S^{\dagger}$.

Swap $S$ for $S^{\dagger}$ and the amplitude on $\ket{1}$ becomes
$-0.707107 i$: that is $\ket{-i}$, pointing along $-y$. The four states
$\ket{+}, \ket{-}, \ket{+i}, \ket{-i}$ all have the same computational-basis
statistics and are mutually distinguishable by other measurements, which is the
clearest sign that the state space grew.

<Check question="Is $\left(\tfrac{1+i}{2},\ \tfrac{1-i}{2}\right)$ a valid state? What are its outcome probabilities?">
Yes. $\left|\tfrac{1+i}{2}\right|^2 = \tfrac{1^2 + 1^2}{4} = \tfrac12$ and
$\left|\tfrac{1-i}{2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12$, so the squared moduli sum to 1 and
each outcome has probability $\tfrac12$. Note that the naive squares would give
$\left(\tfrac{1+i}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{i}{2}$ and
$\left(\tfrac{1-i}{2}\right)^2 = -\tfrac{i}{2}$, summing to 0, which is the
error the modulus bars exist to prevent.
</Check>
