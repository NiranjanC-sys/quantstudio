Deutsch-Jozsa returned one bit and threw away a whole measured string. This
lesson keeps the string. Change the promise from "constant or balanced" to
"linear with an unknown coefficient vector", and the same circuit, unmodified,
reads out all $n$ unknown bits in a single query. Classically you need $n$
queries and there is a clean information-theoretic reason why.

## The promise

There is a hidden string $s \in \{0,1\}^n$, and the oracle computes

$$
f(x) = s \cdot x = s_0 x_0 \oplus s_1 x_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus s_{n-1} x_{n-1},
$$

the inner product modulo 2. Find $s$.

Every such $f$ is either constant, when $s$ is the all-zeros string, or balanced,
for every other $s$. So Bernstein-Vazirani inputs are a subset of Deutsch-Jozsa
inputs, and the same circuit applies. The difference is what you do with the
answer.

<Margin label="Building the oracle">
$U_f\ket{x}\ket{y} = \ket{x}\ket{y \oplus s\cdot x}$ is one CNOT from each query
qubit $i$ with $s_i = 1$ onto the answer qubit. The oracle circuit therefore has
exactly $|s|$ two-qubit gates, which is how the figures below encode the secret.
</Margin>

## The derivation

Same three steps. Answer qubit in $\ket{-}$, Hadamards on the query register,
oracle once, Hadamards again.

Kickback turns the oracle into a phase:

$$
U_f \ket{x}\ket{-} = (-1)^{s \cdot x} \ket{x}\ket{-}.
$$

So after the oracle the query register holds

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_x (-1)^{s\cdot x} \ket{x}.
$$

Apply the second Hadamard layer and collect the amplitude of the outcome $z$:

$$
A_z = \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_x (-1)^{s \cdot x} (-1)^{x \cdot z}
    = \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_x (-1)^{x \cdot (s \oplus z)} .
$$

Now evaluate that sum. If $s \oplus z$ is the all-zeros string, every term is
$+1$ and the sum is $2^n$, giving $A_z = 1$. Otherwise $s \oplus z$ has at least
one bit set, say bit $j$; pair every $x$ with the string $x$ obtained by flipping
bit $j$, the two members of each pair contribute $+1$ and $-1$, and the sum is
exactly $0$.

$$
A_z = \begin{cases} 1 & z = s \\ 0 & z \ne s \end{cases}
$$

The output state is the basis state $\ket{s}$. Measure it and read the secret.
There is no probability to bound and no repetition to schedule.

## Why classical needs exactly $n$

**Upper bound.** Query $f$ on the standard basis vectors. The string with a 1 in
position $i$ and 0 elsewhere gives $f(e_i) = s_i$. Do that $n$ times and you have
every bit of $s$.

**Lower bound.** Each classical query returns a single bit, so $k$ queries return
at most $k$ bits of information. There are $2^n$ possible values of $s$ and they
are all consistent with each other until distinguished, so identifying $s$
requires at least $n$ bits, hence at least $n$ queries. The bound holds for
randomised algorithms too, up to the usual constant for allowing error.

So the separation is $n$ against 1, exact and provable, and unlike Deutsch-Jozsa
it does not evaporate when you allow the classical algorithm to be randomised: $n$
queries are needed either way. What it is not is exponential. Bernstein-Vazirani
buys a linear factor, which is real but modest.

<Callout tone="note" title="What the algorithm actually is">
$H^{\otimes n}$ is the Fourier transform over the group $\{0,1\}^n$ under XOR, and
$(-1)^{s \cdot x}$ is exactly one of its characters. So the algorithm is: encode
the secret as a character, then Fourier transform to read off which character it
was. That framing is the one that generalises, first to Simon in the next lesson
and then to the whole hidden subgroup framework in T2.M6.
</Callout>

<Check question="An oracle computes $f(x) = s \cdot x \oplus 1$, the linear function with a constant offset. What does the circuit return?">
Still $s$, with probability 1. The extra $\oplus 1$ multiplies every term of the
superposition by $-1$, which is a global phase and unobservable, so every amplitude
$A_z$ is negated and every probability is unchanged. You can see it in the figures:
the two surviving amplitudes would flip from $+0.7071$ and $-0.7071$ to $-0.7071$
and $+0.7071$, and the probabilities stay at $0.5$ each. The algorithm recovers
$s$ but cannot tell you the offset bit, for the same reason Deutsch cannot tell
$f(x) = 0$ from $f(x) = 1$.
</Check>
