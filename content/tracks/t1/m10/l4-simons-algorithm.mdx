Deutsch-Jozsa gave an exponential separation that a randomised classical
algorithm destroys in twenty queries. Bernstein-Vazirani gave a linear separation
that survives randomisation. Simon's problem gives both at once: an exponential
separation that no randomised classical algorithm can close. It is the first
algorithm in this track that a complexity theorist takes seriously, and it is the
direct ancestor of Shor.

## The promise

A function $f: \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^m$ is promised to be two-to-one with a
hidden period: there is a non-zero $s \in \{0,1\}^n$ such that

$$
f(x) = f(y) \iff y = x \text{ or } y = x \oplus s .
$$

Find $s$. The inputs fall into $2^{n-1}$ pairs, each pair differing by $s$, and
each pair sharing one output value. Nothing else about $f$ is known or assumed.

<Margin label="Reading the promise">
Setting $y = x \oplus s$ shows $f(x) = f(x \oplus s)$ for every $x$, so $s$ is a
period under XOR. The "only if" half is what makes $f$ exactly two-to-one rather
than merely periodic, and it is what makes the algorithm work.
</Margin>

## Each run samples a constraint

The circuit is the familiar sandwich with one change: the output register is $m$
qubits wide and is not put into $\ket{-}$, because we want $f$'s value written
into it rather than kicked back as a phase.

Hadamard the $n$ input qubits, apply $U_f\ket{x}\ket{0} = \ket{x}\ket{f(x)}$
once, Hadamard the input qubits again, measure the input register.

**After the oracle.** The state is
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_x \ket{x}\ket{f(x)}$. Group the $2^n$ terms into the
$2^{n-1}$ pairs that share an output:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{\text{pairs}} \big(\ket{x} + \ket{x \oplus s}\big) \ket{f(x)} .
$$

**After the second Hadamard layer.** Apply
$H^{\otimes n}\ket{x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_z (-1)^{x\cdot z}\ket{z}$ to each
pair:

$$
\ket{x} + \ket{x \oplus s} \;\longrightarrow\;
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_z (-1)^{x \cdot z}\Big(1 + (-1)^{s \cdot z}\Big) \ket{z}.
$$

The bracket is the whole algorithm. It equals $2$ when $s \cdot z = 0$ and $0$
when $s \cdot z = 1$. Every $z$ that fails the constraint is cancelled, exactly
and completely, by the two members of every pair interfering destructively.

So a measurement returns a uniformly random $z$ from the set
$\{z : s \cdot z = 0 \bmod 2\}$, which is a subspace of dimension $n-1$
containing $2^{n-1}$ strings. One run, one linear constraint on $s$.

## Two runs you can watch

Start with $n = 2$ and $s = 11$. Take $f(x_0 x_1) = x_0 \oplus x_1$, a single
output bit: $f(00) = f(11) = 0$ and $f(01) = f(10) = 1$, so the pairs differ by
$11$ as promised. The oracle is one CNOT from each input onto the output qubit.

the constraint and the other four have been cancelled to nothing.

## From constraints to $s$

Each run gives one vector $z$ with $z \cdot s = 0$. Collect several and solve the
homogeneous linear system over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ by Gaussian elimination. Once the
collected vectors span the full $(n-1)$-dimensional constraint space, the solution
space is exactly $\{0, s\}$, and discarding the trivial solution leaves $s$.

How many runs? The vectors are uniform over an $(n-1)$-dimensional space, so
this is the classic question of how many random vectors span. With $k = n-1$ and
$m$ samples the probability of spanning is
$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(1 - 2^{\,j-m}\right)$:

| runs, $n = 50$ | probability of spanning |
| --- | --- |
| $49$ | $0.288788$ |
| $52$ | $0.880116$ |
| $59$ | $0.999024$ |
| $69$ | $0.999999$ |

The bare minimum of $n-1$ runs works about 29% of the time, and every extra run
roughly halves the remaining failure probability. Ten extra runs put you past one
failure in a million, for any $n$. So $O(n)$ queries and $O(n^3)$ classical work
for the elimination, and the algorithm is polynomial end to end.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The zero vector is a legal sample">
$z = 0\cdots0$ satisfies $s \cdot z = 0$ and is returned with probability
$2^{-(n-1)}$ like any other member of the subspace. It carries no information and
contributes nothing to the rank. Count useful samples by rank, not by runs.
</Callout>

## The classical side

Classically you are searching for a collision. Query $f$ on distinct inputs until
two of them return the same value; then those two inputs differ by $s$ and you are
done. By the birthday bound, $2^{n/2}$ or so queries are needed before a collision
becomes likely among $2^{n-1}$ possible outputs, and that intuition is provable:
any randomised classical algorithm that finds $s$ with bounded error needs
$\Omega(2^{n/2})$ queries.

Put the two sides together for $n = 50$: about 60 quantum queries against roughly
$2^{25}$, some 33 million, classical ones. That is the first exponential
separation in this curriculum that randomisation does not erase, and the reason it
matters historically is structural rather than practical. Simon's problem is
period finding over $\{0,1\}^n$ under XOR. Replace that group with $\Z_N$ under
addition, replace the Hadamard transform with the quantum Fourier transform over
$\Z_N$, and the same algorithm becomes order finding, which is Shor's algorithm.

<Check question="You run Simon four times on $n = 4$ and collect $z = 0000$, $1100$, $1100$ and $0011$. Can you determine $s$?">
Not yet. The distinct non-zero vectors you hold are $1100$ and $0011$, so you have
rank 2 out of the 3 constraints needed for $n = 4$. The solution space of
$\{z \cdot s = 0\}$ for those two constraints has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$, so four
candidates for $s$ remain: $0000$, $1100$, $0011$ and $1111$, of which three are
non-zero and all are consistent. The repeated $1100$ and the zero vector added no
rank at all, which is exactly the effect the table above accounts for. Run the
circuit again until the rank reaches 3.
</Check>
