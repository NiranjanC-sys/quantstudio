$T_1$ and $T_2$ are the two numbers every device datasheet leads with, and they
are not theoretical parameters. Each is defined by an experiment you can describe
in three sentences, and each answers a different question: how long the qubit
keeps its energy, and how long it keeps its phase. This lesson defines both
operationally and derives the one inequality that ties them together.

## $T_1$: the relaxation experiment

Prepare $\ket{1}$ with an $X$ gate. Wait a time $t$, doing nothing. Measure. Plot
the fraction of shots that still return 1 as a function of $t$.

The curve is an exponential decay:

$$
P_1(t) = e^{-t/T_1},
$$

and $T_1$ is defined as the time constant of that fit. It is not the time until
the qubit is dead; it is the time at which the excited-state population has fallen
to $1/e$.

<Margin label="Why exponential">
The decay is memoryless: an excited qubit that has survived to time $t$ is
statistically identical to one just prepared. A memoryless waiting time is
exponentially distributed, which is the same argument that gives radioactive
half-lives.
</Margin>

Concrete numbers for $T_1 = 100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$:

| wait $t$ | $t/T_1$ | $P_1(t)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ | $0.1$ | $0.904837$ |
| $50\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ | $0.5$ | $0.606531$ |
| $100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ | $1.0$ | $0.367879$ |
| $200\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ | $2.0$ | $0.135335$ |

The half-life is $T_1 \ln 2 = 0.693\,T_1$, so about $69\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ here. Put
that beside a two-qubit gate time of a few hundred nanoseconds and you get the
number that governs everything in the next three lessons: a few hundred two-qubit
gates fit inside one $T_1$, and that is the whole budget.

## $T_2$: the Ramsey experiment

Relaxation is about population. Coherence is about phase, and to see phase you
need an interference experiment.

Prepare $\ket{+}$ with a Hadamard. Wait a time $t$, during which the qubit
accumulates a phase $\varphi$ that you can control by detuning the drive. Apply a
second Hadamard and measure. The outcome probability is

$$
P_1 = \sin^2\frac{\varphi}{2},
$$

so sweeping $\varphi$ traces out a fringe running from 0 to 1 and back.

With $\varphi = \pi/3$ the noiseless answer is $\sin^2(\pi/6) = 0.25$, which is
what the panel reports. Set $\varphi = \pi/2$ and it is $0.5$; set $\varphi = \pi$
and it is $1$. Full contrast, from 0 to 1, at every wait time.

On hardware the contrast shrinks as $t$ grows. The measured probability becomes

$$
P_1(t) = \half - \half e^{-t/T_2}\cos\varphi,
$$

a fringe of amplitude $e^{-t/T_2}$ around $\tfrac12$. At $t = 0$ the amplitude is
$1$ and the fringe runs the full range. At $t = T_2$ it is $0.367879$, so the
fringe runs only from $0.316$ to $0.684$. At $t \gg T_2$ the fringe is flat at
$\tfrac12$ and the qubit has become a fair coin: no phase information remains.
$T_2$ is defined as the time constant of that envelope.

<Callout tone="note" title="T2 and T2 star">
The raw Ramsey decay is usually called $T_2^*$, because it includes slow drifts in
the qubit frequency that are not truly random. A spin-echo sequence, which inserts
an $X$ halfway through the wait, refocuses those drifts and yields a longer time
constant conventionally called $T_2$. Datasheets differ about which one they
quote; check.
</Callout>

## Why $T_2 \le 2T_1$

The bound comes from noticing that relaxation destroys phase as a side effect.

Take the qubit in $\ket{+}$, so the off-diagonal element of its density matrix,
the coherence, has magnitude $\tfrac12$. Coherence is a statement about a
superposition of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, so it survives only in the branch where
the qubit has not relaxed. The probability of not having relaxed is $e^{-t/T_1}$,
and the coherence, which is a product of amplitudes rather than probabilities,
carries the square root of that:

$$
|\rho_{01}(t)| \le \half \, e^{-t/(2T_1)}.
$$

Write the general decay as $e^{-t/T_2}$ and combine the two channels:

$$
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\varphi},
$$

where $T_\varphi$ is the pure-dephasing time, describing frequency noise that
scrambles the phase without moving any population. Since $T_\varphi > 0$, the
first term alone gives

$$
T_2 \le 2 T_1 .
$$

Equality holds exactly when $T_\varphi \to \infty$, that is when there is no pure
dephasing at all and the only thing spoiling coherence is relaxation. Real devices
are usually well below the bound, with $T_2$ nearer $T_1$ than $2T_1$, because
low-frequency flux and charge noise supply plenty of pure dephasing.

<Check question="A device reports $T_1 = 120\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and $T_2 = 260\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. What should you conclude?">
That one of the numbers is wrong, or that they were measured on different qubits,
or at different times, or that $T_2$ was extracted from a fit whose model does not
match the data. $T_2 \le 2T_1 = 240\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ is not a rule of thumb, it is a
consequence of relaxation destroying coherence at half the population rate, so
$260\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ is not physically available. The productive response is to
re-run both experiments on the same qubit in the same calibration window rather
than to reason about what a $T_2$ of $260\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ would imply.
</Check>

## What this buys you

Two operational definitions and one inequality is enough to make a depth estimate
before you run anything. Given a gate time $t_g$ and a coherence time $T$, a
circuit of depth $d$ occupies $d \, t_g$ of wall clock, and the fraction of
coherence you have consumed is $d\,t_g/T$. When that ratio approaches 1 the result
is noise. The next three lessons turn this back-of-the-envelope into an actual
fidelity number.
