T1.M8 told you to count depth. T1.M11 has now told you what a gate error is. This
lesson multiplies the two together and produces the single most useful estimate in
practical quantum computing: given a device's error rates, how big a circuit can
you run before the answer is noise? The arithmetic is one line, and it explains
almost every design decision in the rest of the curriculum.

## The product rule

Model each gate as succeeding with probability $1 - e$ and failing independently.
Then a circuit of $N$ gates produces the right state with probability

$$
P_{\text{success}} \approx \prod_{i=1}^{N} (1 - e_i).
$$

The approximation is crude in two directions. It assumes independence, which
coherent errors violate, and it treats "failed" as "gave a useless answer", which
is pessimistic because some errors cancel. It is nonetheless the estimate every
practitioner does in their head, and it is accurate enough to be the deciding
factor in whether to attempt a circuit at all.

<Margin label="Two rates, not one">
Device datasheets list single-qubit and two-qubit error rates separately, and the
two-qubit rate is typically about ten times the single-qubit rate. Any estimate
that uses one average rate for both will be wrong by an order of magnitude on
circuits dominated by entangling gates.
</Margin>

## The depth at which you lose

Take a circuit that is a chain of two-qubit gates, so depth and two-qubit gate
count are the same number $d$, and ask for the $d$ at which success falls to
one half:

$$
(1 - e)^d = \half \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
d = \frac{\ln \half}{\ln(1-e)} \approx \frac{0.693}{e}.
$$

| two-qubit error $e$ | depth for 50% success |
| --- | --- |
| $0.02$ | $34.31$ |
| $0.01$ | $68.97$ |
| $0.005$ | $138.28$ |
| $0.001$ | $692.80$ |

Read the third row as the practical statement it is. A device with a half-percent
two-qubit error rate gives you roughly 138 sequential entangling gates before the
result is a coin flip. Not 138 per qubit, not 138 per layer: 138 in a chain.

Push past it and the decay is unforgiving, because it is exponential:

| two-qubit gates at $e = 0.005$ | success |
| --- | --- |
| $100$ | $0.605770$ |
| $500$ | $0.081572$ |
| $1000$ | $0.006654$ |

At a thousand gates you would need to repeat the experiment about 150 times to see
one clean shot, and you would have no way to know which one it was.

## Which gates to cut

Now the practical question. You have a circuit with 200 single-qubit gates at
$e_1 = 0.0005$ and 100 two-qubit gates at $e_2 = 0.005$. Its success estimate is

$$
(1 - 0.0005)^{200} \times (1 - 0.005)^{100}
= 0.904815 \times 0.605770 = 0.548110 .
$$

An optimisation pass can remove 50 gates. Which 50?

| version | success |
| --- | --- |
| baseline: 200 single, 100 two-qubit | $0.548110$ |
| remove 50 single-qubit gates | $0.561989$ |
| remove 50 two-qubit gates | $0.704229$ |

Removing 50 single-qubit gates gains $0.0139$. Removing 50 two-qubit gates gains
$0.1561$, eleven times as much. The ratio is just $e_2 / e_1$, and it is why every
transpiler optimisation level is scored on two-qubit gate count first and
everything else second.

The same arithmetic explains the cost of connectivity. A SWAP is three CNOTs, so
every SWAP the router inserts to bring two logical qubits together costs
$1 - (1 - 0.005)^3 = 1.5\%$ of your success probability. On a device where the
qubits you want to entangle are five hops apart, routing can easily cost more than
the algorithm.

<Callout tone="note" title="Depth and count are different resources">
Gate count sets the error you accumulate from gates. Depth sets the wall-clock
duration, and therefore the decoherence you accumulate while *idle*. A circuit can
be short in gates and long in time, and its idle qubits are still relaxing.
</Callout>

## Idle time is not free

Take the two GHZ circuits from T1.M8.L1. Both have one Hadamard and seven CNOTs,
so both accumulate the same gate error: $1 - (1-0.005)^7 = 0.034479$.

Both panels show $\ket{00000000}$ and $\ket{11111111}$ at $0.5$ each, and both
footers report 7 two-qubit gates. The footers differ on depth: 8 against 4.

Put a clock on it. With a two-qubit gate time of $300\,\mathrm{ns}$, the ladder
runs for $2.4\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and the tree for $1.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. With
$T_2 = 100\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, each qubit accumulates a dephasing error of roughly
$t/T_2$, which is $0.024$ for the ladder and $0.012$ for the tree, per qubit.
Across eight qubits that is a difference of about $0.096$ in accumulated idle
error, three times larger than the entire gate-error budget of either circuit.
Same gates, same state, twice the damage.

<Check question="Your circuit has depth 400 in two-qubit gates and the device quotes $e_2 = 0.008$. Is it worth running?">
No. The depth for 50% success is $0.693/0.008 \approx 87$, and at depth 400 the
estimate is $(1 - 0.008)^{400} = 0.0402$, so about one shot in twenty-five carries
the signal and the rest are noise you cannot label. Three responses are worth
considering, in order of effect: cut the two-qubit gate count, which is where all
the error is; reduce depth by parallelising, which cuts idle decoherence; or
restructure the algorithm to need a shallower circuit. Repeating the experiment
more times does not help, because the error is bias rather than variance and more
shots estimate the wrong distribution more precisely.
</Check>
