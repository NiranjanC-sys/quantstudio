Every circuit in this track so far has been exact. Run any of them on hardware and
the numbers come out wrong, and the interesting question is not "how wrong" but
"wrong in which of several distinguishable ways". A practitioner who can name the
mechanism from the shape of the deviation can fix it. One who cannot is left
adding a fudge factor. This lesson is the taxonomy, four modules before T4
formalises any of it.

## Four sources, four timescales

**Relaxation.** A qubit in $\ket{1}$ is an excited state, and excited states decay
by emitting energy into whatever they are coupled to. The rate is characterised by
$T_1$, and on current superconducting devices $T_1$ is on the order of a hundred
microseconds. Relaxation has a preferred direction: it pushes states towards
$\ket{0}$ and never the other way.

**Dephasing.** Even a qubit that never decays loses the *phase* relationship
between $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, because its transition frequency wanders as the
local field fluctuates. The rate is $T_2$, generally shorter than $T_1$ and always
at most $2T_1$. Dephasing has no preferred direction on the $z$ axis; it shrinks
the equator of the Bloch sphere.

**Control error.** A gate is a shaped pulse. If the amplitude is a fraction of a
percent high, every gate over-rotates by the same small angle. If the drive is
slightly off resonance, every gate rotates about a slightly tilted axis. Crosstalk
belongs here too: a pulse aimed at one qubit reaches its neighbours.

**Readout error.** Measurement discriminates two distributions that overlap. A
qubit in $\ket{0}$ is sometimes reported as 1 and a qubit in $\ket{1}$ sometimes as
0, at rates that are typically different from each other. Readout takes on the
order of a microsecond, an order of magnitude longer than a gate, and its error
rate is usually the largest single number on a device calibration page.

<Margin label="Orders of magnitude">
On a typical current superconducting device: single-qubit gate tens of
nanoseconds, two-qubit gate a few hundred nanoseconds, readout around a
microsecond, $T_1$ and $T_2$ around a hundred microseconds. The ratio that matters
is gate time to coherence time, and it is roughly one to a thousand.
</Margin>

## Coherent and incoherent errors scale differently

This is the distinction that changes how you debug, and it is visible without any
noise model at all. A coherent error is a small unitary you did not ask for. It
is deterministic and it *repeats*, so the errors add as amplitudes.

$d$ gates the total unwanted rotation is $d\varepsilon$, and the probability of
finding the qubit in the wrong state is $\sin^2(d\varepsilon/2)$. One gate:

Read the three numbers: $0.000625$, $0.061209$, $0.708073$. Ten gates gave
roughly a hundred times the error of one, and forty gates gave over a thousand
times. That is quadratic growth, $\sin^2(d\varepsilon/2) \approx d^2\varepsilon^2/4$
for small angles, and it is what "errors add as amplitudes" means numerically.

An incoherent error behaves differently. Each gate independently damages the state
with probability $p$, the damage is random rather than systematic, and the
survival probability is $(1-p)^d$, which decays linearly at first:
$1 - dp$ for small $dp$. Same per-gate error rate, very different behaviour at
depth.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Why this matters for debugging">
A coherent error is a calibration bug and it is fixable: recalibrate, or echo it
away with a pulse sequence that inverts it halfway through. An incoherent error is
physics and it is not fixable, only mitigated or corrected. If your fidelity falls
quadratically with depth, go and look at the calibration before you blame the
environment.
</Callout>

## Why readout sits outside the gate budget

Gate errors and readout errors enter a result in structurally different ways, and
that is why every error budget lists them separately.

Gate error corrupts the *state*. It happens once per gate, it compounds with
depth, and by the time you measure, the thing you are measuring is already the
wrong state. There is nothing about the outcome that tells you the state was
wrong.

Readout error corrupts the *classical bit*, once, at the end. It does not compound
with depth, it does not depend on what the circuit did, and crucially it is
described by a small classical matrix: the probability of reporting $j$ when the
state was $i$. On one qubit that is a two by two confusion matrix with two
parameters, $e_0$ and $e_1$, and you can measure it directly by preparing
$\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ and counting mistakes.

Because it is a classical linear map applied after everything else, it can be
inverted. Measure the confusion matrix, apply its inverse to your observed
distribution, and recover an unbiased estimate of what the state distribution was.
That is measurement-error mitigation, you will implement it in the next lesson but
one, and it works precisely because readout error factors out of the rest of the
circuit.

<Check question="You run the same circuit at depth 20 and depth 40 and find the error probability goes from 4% to 15%. Coherent or incoherent?">
Coherent, most likely. Doubling the depth roughly quadrupled the error, which is
the quadratic signature $d^2\varepsilon^2/4$. An incoherent error at 4% for depth
20 corresponds to about $p = 0.002$ per gate, and at depth 40 that would give
$1 - 0.998^{40} \approx 7.7\%$, not 15%. The test is worth running deliberately:
sweep depth, plot the error, and look at whether the curve starts out linear or
quadratic before it saturates.
</Check>
