The last two lessons described noise. This one makes you compute with it. Two
models, both linear, both a dozen lines of Python: the depolarising channel, which
is the standard stand-in for "something went wrong and we do not know what", and
the readout confusion matrix, which is the only error in this module you can
simply undo.

## The depolarising channel

A pure state is a vector, but a state that *might* have been damaged is a mixture
and needs a density matrix. For one qubit,
$\rho = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$ when the state is pure, and a general
$\rho$ is any positive semidefinite matrix with $\tr \rho = 1$.

The depolarising channel with parameter $p$ replaces the state with the maximally
mixed state with probability $p$ and leaves it alone otherwise:

$$
\mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\,\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2}.
$$

Two properties to check before you code it. It is **trace preserving**, since
$\tr\rho = 1$ and $\tr(I/2) = 1$ give $\tr \mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p) + p = 1$.
And it is **linear** in $\rho$, so composing two of them just multiplies the
surviving weights: $\mathcal{D}_q \circ \mathcal{D}_p$ has surviving weight
$(1-p)(1-q)$.

<Margin label="Why this channel">
Any noise process, averaged over a random Clifford twirl, becomes depolarising
with the same average error rate. That is why randomised benchmarking reports a
single depolarising parameter and why it is the honest default when you have one
number and no model.
</Margin>

The fidelity with $\ket{0}$ is the diagonal entry
$F = \bra{0}\rho\ket{0} = \rho_{00}$. Starting from the pure state
$\rho = \ketbra{0}{0}$:

$$
F(p) = (1-p) \cdot 1 + p \cdot \half = 1 - \frac{p}{2}.
$$

| $p$ | $F$ |
| --- | --- |
| $0$ | $1.000$ |
| $0.1$ | $0.950$ |
| $0.37$ | $0.815$ |
| $1$ | $0.500$ |

Note the floor. Even at $p = 1$, when the state has been completely destroyed, the
fidelity with $\ket{0}$ is $0.5$, because a coin flip agrees with any fixed answer
half the time. A fidelity of $0.5$ on one qubit means no information at all, not
half the information.

## The readout confusion matrix

Readout error is classical. Let $e_0$ be the probability of reporting 1 when the
state was $\ket{0}$, and $e_1$ the probability of reporting 0 when the state was
$\ket{1}$. Then the observed distribution $m$ relates to the true distribution
$q$ by

$$
m = A q, \qquad
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - e_0 & e_1 \\ e_0 & 1 - e_1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

$A$ has determinant $(1-e_0)(1-e_1) - e_0 e_1 = 1 - e_0 - e_1$, which is non-zero
unless $e_0 + e_1 = 1$, the case where readout is pure noise. So invert it:

$$
q = \frac{1}{1 - e_0 - e_1}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 - e_1 & -e_1 \\ -e_0 & 1 - e_0 \end{pmatrix} m .
$$

Worked example with $e_0 = e_1 = 0.05$. The determinant is $0.9$. Observed
frequencies $m = (0.95, 0.05)$, which is 950 counts of 0 and 50 of 1 out of 1000:

$$
q_0 = \frac{0.95 \cdot 0.95 - 0.05 \cdot 0.05}{0.9}
    = \frac{0.9}{0.9} = 1, \qquad q_1 = 0 .
$$

Exactly right: a state that was really $\ket{0}$ every time, misreported 5% of the
time, corrected back to certainty. Observed $m = (0.9, 0.1)$ instead and the
correction returns $q_0 = 0.944444$ and $q_1 = 0.055556$.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The inverse is not a probability distribution">
Feed the corrector $m = (1.0, 0.0)$ with $e_0 = e_1 = 0.05$ and it returns
$q_0 = 1.055556$ and $q_1 = -0.055556$. A negative probability. Matrix inversion
knows nothing about the simplex, and with finite shots the observed $m$ routinely
lands outside the image of $A$ restricted to valid distributions. Production
mitigation therefore uses a constrained least-squares fit instead of a raw
inverse. Your implementation should return the raw inverse, because that is what
the tests check and because seeing the negative number is the point.
</Callout>

```python
    """Invert the confusion matrix and return corrected probabilities."""
```

Three things the tests will catch.

`depolarise` must work for any matrix size, not just two by two, because the
maximally mixed state on $n$ dimensions is $I/n$ and the hidden tests use the same
function on a two-qubit density matrix. Divide by the actual dimension.

`fidelity_with_zero` must return a real `float`. A density matrix stored with
complex entries has $\rho_{00}$ complex-typed even though its imaginary part is
zero, and a comparison against a float will fail on the type before it fails on
the value.

`correct_readout` must normalise the counts to frequencies before inverting, and
must not divide by zero when the total is zero.

Public tests: depolarising $\ketbra{0}{0}$ with $p = 0.1$ leaves fidelity $0.95$,
and with $e_0 = e_1 = 0$ the corrected probabilities equal the raw ones. Hidden
tests add the $p = 1$ floor at $0.5$, trace preservation at $p = 0.37$, and a
property check that fidelity decreases monotonically in $p$.

<Check question="Your corrected distribution for a two-qubit experiment sums to $1.0$ but contains one negative entry. Is the correction wrong?">
The arithmetic is right and the answer is unusable. Inverting a stochastic matrix
preserves the sum, because every column of $A$ sums to 1 and therefore so does
every column of $A^{-1}$ applied to a vector summing to 1, but nothing preserves
non-negativity. A negative entry is a signal that the observed counts were not
consistent with any true distribution under your assumed $e_0$ and $e_1$, which
usually means shot noise, a stale calibration, or correlated readout error across
qubits that a per-qubit model cannot express. The fix is a constrained fit, not a
different inverse.
</Check>
