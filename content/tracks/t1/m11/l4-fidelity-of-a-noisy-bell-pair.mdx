Every protocol in T1.M7 assumed a perfect Bell pair. Real ones are not perfect,
and the single number that says how imperfect is the fidelity with the intended
state. This lesson derives that number in closed form for the standard noise
model, checks it against a simulation, and finds the exact point at which a noisy
Bell pair stops being entangled at all.

## Setup

Prepare $\ket{\Phi^+}$ the usual way and then hit each qubit independently with
the depolarising channel from the previous lesson,

$$
\mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\frac{I}{2},
$$

modelling everything that went wrong during preparation and distribution. The
noiseless circuit is the two-gate one you have run a dozen times:

The quantity we want is

$$
F(p) = \bra{\Phi^+} \big(\mathcal{D}_p \otimes \mathcal{D}_p\big)
\big(\ketbra{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}\big) \ket{\Phi^+} .
$$

## Rewriting the channel as random Paulis

Direct matrix multiplication would work and would teach nothing. Instead rewrite
the channel in Pauli form. The identity

$$
\frac{1}{4}\big(\rho + X\rho X + Y \rho Y + Z \rho Z\big) = \frac{I}{2}
$$

holds for every single-qubit $\rho$ with unit trace. Substituting it,

$$
\mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = \left(1 - \frac{3p}{4}\right)\rho
+ \frac{p}{4}\big(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z\big).
$$

Read that as a random process: with probability $q = 3p/4$ some non-identity Pauli
is applied, uniformly chosen among $X$, $Y$ and $Z$, and with probability $1-q$
nothing happens. Depolarising noise *is* a random Pauli error, which is why
Pauli errors are the only ones T4 will bother to correct.

<Margin label="Two parameters, one channel">
$p$ is the depolarising probability and $q = 3p/4$ is the probability of an actual
Pauli error. Papers use both and rarely say which. Check whether the fully
depolarising case is $p = 1$ or $q = 3/4$.
</Margin>

## The overlap table

Apply an independent random Pauli to each qubit. The resulting state is
$(P_A \otimes P_B)\ket{\Phi^+}$, and its overlap with $\ket{\Phi^+}$ takes only
two values:

| | $P_B = I$ | $X$ | $Y$ | $Z$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P_A = I$ | $1$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $X$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $Y$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ |
| $Z$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ |

The diagonal is the whole story: the state survives exactly when the *same* Pauli
hit both qubits, and any mismatched pair sends $\ket{\Phi^+}$ to one of the other
three Bell states, which are orthogonal to it. The reason is the transpose
identity $(M \otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = (I \otimes M^{\mathsf T})\ket{\Phi^+}$: a
Pauli on the left can be moved to the right, where it cancels against an identical
Pauli.

## The formula

Sum the diagonal:

$$
F = \underbrace{(1-q)^2}_{\text{no error at all}}
+ \underbrace{3\left(\frac{q}{3}\right)^2}_{\text{same Pauli twice}}
= (1-q)^2 + \frac{q^2}{3}.
$$

Substitute $q = 3p/4$:

$$
F(p) = \left(1 - \frac{3p}{4}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{3p}{4}\right)^2
= 1 - \frac{3p}{2} + \frac{9p^2}{16} + \frac{3p^2}{16}
= 1 - \frac{3p}{2} + \frac{3p^2}{4}.
$$

Evaluate it:

| $p$ | $1 - 3p/2$ | $3p^2/4$ | $F(p)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.01$ | $0.985000$ | $0.000075$ | $0.985075$ |
| $0.05$ | $0.925000$ | $0.001875$ | $0.926875$ |
| $0.10$ | $0.850000$ | $0.007500$ | $0.857500$ |
| $0.50$ | $0.250000$ | $0.187500$ | $0.437500$ |
| $1.00$ | $-0.500000$ | $0.750000$ | $0.250000$ |

Two sanity checks. At $p = 0$ the formula gives $F = 1$, as the noiseless figure
above shows. At $p = 1$ it gives $F = 0.25$, which is right: both qubits have been
replaced by maximally mixed states, the two-qubit state is $I/4$, and the overlap
of $I/4$ with any pure state is $1/4$. The formula never goes below its floor even
though the linear term alone would go negative.

## Why the linear term dominates

Look at the third column. At $p = 0.01$ the quadratic correction is $0.000075$,
which is $0.0076\%$ of the fidelity: entirely negligible. At $p = 0.05$ it is
$0.001875$, still under a fifth of a percent. Only around $p = 0.5$ does it become
comparable to the linear term.

So in the regime any real device operates in, the working rule is

$$
F \approx 1 - \frac{3p}{2},
$$

a fidelity loss of one and a half times the depolarising parameter, per Bell pair.
The factor of $3/2$ is $2 \times 3/4$: two qubits, each with an error probability
$q = 3p/4$, and to first order the errors are independent so the losses add.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Fidelity 0.5 is not half-good">
On two qubits the floor is $0.25$, so a fidelity of $0.5$ is halfway between
useless and perfect on a scale that starts at a quarter, not at zero. Always ask
what the fidelity of a random state would be before deciding whether a number is
impressive.
</Callout>

## Where the entanglement actually dies

Because the noise is symmetric, the damaged state is a Werner state: weight $F$ on
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ and weight $(1-F)/3$ on each of the other three Bell states. Such a
state is entangled if and only if $F > 1/2$. Solve $F(p) = 1/2$:

$$
\frac{3p^2}{4} - \frac{3p}{2} + \half = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
p^2 - 2p + \frac{2}{3} = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
p = 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt3} = 0.422650 .
$$

Above $p = 0.422650$ the pair is separable: it still has correlations, but they
are the kind a classical shared random variable could produce, and no protocol in
T1.M7 works with it. Below that threshold it remains entangled, though a
teleportation fidelity of $0.5$ is nothing to celebrate.

<Check question="You measure a Bell-pair fidelity of $0.92$. What depolarising parameter does that correspond to, and what does the same noise do to a three-qubit GHZ state?">
Solving $1 - 1.5p + 0.75p^2 = 0.92$ gives $p = 0.054837$, since
$p = 1 - \sqrt{1 - 0.08/0.75}$. For GHZ the counting changes: a single Pauli error
on any one of three qubits sends the GHZ state to something orthogonal, so to
first order the fidelity is $1 - 3q$ with $q = 3p/4 = 0.041128$, which gives
$1 - 0.123383 = 0.876617$. The exact value at that $p$ is $0.882173$, a little
higher because two-error terms can cancel. The shape to remember is that fidelity
decays roughly like $(1-q)^n$ in the number of qubits, which is the same
exponential in circuit size that the next lesson develops for depth.
</Check>
