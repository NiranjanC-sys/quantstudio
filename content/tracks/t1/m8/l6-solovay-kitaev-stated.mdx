The last lesson proved that $H$, $T$ and CX can approximate anything. It said
nothing about how long the approximation takes. "Dense" is compatible with
"needs $2^{100}$ gates to get three decimal places", and if that were the cost
the theory would be a curiosity. Solovay-Kitaev is the theorem that says it is
not: the cost is polylogarithmic in the accuracy, and that single fact is why
nobody argues about which gate set to build hardware around.

## The statement

Let $G$ be a finite set of gates in $SU(2)$ that is closed under inverses and
generates a dense subgroup. Then for any $U \in SU(2)$ and any
$\varepsilon > 0$ there is a product of

$$
O\!\left(\log^{c}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
$$

gates from $G$ that approximates $U$ to within $\varepsilon$ in operator norm.
Nielsen and Chuang state the exponent as $c \approx 2$. Explicit constructive
versions of the proof, which give you an algorithm that finds the sequence rather
than only proving one exists, run with a larger exponent, close to 4.

<Margin label="What the theorem does not give you">
It does not give a constant. $O(\log^2(1/\varepsilon))$ hides a factor that
depends on the gate set and on how the recursion is seeded. Two dense gate sets
with the same asymptotic bound can differ by a large constant in practice, which
is why compiler teams still care which one they target.
</Margin>

## Reading the bound as a number

The useful way to hold this is as a ratio rather than an absolute count. With
$c = 2$, the length grows as $\log^2(1/\varepsilon)$:

| $\varepsilon$ | $\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ | $\log_2^2(1/\varepsilon)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{-2}$ | $6.644$ | $44.1$ |
| $10^{-3}$ | $9.966$ | $99.3$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $19.932$ | $397.3$ |
| $10^{-9}$ | $29.897$ | $893.9$ |

Going from three decimal places to six multiplies $\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ by
exactly 2, so it multiplies $\log^2(1/\varepsilon)$ by exactly 4. A thousandfold
improvement in accuracy costs a factor of four in gates. Compare that with the
alternative the theorem rules out, where accuracy $\varepsilon$ would cost
$1/\varepsilon$ gates: there, the same thousandfold improvement would cost a
factor of a thousand.

## Watching a sequence converge

You can see the mechanism without any of the recursion. Take the word
$H, T, H, T$, which up to global phase is the irrational rotation from the last
lesson, and apply it repeatedly to $\ket{0}$. If the rotation angle were a
rational multiple of $2\pi$ the state would cycle through finitely many points.
It is not, so the orbit is dense on a circle of latitude and repeatedly comes
back near where it started, closer and closer as you allow more repetitions.

The residual error falls from $0.1464$ to $0.0115$ to $0.000790$ as the sequence
goes from 4 to 24 to 92 gates. This is brute force, not Solovay-Kitaev, and brute
force is exactly what the theorem improves on. A counting argument gives the
scale: there are about $|G|^{\ell}$ words of length $\ell$ and $SU(2)$ is three
dimensional, so the best a brute-force search over that many words can do is
accuracy of order $|G|^{-\ell/3}$, which is fine asymptotically but requires
searching exponentially many words. Solovay-Kitaev replaces
the search with a recursion that refines a coarse approximation using group
commutators, and it is the recursion that turns $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ into a
polynomial rather than an exponent.

<Callout tone="note" title="Where the error budget goes">
A circuit of $m$ gates needs each gate approximated to about $\varepsilon/m$ for
the whole circuit to be within $\varepsilon$, because operator-norm errors add.
The total overhead is therefore $O(m \log^c(m/\varepsilon))$, still polylog in the
accuracy and only linear in the circuit size.
</Callout>

## Why the choice of gate set stops mattering

Suppose you have two dense finite gate sets, $A$ and $B$. Every gate in $A$ can
be approximated by a sequence from $B$ of length $O(\log^c(1/\varepsilon))$, and
vice versa. So any circuit written in $A$ can be rewritten in $B$ at a
polylogarithmic multiplicative cost, and any complexity class defined by
polynomial-size circuits over $A$ equals the one defined over $B$.

That is a strong statement and it has two consequences worth separating.

**For theory,** it means BQP is well defined without naming a gate set. Papers
can say "a universal gate set" and mean it, and nobody has to redo a proof
because a device changed its native operations.

**For engineering,** it means the hardware designer picks native gates for
physical reasons, not computational ones. Ion traps get $R_{xx}$ because that is
what a shared motional mode gives you. IBM superconducting devices get ECR
because that is what cross-resonance gives you. Neither choice restricts what can
be computed, and the compiler absorbs the difference at a cost the theorem bounds.

What the theorem does *not* do is make everything cheap. Under error correction
the constant in front matters enormously, because each $T$ in the approximating
sequence needs a distilled magic state, and $T$-count is the dominant cost in
every resource estimate in T3.M4. Solovay-Kitaev tells you the count grows slowly.
It does not tell you it is small.

<Check question="You need 500 different single-qubit rotations, each to accuracy $10^{-6}$, and you are told each one costs about 400 gates. What accuracy would 800 gates per rotation buy, on the $c = 2$ reading?">
About $3 \times 10^{-9}$. If length scales as $\log^2(1/\varepsilon)$ then
$\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ scales as the square root of the length, so doubling the
budget multiplies $\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ by $\sqrt2$: from $19.932$ to about
$28.19$, and $2^{-28.19} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-9}$. Twice the gates bought a
factor of roughly three hundred in accuracy. The asymmetry is the whole point, and
the number to remember is the one from the table: every doubling of
$\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ costs a factor of four in gates, not a factor of a
thousand.
</Check>
