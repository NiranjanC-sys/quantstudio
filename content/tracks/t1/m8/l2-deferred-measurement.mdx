In the teleportation lesson you analysed a protocol with a measurement in the
middle by drawing a circuit with no measurement in the middle, and then claimed
the two agreed. That step was not a shortcut. It is a theorem, it has a name, and
it is the single most useful structural fact about the circuit model: any
measurement can be postponed to the end.

## The claim

**Principle of deferred measurement.** If a circuit measures a qubit and uses the
classical outcome to control later operations, you may instead leave the qubit
unmeasured, replace each classically controlled operation by the corresponding
quantum-controlled operation on that qubit, and measure at the very end. The
distribution over all measured bits is unchanged.

<Margin label="Scope">
The principle is about output distributions. The two circuits are not the same
map on states, and after the measured qubit is measured they are not even in the
same Hilbert space. What matches is everything you can observe.
</Margin>

## Proof on the smallest example

Take one qubit in $\ket{\psi} = c_0\ket{0} + c_1\ket{1}$ and a second qubit in
$\ket{0}$. The classical version measures qubit 0 and applies $X$ to qubit 1 if
the outcome was 1.

*Measure first.* The outcome is $a$ with probability $|c_a|^2$, and qubit 0
collapses to $\ket{a}$. Then $X^a$ takes qubit 1 from $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{a}$. The
pair of classical bits recorded at the end is $(a, a)$, with probability
$|c_a|^2$.

*Defer.* Apply $\mathrm{CX}_{0 \to 1}$ instead. Linearity gives

$$
\mathrm{CX}\big(c_0\ket{0} + c_1\ket{1}\big)\ket{0}
= c_0\ket{00} + c_1\ket{11}.
$$

Measuring both qubits returns $(0,0)$ with probability $|c_0|^2$ and $(1,1)$ with
probability $|c_1|^2$. Identical distribution. The proof for a general controlled
operation is the same two lines with $U$ in place of $X$: the classical branch
structure and the coherent branch structure are the same sum, and measurement in
the computational basis cannot tell which one you built.

The second circuit contains a classical condition, so the simulator cannot
evolve a single statevector through it. It samples trajectories instead, and the
panel reports the average over them. Two things to read off. First, only
$\ket{00}$ and $\ket{11}$ ever appear, at close to half each: the mismatched
strings $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{10}$ have probability zero in both circuits.
Second, the reported numbers are not exactly $0.5$, because 4096 samples of a
fair coin do not land exactly even. The deferred circuit reports exactly $0.5$
because it never sampled anything.

## The same proof for a general controlled operation

Nothing about the argument used the fact that the gate was $X$. Suppose the
classical version measures qubit 0 and applies some unitary $U$ to a register
$R$ when the outcome is 1, and let the pre-measurement state be
$(c_0\ket{0} + c_1\ket{1})\ket{\chi}$ with $\ket{\chi}$ the state of $R$.

Measuring first gives outcome $a$ with probability $|c_a|^2$ and leaves $R$ in
$U^a\ket{\chi}$. Deferring instead applies the coherent controlled-$U$, giving

$$
c_0 \ket{0}\ket{\chi} + c_1 \ket{1} U\ket{\chi},
$$

and then measuring qubit 0 returns $a$ with probability $|c_a|^2$ and leaves $R$
in $U^a\ket{\chi}$. The two lines are the same statement, which is the proof.
What changed is only when the branch was collapsed, and a branch nobody has looked
at yet is indistinguishable from one that was chosen and hidden.

## What deferring costs

The principle says the distributions match. It does not say the two circuits are
equally good to run.

Deferring keeps the measured qubit alive for the rest of the circuit. That is one
more qubit of width you cannot reuse, and one more qubit accumulating decoherence
for the whole remaining depth. On a device with 5 usable qubits and a circuit
that would otherwise need 7, deferring is not an option.

Not deferring costs latency instead. A mid-circuit measurement has to complete,
the result has to travel to the classical controller, the controller has to
decide, and the decision has to reach the pulse generator, all while the
untouched qubits sit idle and decohere. That round trip is the subject of the
next two lessons.

<Callout tone="note" title="Which direction you use it in">
Analysts defer, because a circuit with no measurement in the middle has a
statevector you can write down. Engineers un-defer, because a circuit that
measures early frees a qubit. The principle guarantees the two views agree, so
you are free to switch whenever it is convenient.
</Callout>

## Where the theorem earns its keep

Three places in this track already depend on it.

Teleportation, where the coherent circuit gave you an exact eight-amplitude
statevector to read the correction table off, and the real protocol with two
measurements and two conditional gates has the same statistics.

Reset, which is defined as a measurement followed by a conditional $X$. Deferred
measurement is what lets you reason about a reset as though it were a coherent
operation into a fresh ancilla.

Error correction, where syndrome extraction measures ancillas mid-circuit and
applies corrections. Textbook analyses defer all of it, prove the code works, and
only then worry about whether the hardware can turn a syndrome round in time.

<Check question="A circuit measures qubit 0, and if the result is 1 applies $H$ to qubit 1. You defer the measurement. What does the circuit contain now, and what is its width?">
A controlled-$H$ with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target, followed by
measurements of both qubits at the end. The width is unchanged at 2, because both
qubits already existed; what changes is that qubit 0 must stay coherent until the
final measurement instead of being freed early. The saving that deferral gives up
is qubit *reuse*, not qubit count, and reuse is exactly what the next lesson
buys back with reset.
</Check>
