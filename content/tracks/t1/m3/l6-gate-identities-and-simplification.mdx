Depth is the resource that noise eats. A circuit of eight single-qubit gates
takes eight times as long to run as one gate, accumulates eight gate errors, and
computes something a single gate could have computed. Recognising that is the
first optimisation pass any compiler makes, and doing it by hand once is how you
learn to trust the ones that do it automatically.

## The toolbox

Six identities cover almost every single-qubit simplification you will meet.

| identity | why |
| --- | --- |
| $X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = H^2 = I$ | each is a half turn, or its own inverse |
| $S^2 = Z$, $T^2 = S$, $T^4 = Z$ | the phase family adds angles |
| $HZH = X$, $HXH = Z$, $HYH = -Y$ | $H$ swaps the $x$ and $z$ axes |
| $ZX = iY$, $XY = iZ$, $YZ = iX$ | the Pauli cyclic rule |
| $P(\lambda)P(\mu) = P(\lambda + \mu)$ | diagonal gates merge |
| $R_a(\theta)R_a(\phi) = R_a(\theta + \phi)$ | same-axis rotations merge |

Two rules of thumb make them usable. Adjacent diagonal gates always merge.
Anything sandwiched between two Hadamards has its axis swapped, so a $z$ gate
becomes an $x$ gate and vice versa.

<Margin label="Reading order">
A circuit drawn left to right as $g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n$ has matrix
$g_n \cdots g_2 g_1$: the first gate applied is the rightmost factor. Every
identity below is stated as a matrix product, so reverse the reading direction
when you turn one into a circuit.
</Margin>

## The sequence to reduce

Apply these eight gates in order to one qubit:

$$
H,\quad S,\quad S,\quad H,\quad T,\quad T,\quad T,\quad T .
$$

As a matrix that is $T^4 H S^2 H$. Work it inside out.

**Step one.** $S^2 = Z$, since $S = P(\pi/2)$ and the phases add to $\pi$. The
middle of the expression becomes $H Z H$.

**Step two.** $H Z H = X$, the axis swap. The right-hand block is now $X$.

**Step three.** $T^4 = P(\pi/4)^4 = P(\pi) = Z$. The whole product is $Z X$.

**Step four.** $Z X = iY$ by the cyclic rule, or by multiplying directly:

$$
ZX = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = i Y .
$$

Eight gates collapse to one $Y$, up to the global phase $i$.

The eight-gate circuit reports amplitude $-1$ on $\ket{1}$ and the single $Y$
reports $+i$. The ratio is $i$, constant, so by the test of T1.M1.L7 these are
the same state. Feed both $\ket{+}$ instead and the same thing happens: the
sequence gives $(0.707107,\ -0.707107)$ and $Y$ gives
$(-0.707107 i,\ 0.707107 i)$, again in the ratio $i$.

## Your task

Return a Cirq circuit containing at most one gate that equals the eight-gate
sequence up to global phase.

```python
import cirq


def simplified() -> cirq.Circuit:
    q = cirq.LineQubit(0)
    # The sequence to reduce, for reference:
    #   cirq.Circuit([cirq.H(q), cirq.S(q), cirq.S(q), cirq.H(q),
    #                 cirq.T(q), cirq.T(q), cirq.T(q), cirq.T(q)])
    return cirq.Circuit()  # your code here
```

The graded tests check three things: that the unitary matches $Y$ to a tolerance
of $10^{-12}$ up to global phase, that the circuit contains at most one gate and
has depth at most 1, and that it maps $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{1}$ to
$\ket{0}$ up to phase.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Up to global phase is a real tolerance, not a get-out">
The test compares $U$ and $V$ by checking that $V^{\dagger}U$ is a multiple of
the identity, not that the entries agree. Returning $Y$ passes. Returning
$-Y$, $iY$ or $R_y(\pi)$ also passes, since all four differ from one another
only by a phase. Returning $X$ does not, however close it looks: $X$ and $Y$
disagree on $\ket{+}$, which the hidden statevector tests exercise.
</Callout>

## Working method

Three habits make hand simplification reliable.

**Push diagonals together.** Any $Z$, $S$, $T$ or $P(\lambda)$ adjacent to
another merges by adding phases. Do these first; they never fail and they
shorten the expression.

**Conjugate by Hadamards deliberately.** When you see $H \dots H$, rewrite the
middle using $HZH = X$ and $HXH = Z$. This converts a diagonal block into a
Pauli-$X$ block that can then merge with other $X$s.

**Track the phase, then discard it at the very end.** Carrying $i$ and $-1$
through the calculation costs nothing and catches sign errors. Discard the
accumulated factor only once, after the last step, and only if the circuit is
not going to be controlled.

<Check question="Simplify the circuit $H$, $T$, $T$, $T$, $T$, $H$, $Z$ applied in that order.">
The matrix is $Z H T^4 H$. Inside, $T^4 = Z$, so $H T^4 H = H Z H = X$, and the
product is $Z X = iY$. The circuit is a single $Y$ up to global phase, the same
answer as the eight-gate sequence by a different route. Seven gates become one.
</Check>

<Solution>
Return `cirq.Circuit([cirq.Y(q)])`.

The reduction in one line: $H S^2 H = H Z H = X$ and $T^4 = Z$, so the sequence
is $ZX = iY$, which is $Y$ up to global phase. Any of `cirq.Y(q)`,
`cirq.ry(np.pi)(q)` or `cirq.Y(q) ** 1.0` satisfies the unitary test; the gate
count and depth limits rule out returning the original sequence unchanged.
</Solution>
