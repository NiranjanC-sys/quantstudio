A compiler is handed a $2 \times 2$ unitary and a device that offers rotations
about $z$ for free and rotations about $y$ at a cost. How many of each does it
need? The answer is three, always, and the constructive proof is the same
decomposition that Euler wrote down for rigid-body orientations in 1775.

## The theorem

Every one-qubit unitary $U$ can be written

$$
U = e^{i\alpha}\, R_z(\beta)\, R_y(\gamma)\, R_z(\delta)
$$

with four real numbers $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$. As a circuit the gates
run right to left: $R_z(\delta)$ first, then $R_y(\gamma)$, then $R_z(\beta)$,
and the phase is not a gate at all.

The parameter count explains why three angles is the right number and not two or
four. A $2 \times 2$ unitary has 4 real degrees of freedom: 8 real entries minus
the 4 real constraints in $U^{\dagger}U = I$. Discarding global phase removes
one, leaving 3. Geometrically, the group of rotations of the Bloch sphere is
three dimensional, and Euler angles are the standard coordinates on it.

<Margin label="Other conventions">
ZYZ is one choice. ZXZ and the ZSX form used by IBM hardware are equally valid,
and the registry's $U(\theta,\varphi,\lambda)$ is ZYZ with the phase absorbed:
$U(\theta,\varphi,\lambda) = e^{i(\varphi + \lambda)/2} R_z(\varphi) R_y(\theta) R_z(\lambda)$.
Always check which convention a tool means before comparing angles.
</Margin>

## Why the product has this shape

Multiply the three factors out once and the extraction recipe falls out. With
$c = \cos(\gamma/2)$ and $s = \sin(\gamma/2)$,

$$
R_z(\beta) R_y(\gamma) R_z(\delta) =
\begin{pmatrix}
e^{-i(\beta + \delta)/2} c & -e^{-i(\beta - \delta)/2} s \\[4pt]
e^{i(\beta - \delta)/2} s & e^{i(\beta + \delta)/2} c
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

The $R_z$ factors are diagonal, so the left one scales rows and the right one
scales columns; only $R_y$ moves amplitude between rows, and it does so with
real entries. So the moduli of the four entries are decided by $\gamma$ alone,
and the four phases are decided by $\beta$ and $\delta$ together with $\alpha$.

## Extracting the angles

Given $U$, the recipe is four steps. Normalise the determinant first, because
that is what separates the global phase from the rest.

**One.** Compute $\varphi = \arg \det U$ and set $\alpha = \varphi/2$. The
matrix $U' = e^{-i\varphi/2} U$ then has $\det U' = 1$, so it lies in $SU(2)$
and matches the product above exactly.

**Two.** Read $\gamma$ off the moduli:

$$
\gamma = 2 \arctan\!\left( \frac{|U'_{10}|}{|U'_{00}|} \right) .
$$

**Three.** Read the phase sum and difference off the bottom row, where the
entries are $e^{i(\beta+\delta)/2} c$ and $e^{i(\beta-\delta)/2} s$:

$$
\beta = \arg U'_{11} + \arg U'_{10},
\qquad
\delta = \arg U'_{11} - \arg U'_{10} .
$$

**Four.** If $s = 0$ the bottom-left entry has no phase to read, and $\beta$ and
$\delta$ are only determined through their sum; take $\delta = 0$ and
$\beta = 2\arg U'_{11}$.

Run it on the Hadamard. $\det H = -1$, so $\varphi = \pi$ and
$\alpha = \pi/2$, and $U' = -iH$ has entries

$$
U' = \begin{pmatrix} -0.707107 i & -0.707107 i \\ -0.707107 i & 0.707107 i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Then $\gamma = 2\arctan(1) = \pi/2$, and with $\arg U'_{11} = \pi/2$ and
$\arg U'_{10} = -\pi/2$,

$$
\beta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{2} = 0,
\qquad
\delta = \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi .
$$

So $H = e^{i\pi/2} R_z(0)\, R_y(\pi/2)\, R_z(\pi)$, which is two gates plus a
phase, since $R_z(0) = I$.

Compare with $H\ket{0} = (0.707107,\ 0.707107)$. The two amplitude columns
differ by the factor $-i$, and the probability columns are identical, which is
the global phase $e^{i\alpha}$ doing exactly what T1.M1.L7 said it would. A
circuit cannot produce the factor and does not need to.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The phase is not always ignorable">
Dropping $e^{i\alpha}$ is safe for a gate applied to the whole register at the
end of a circuit. It is not safe inside a controlled operation: a controlled
version of $e^{i\alpha}U$ differs from a controlled $U$ by a phase gate on the
control, which is measurable. T1.M4.L5 builds controlled gates and has to carry
$\alpha$ explicitly for exactly this reason.
</Callout>

## Three worked results

Running the recipe on the standard gates gives a table worth checking by hand
once each. Angles are in radians.

| $U$ | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | $\gamma$ | $\delta$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $H$ | $\pi/2$ | $0$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi$ |
| $S$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/2$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $T$ | $\pi/8$ | $\pi/4$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $X$ | $\pi/2$ | $-\pi/2$ | $\pi$ | $\pi/2$ |
| $Y$ | $\pi/2$ | $0$ | $\pi$ | $0$ |
| $\sqrt{X}$ | $\pi/4$ | $-\pi/2$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi/2$ |

The diagonal gates all have $\gamma = 0$, which is the statement that they need
no $R_y$ at all: they are already rotations about $z$. The $\sqrt{X}$ row is the
one hardware compilers use constantly, since it turns an $x$ rotation into a
free-free-cheap sandwich on devices where $R_z$ costs nothing.

## Why this makes a device universal

Euler decomposition is the reason a machine offering only $R_z$ and one fixed
non-diagonal gate can run anything. IBM devices expose $R_z$, $\sqrt{X}$ and a
two-qubit entangler; since
$R_y(\gamma) = R_z(-\pi/2) R_x(\gamma) R_z(\pi/2)$ and $R_x$ is built from two
$\sqrt{X}$ gates with an $R_z$ between them, every one-qubit unitary compiles to
at most two $\sqrt{X}$ gates and three $R_z$ gates. Combined with the two-qubit
gate of T1.M4, that is a universal set, and T1.M8.L5 completes the argument.

<Check question="A gate has $\gamma = 0$ in its Euler decomposition. What can you say about it, and what does it do to the Bloch sphere?">
$\gamma = 0$ makes $R_y(\gamma) = I$, so
$U = e^{i\alpha} R_z(\beta) R_z(\delta) = e^{i\alpha} R_z(\beta + \delta)$. The
gate is diagonal in the computational basis, it is a member of the phase family
$P(\lambda)$ of T1.M3.L2 up to global phase, and on the Bloch sphere it is a
rotation about the $z$ axis by $\beta + \delta$. Only the sum is determined,
which is why the extraction recipe has to special-case it.
</Check>
