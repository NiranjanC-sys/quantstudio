The Paulis are half turns, and half turns alone cannot reach most of the sphere.
What is missing is a way to turn by less than half, and on the $z$ axis that is
supplied by a family of diagonal gates that differ only in how much phase they
put on $\ket{1}$. Three members of that family, together with the Hadamard, turn
out to be enough to approximate every one-qubit gate there is.

## One family, three members

$$
P(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\lambda} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Z = P(\pi), \quad S = P(\pi/2), \quad T = P(\pi/4) .
$$

Concretely,

$$
S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac{1+i}{\sqrt2} \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.707107 + 0.707107 i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Every one of them leaves $\ket{0}$ untouched and multiplies the amplitude of
$\ket{1}$ by a unit-modulus number. That means none of them changes any
computational-basis probability: $|e^{i\lambda}\beta|^2 = |\beta|^2$. They are
pure relative-phase gates, invisible in the $Z$ basis and decisive in every
other.

<Margin label="Naming">
$S$ is the "phase gate" and $T$ is the "$\pi/8$ gate", a name inherited from the
symmetric form $e^{-i\pi/8}\,\mathrm{diag}(e^{i\pi/8}, e^{-i\pi/8})$ where the
exponent really is $\pi/8$. The relative phase it applies is $\pi/4$. Both names
are in wide use and mean the same gate.
</Margin>

Because the phases add when the gates compose, $P(\lambda)P(\mu) = P(\lambda + \mu)$,
the family is a circle group and the powers are immediate:

$$
S^2 = P(\pi/2)^2 = P(\pi) = Z,
\qquad
T^2 = P(\pi/4)^2 = P(\pi/2) = S,
$$

$$
T^4 = P(\pi) = Z, \qquad T^8 = P(2\pi) = I .
$$

So $S$ is a square root of $Z$ and $T$ is a fourth root. On the Bloch sphere,
$Z$ is a half turn about $z$, $S$ a quarter turn, and $T$ an eighth of a turn.

Four $T$ gates in a row are therefore a $Z$, and if you sandwich them between
Hadamards, $H Z H = X$ turns the whole sequence into a bit flip.

## The Hadamard is the odd one out

$$
H = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is not diagonal, and that is exactly its function. The phase family can only
rotate about $z$, and rotating about one axis forever never leaves a single
circle of latitude. $H$ exchanges the $x$ and $z$ axes, so it converts a
$z$ rotation into an $x$ rotation:

$$
H S H = \text{a quarter turn about } x,
\qquad
H P(\lambda) H = \text{a } \lambda \text{ turn about } x .
$$

With rotations about two different axes available you can reach anywhere, in the
same way that pitch and yaw generate every orientation of an aircraft.

<Callout tone="note" title="Clifford, and the one gate that is not">
$H$ and $S$ map Paulis to Paulis under conjugation: $HZH = X$, $SXS^{\dagger} = Y$.
Gates with that property are Cliffords, and the Gottesman-Knill theorem says a
circuit built only from Cliffords can be simulated classically in polynomial
time. $T$ is not Clifford: $TXT^{\dagger} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(X + Y)$, which is
not a Pauli. Adding $T$ is what makes the gate set universal, and it is also
what makes the circuit expensive under error correction in T4.
</Callout>

## Why H, S and T are enough

The claim is that finite products of $H$, $S$ and $T$ come arbitrarily close to
every one-qubit unitary, in the sense that for any target $U$ and any
$\varepsilon > 0$ there is a product $V$ with
$\lVert U - V \rVert < \varepsilon$ up to global phase. The argument has three
steps and none of them needs machinery beyond this module.

**Step one: build an irrational rotation.** The product $THTH$ is a rotation of
the Bloch sphere by an angle $\theta$ satisfying
$\cos(\theta/2) = \cos^2(\pi/8)$, and that $\theta$ is an irrational multiple of
$\pi$. Repeating an irrational rotation about a fixed axis fills that circle
densely: the angles $k\theta \bmod 2\pi$ never repeat and come arbitrarily close
to every value.

**Step two: get a second axis.** Conjugating the same product by $H$ gives the
same rotation angle about a different axis, since $H$ moves the axis and
preserves the angle.

**Step three: compose.** Rotations by dense sets of angles about two distinct
axes generate a dense subgroup of all rotations of the sphere, by the Euler
decomposition of T1.M3.L5: any orientation is three successive rotations about
two alternating axes, and each of the three can be approximated as closely as
you like.

Density is not efficiency. Reaching precision $\varepsilon$ naively could need
exponentially many gates; the Solovay-Kitaev theorem, stated in T1.M8.L6,
guarantees $O(\log^{c}(1/\varepsilon))$ gates instead, with $c$ a small
constant. That is the result that makes a discrete gate set practical.

## Reading a phase off the amplitude table

Phases are invisible in the probability column and obvious in the amplitude
column. Apply $T$ to $\ket{+}$: the amplitude on $\ket{0}$ stays at $0.707107$
and the amplitude on $\ket{1}$ becomes $0.707107 \times e^{i\pi/4} = 0.5 + 0.5i$.
The probabilities are still $0.5$ and $0.5$.

<Check question="What single standard gate equals $S T^2$, and what does $H S^{\dagger} H$ do to the Bloch sphere?">
$T^2 = S$, so $S T^2 = S \cdot S = S^2 = Z$. For the second, $S^{\dagger}$ is a
quarter turn about $z$ in the negative direction, and conjugating by $H$
exchanges the $x$ and $z$ axes, so $H S^{\dagger} H$ is a quarter turn about the
$x$ axis in the negative direction. In matrix form it is
$\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 - i & 1 + i \\ 1 + i & 1 - i \end{pmatrix}$, which is
the gate the registry calls $\sqrt{X}^{\dagger}$.
</Check>
