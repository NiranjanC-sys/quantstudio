Named gates are discrete and hardware is continuous. A pulse of half the
duration produces half the rotation, and a variational algorithm needs to tune
that angle by gradient descent. The gates that expose the angle are the rotation
gates, and they come from one exponential.

## The derivation

Physical evolution for a time $t$ under a Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}$ is
$e^{-i\mathcal{H}t}$. Take $\mathcal{H} = X$ and write $\theta$ for $2t$:

$$
R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta X/2} .
$$

Expand the exponential as its power series and use $X^2 = I$, so that
$X^{2k} = I$ and $X^{2k+1} = X$:

$$
e^{-i\theta X/2}
= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k!}\left( \frac{-i\theta}{2} \right)^{k} X^{k}
= \underbrace{\sum_{k \text{ even}} \frac{(-i\theta/2)^k}{k!}}_{\text{coefficient of } I} I
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{k \text{ odd}} \frac{(-i\theta/2)^k}{k!}}_{\text{coefficient of } X} X .
$$

The even terms are $\sum_m \frac{(-1)^m (\theta/2)^{2m}}{(2m)!} = \cos(\theta/2)$,
since the two factors of $-i$ in each pair give $-1$. The odd terms carry one
leftover $-i$ and give $-i\sin(\theta/2)$. Hence

$$
R_x(\theta) = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\, I - i \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\, X
= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} & -i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} \\[2pt]
-i\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} & \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

<Margin label="Where the half comes from">
Nothing in the series produced a half by itself; it was put into the definition
so that $R_a(\theta)$ rotates the *Bloch sphere* by $\theta$. States pick up
$\theta/2$ and arrows pick up $\theta$, because the Bloch map already halves the
polar angle. Every appearance of $\theta/2$ in this module traces to that one
choice.
</Margin>

The derivation used only $X^2 = I$, so it repeats verbatim for $Y$ and $Z$:

$$
R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} & -\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} \\[2pt]
\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} & \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$R_y$ is real, which is why it was the workhorse of T1.M1: it is exactly the
rotation of the real unit circle by $\theta/2$.

## A full turn is minus one

Set $\theta = 2\pi$:

$$
R_x(2\pi) = \cos(\pi) I - i \sin(\pi) X = -I .
$$

The Bloch arrow has come all the way round and is exactly where it started. The
state vector is not: it is $-\ket{\psi}$. A $4\pi$ rotation is needed to return
the vector itself, since $R_x(4\pi) = I$.

On a lone qubit the sign is a global phase and unobservable, exactly as T1.M1.L7
proved. It stops being unobservable the moment the rotation is controlled, or
applied to one half of an entangled pair, because then it is a relative phase
between branches. This is the spinor property of spin-one-half systems, and it
is measurable: neutron interferometry experiments detect the sign change from a
$2\pi$ rotation directly.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Rz on |0> looks like nothing">
$R_z(\theta)\ket{0} = e^{-i\theta/2}\ket{0}$, so a simulator will report
probability 1 on $\ket{0}$ for every $\theta$ and the Bloch arrow will not move.
The gate is not broken and it is not a no-op; the north pole is on its axis.
Sandwich it between Hadamards, or apply it to $\ket{+}$, and the effect appears
at once.
</Callout>

## The named gates, recovered

Every gate of T1.M3.L1 and T1.M3.L2 is a rotation times a phase. Substituting
$\theta = \pi$ into the formula gives $R_a(\pi) = -i A$, so

$$
X = i R_x(\pi) = e^{i\pi/2} R_x(\pi),
\qquad
Y = e^{i\pi/2} R_y(\pi),
\qquad
Z = e^{i\pi/2} R_z(\pi) .
$$

For the phase family, compare $R_z(\lambda) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\lambda/2}, e^{i\lambda/2})$
with $P(\lambda) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\lambda})$: they differ by the overall
factor $e^{-i\lambda/2}$, so

$$
P(\lambda) = e^{i\lambda/2} R_z(\lambda),
\qquad
S = P(\pi/2) = e^{i\pi/4} R_z(\pi/2),
\qquad
T = e^{i\pi/8} R_z(\pi/4) .
$$

Hardware exploits this. On superconducting devices $R_z$ is implemented by
shifting the phase of the microwave drive rather than by a pulse, so it takes no
time and adds no error; a compiler will happily convert every $S$ and $T$ into
$R_z$ and push them through the circuit.

## The general one-qubit gate

Three angles fix any one-qubit unitary up to global phase, and the registry's
canonical form is

$$
U(\theta, \varphi, \lambda)
= \begin{pmatrix}
\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} & -e^{i\lambda}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} \\[2pt]
e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} & e^{i(\varphi + \lambda)}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

which acts on $\ket{0}$ to give exactly the canonical state
$\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\ket{0} + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\ket{1}$ of
T1.M1.L7. It factors into rotations as

$$
U(\theta, \varphi, \lambda) = e^{i(\varphi + \lambda)/2}\,
R_z(\varphi)\, R_y(\theta)\, R_z(\lambda) ,
$$

which you can confirm by multiplying the three diagonal and real matrices
directly: the two $R_z$ factors contribute $e^{\pm i\varphi/2}$ and
$e^{\pm i\lambda/2}$ to the rows and columns, and pulling out
$e^{-i(\varphi+\lambda)/2}$ leaves the matrix above. That factorisation is the
Euler decomposition, and T1.M3.L5 runs it in the other direction: given a matrix,
extract the angles.

Setting $\theta = \pi/2$, $\varphi = 0$, $\lambda = \pi$ gives
$\begin{pmatrix} 0.707107 & 0.707107 \\ 0.707107 & -0.707107\end{pmatrix} = H$,
so the Hadamard is a member of this family too.

<Check question="Express $\sqrt{X}$, the gate with $\sqrt{X}^2 = X$, as a rotation times a phase, and give its matrix.">
A half turn about $x$ is $R_x(\pi)$, so a quarter turn is $R_x(\pi/2)$ and
$R_x(\pi/2)^2 = R_x(\pi) = -iX$. To fix the phase, take
$\sqrt{X} = e^{i\pi/4} R_x(\pi/2)$, whose square is $e^{i\pi/2}(-iX) = X$.
Numerically $R_x(\pi/2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1\end{pmatrix}$,
and multiplying by $e^{i\pi/4} = \tfrac{1+i}{\sqrt2}$ gives
$\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1+i & 1-i \\ 1-i & 1+i \end{pmatrix}$, which is the
registry's $\sqrt{X}$ and a native gate on IBM hardware.
</Check>
