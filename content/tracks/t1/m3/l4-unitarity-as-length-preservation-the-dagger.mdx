T1.M1.L4 showed that a real gate must be orthogonal, because it has to keep
states on the unit circle. The complex version of that argument gives unitarity,
and it is worth doing carefully once, because the same step is what makes every
quantum circuit reversible and every algorithm in T1.M10 possible.

## From lengths to inner products

Let $U$ be a linear map on $\C^2$ with $\lVert U\ket{\psi} \rVert = \lVert \ket{\psi} \rVert$
for every $\ket{\psi}$. Squaring,

$$
\bra{\psi} U^{\dagger} U \ket{\psi} = \braket{\psi}{\psi}
\qquad \text{for all } \ket{\psi} .
$$

Write $M = U^{\dagger}U - I$, so the hypothesis is $\bra{\psi} M \ket{\psi} = 0$
for every $\ket{\psi}$, with $M$ Hermitian. Feed it $\ket{\psi} = \ket{a} + \ket{b}$
and expand:

$$
0 = \bra{a}M\ket{a} + \bra{b}M\ket{b} + \bra{a}M\ket{b} + \bra{b}M\ket{a}
= \bra{a}M\ket{b} + \bra{b}M\ket{a} .
$$

Now feed it $\ket{a} + i\ket{b}$, which gives
$-i\bra{a}M\ket{b} + i\bra{b}M\ket{a} = 0$, that is
$\bra{a}M\ket{b} = \bra{b}M\ket{a}$. Combining the two lines forces
$\bra{a}M\ket{b} = 0$ for all $\ket{a}$ and $\ket{b}$, hence $M = 0$ and

$$
U^{\dagger} U = I .
$$

<Margin label="Why the complex step is needed">
Over $\R$ the first equation alone would leave the antisymmetric part of $M$
undetermined. The trick of substituting $\ket{a} + i\ket{b}$ has no real
analogue, which is why the real proof of T1.M1.L4 needed the inner-product
argument instead. Both routes reach the same conclusion.
</Margin>

The converse is immediate: if $U^{\dagger}U = I$ then
$\lVert U\ket{\psi}\rVert^2 = \bra{\psi}U^{\dagger}U\ket{\psi} = \braket{\psi}{\psi}$.
So **unitary** and **length preserving** are the same condition, and the Born
rule is what makes it compulsory.

Two equivalent readings of $U^{\dagger}U = I$ are worth carrying. The columns of
$U$ are orthonormal, since entry $(j,k)$ of $U^{\dagger}U$ is the inner product
of columns $j$ and $k$. And $|\det U| = 1$, since
$\det(U^{\dagger})\det(U) = |\det U|^2 = 1$.

## The dagger

$U^{\dagger}$ is the conjugate transpose: transpose, then conjugate every entry.
For the gates of this module,

| gate | dagger | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X, Y, Z, H$ | itself | Hermitian and unitary, so self-inverse |
| $S$ | $S^{\dagger} = \mathrm{diag}(1, -i)$ | the registry calls it `sdg` |
| $T$ | $T^{\dagger} = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{-i\pi/4})$ | `tdg` |
| $P(\lambda)$ | $P(-\lambda)$ | negate the phase |
| $R_a(\theta)$ | $R_a(-\theta)$ | rotate the other way |
| $U(\theta,\varphi,\lambda)$ | $U(-\theta, -\lambda, -\varphi)$ | note the swap |

Since $U^{\dagger}U = I$, the dagger *is* the inverse: $U^{-1} = U^{\dagger}$.
Every quantum gate is invertible, and its inverse is another quantum gate, which
is not true of classical logic: AND has no inverse, and T0.M6.L3 is about the
cost of making it reversible.

## Reversing a circuit

Gates compose by matrix multiplication with the *first* gate on the right, so a
circuit applying $A$, then $B$, then $C$ has matrix $CBA$. Daggering reverses
products:

$$
(CBA)^{\dagger} = A^{\dagger} B^{\dagger} C^{\dagger} .
$$

Read as a circuit, that says: to undo a circuit, apply the daggered gates in the
reverse order. Both reversals are needed, and dropping either one is a common
bug.

Take $V$ to be $R_y(0.9)$, then $T$, then $H$. Its inverse is $H$, then
$T^{\dagger}$, then $R_y(-0.9)$. Running $V$ alone gives amplitudes
$0.854195 + 0.217483i$ and $0.419229 - 0.217483i$; running $V$ followed by its
inverse must give $\ket{0}$ back exactly.

The middle two Hadamards annihilate, then $T^{\dagger}T = I$, then the two
rotations cancel. Every step is an inverse pair meeting in the middle, which is
what makes the identity easy to see by eye in a drawn circuit.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Order matters twice">
Applying $A^{\dagger}, B^{\dagger}, C^{\dagger}$ in that order gives
$C^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}$, which is $(ABC)^{\dagger}$, not
$(CBA)^{\dagger}$. Unless the gates commute, it does not undo the original
circuit. In code the fix is one line, `reversed(ops)` with each gate daggered,
and forgetting the `reversed` produces a circuit that is unitary, well formed,
and wrong.
</Callout>

## What unitarity rules out

Three operations you might want and cannot have, all for this reason.

You cannot delete information. A gate mapping both $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ to
$\ket{0}$ would send two orthogonal vectors to the same vector, destroying an
inner product of 0 and violating length preservation on their difference.

You cannot copy an unknown state. T1.M5.L4 proves this from linearity, and
unitarity is what forces the cloning map to be linear in the first place.

You cannot renormalise mid-circuit. Since every gate preserves length exactly,
there is never anything to renormalise until a measurement, which is not a gate.

<Check question="A student wants to undo the circuit $S$, then $H$, then $T$. They write $S^{\dagger}$, then $H$, then $T^{\dagger}$. Does it work, and what does their circuit actually compute?">
It does not. The original matrix is $THS$, so the inverse is
$S^{\dagger}H T^{\dagger}$, which as a circuit is $T^{\dagger}$ first, then $H$,
then $S^{\dagger}$. Their version has matrix
$T^{\dagger} H S^{\dagger}$, and composing it with the original gives
$T^{\dagger} H S^{\dagger} T H S$, which does not simplify to the identity
because $H$ and the diagonal gates do not commute. Their circuit is a perfectly
legal unitary; it simply is not the inverse of anything they intended.
</Check>
