Three matrices generate almost everything you will need to say about one qubit,
and their algebra is small enough to memorise in an afternoon. Learn it now and
the rest of the track gets easier: Pauli operators are the observables in
T1.M2, the errors in T1.M11, the stabilisers in T4, and the terms of every
Hamiltonian in T5.

## The matrices

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$X$ exchanges the two amplitudes, which is why it is called the bit flip. $Z$
leaves $\ket{0}$ alone and negates $\ket{1}$, the phase flip. $Y$ does both and
adds a factor of $i$, and the cleanest way to remember it is $Y = iXZ$, which
you can verify by multiplying: $iXZ = i\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = Y$.

All three are Hermitian, $A^{\dagger} = A$, and unitary, so they are both
observables and gates. That double role is unusual and specific to this family.

<Margin label="Convention">
$X$, $Y$ and $Z$ are the Pauli operators. With the identity they span all
$2 \times 2$ complex matrices, so any one-qubit operator can be written as
$c_0 I + c_x X + c_y Y + c_z Z$. This is the decomposition T1.M2.L4 used to turn
three measurements into a full state description.
</Margin>

## Action on the six poles

Each Pauli is a half turn of the Bloch sphere about its own axis, so it fixes
the two poles on that axis and swaps the other four in pairs. Written as states,
with $\simeq$ meaning "up to global phase":

| | $\ket{0}$ | $\ket{1}$ | $\ket{+}$ | $\ket{-}$ | $\ket{+i}$ | $\ket{-i}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $X$ | $\ket{1}$ | $\ket{0}$ | $\ket{+}$ | $-\ket{-}$ | $\simeq\ket{-i}$ | $\simeq\ket{+i}$ |
| $Y$ | $i\ket{1}$ | $-i\ket{0}$ | $\simeq\ket{-}$ | $\simeq\ket{+}$ | $\ket{+i}$ | $-\ket{-i}$ |
| $Z$ | $\ket{0}$ | $-\ket{1}$ | $\ket{-}$ | $\ket{+}$ | $\ket{-i}$ | $\ket{+i}$ |

Read a row as "this axis survives, the other two reverse". $X$ fixes $\pm x$ and
negates $y$ and $z$; $Z$ fixes $\pm z$ and negates $x$ and $y$. The entries
carrying an explicit sign are eigenvalue statements: $X\ket{-} = -\ket{-}$ says
$\ket{-}$ is the $-1$ eigenvector of $X$, and $Z\ket{1} = -\ket{1}$ says the
same for $\ket{1}$ and $Z$. Each Pauli has eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$, and its
eigenvectors are the two poles on its own axis.

The $Y$ gate is the one people get wrong, so run it.

The amplitude table reads $0.0000 + 1.0000i$. As a state, $i\ket{1} \simeq \ket{1}$,
so $Y$ and $X$ are indistinguishable when applied to $\ket{0}$ and then measured.
They are not indistinguishable in general, and never when either is controlled.

## Squares and anticommutation

Each Pauli squares to the identity:

$$
X^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^2
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I,
$$

and similarly for $Y$ and $Z$. Geometrically, two half turns about the same axis
make a full turn. Consequently each is its own inverse, and a circuit containing
$X$ twice in a row on the same qubit can delete both.

They pairwise **anticommute**: $AB = -BA$ for distinct Paulis. Check the hardest
pair explicitly.

$$
XY = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix} = iZ ,
$$

$$
YX = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix} = -iZ .
$$

So $XY = -YX$ and $XY + YX = 0$. The same computation in the other two pairings
gives the cyclic rule

$$
XY = iZ, \qquad YZ = iX, \qquad ZX = iY ,
$$

with each reversal costing a sign. The pattern is exactly that of the
three-dimensional cross product with an extra $i$, which is not a coincidence:
both encode rotations in three dimensions.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Anticommuting is not the same as commuting to a phase">
$XY = iZ$ is not $XY \simeq YX$ in any harmless sense. On a single qubit acting
on a single state the sign is a global phase and washes out. Inside a controlled
gate, or on one half of an entangled pair, the same sign is a relative phase and
is measurable. T1.M6.L4 builds an entire nonlocal game out of the fact that
$X \otimes Z$ and $Z \otimes X$ commute while $X$ and $Z$ do not.
</Callout>

## Conjugation, and how gates move Paulis

The most useful identity in the family is what $H$ does by conjugation:

$$
HXH = Z, \qquad HZH = X, \qquad HYH = -Y .
$$

The first two say the Hadamard exchanges the $x$ and $z$ axes, which is exactly
the half turn about the diagonal from T1.M1.L8. The third says $y$ is reversed,
as a half turn about an axis in the $xz$ plane must do.

$HZH = X$ has a circuit reading: a $Z$ sandwiched between Hadamards is a bit
flip, so applying it to $\ket{0}$ gives $\ket{1}$ with certainty.

A gate that maps Paulis to Paulis under conjugation is called a **Clifford**
gate. $H$, $S$ and CNOT are Cliffords; $T$ is not. T1.M3.L2 shows why that
distinction decides which gate sets are cheap to simulate classically.

<Check question="Compute $ZY$ and express it as a Pauli times a phase. Then say whether $Z$ and $Y$ have a common eigenvector.">
$ZY = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ -i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -iX$. The cyclic rule gives
$YZ = iX$, and reversing the order flips the sign, as anticommutation requires.
They share no eigenvector: if $\ket{v}$ were a common eigenvector with
eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\mu$, then $ZY\ket{v} = \lambda\mu\ket{v}$ and also
$-YZ\ket{v} = -\lambda\mu\ket{v}$, forcing $\lambda\mu = 0$, which is impossible
since Pauli eigenvalues are $\pm 1$. Anticommuting observables never have a
common eigenbasis, which is the algebraic root of the uncertainty relation
between them.
</Check>
