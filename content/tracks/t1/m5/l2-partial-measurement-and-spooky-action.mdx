Alice and Bob share a Bell pair and walk to opposite ends of the galaxy. Alice
measures her qubit and instantly knows Bob's. Einstein called this spooky action
at a distance, and popular accounts stop there. The interesting content is the
next step: prove carefully that Alice can learn about Bob and still send him
nothing.

## Measuring half of a Bell pair

Take $\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$ and measure qubit 0
only. The projector for outcome 0 keeps every basis state whose first bit is 0,
so from the rule of T1.M2.L2:

$$
P_0 \ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{00},
\qquad
\Pr[0] = \left\| \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{00} \right\|^2 = \tfrac12 ,
$$

and renormalising by $\sqrt{1/2}$ gives the post-measurement state $\ket{00}$.
The other branch gives $\ket{11}$ with probability $\tfrac12$. In both cases
qubit 1 has become a definite basis state, even though nobody touched it.

Two things are true at once and it is worth separating them. Alice's outcome is
random: she gets 0 or 1 with probability $\tfrac12$, and she cannot choose which.
Her outcome is perfectly correlated with Bob's: whatever she gets, he will get
the same. Correlation without control is the entire phenomenon.

<Margin label="Conditional versus marginal">
Before Alice looks, Bob's outcome distribution is $(0.5, 0.5)$. After Alice
looks and gets 0, Bob's *conditional* distribution is $(1, 0)$. But Bob cannot
compute a conditional distribution without Alice's outcome, and until it arrives
by ordinary classical means his own statistics are the marginal, unchanged.
</Margin>

## Nothing Alice does changes Bob's statistics

The stronger claim is that no operation whatsoever on qubit 0, unitary or
measurement, in any basis, changes the outcome distribution of qubit 1. Apply a
Hadamard to Alice's qubit:

$$
(H \otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac12 \left( \ket{00} + \ket{01} + \ket{10} - \ket{11} \right) .
$$

Qubit 1 reads 0 in $\ket{00}$ and $\ket{10}$, so its marginal is
$0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$, exactly as before the Hadamard. Try an arbitrary gate
instead, say $U(1.1, 0.3, 0.7)$ on qubit 0. The four joint probabilities become
$0.363399, 0.136601, 0.136601, 0.363399$, which are not what they were, and the
marginal of qubit 1 is $0.363399 + 0.136601 = 0.5$ again.

The general proof is three lines. Let $\ket{\psi}$ be any two-qubit state and let
$U$ act on qubit 0 alone. Bob's probability of outcome $b$ is the sum over
Alice's outcomes:

$$
\Pr[b] = \sum_{a \in \{0,1\}} \left| \left( \bra{a} \otimes \bra{b} \right) (U \otimes I) \ket{\psi} \right|^2
= \bra{\psi} (U^{\dagger} \otimes I) \left( \sum_a \ketbra{a}{a} \otimes \ketbra{b}{b} \right) (U \otimes I) \ket{\psi} .
$$

The inner sum is $I \otimes \ketbra{b}{b}$, because Alice's projectors are
complete. That operator commutes with $U \otimes I$, so the $U^{\dagger}$ and
$U$ meet and cancel:

$$
\Pr[b] = \bra{\psi} \left( U^{\dagger}U \otimes \ketbra{b}{b} \right)\ket{\psi}
= \bra{\psi} \left( I \otimes \ketbra{b}{b} \right)\ket{\psi} ,
$$

which is the probability with no $U$ at all. The same argument with a
measurement in place of $U$ works because a measurement Alice does not report is
a sum over its branches, and that sum is again a complete set of projectors.

## Why this is not signalling

Suppose Alice wants to send Bob one bit at a distance. She can measure or not
measure, measure in the $Z$ basis or the $X$ basis, apply any gate she likes.
The theorem above says every one of those choices leaves Bob's distribution
exactly $(0.5, 0.5)$. Bob, holding a billion Bell halves and every measurement
apparatus in existence, sees the same statistics regardless of what Alice did.
There is no channel.

What Alice and Bob do share is a *correlation*, and correlations become visible
only when the two records are brought together, which requires ordinary
communication at or below the speed of light. This is the same reason a pair of
gloves separated into two boxes carries no signal: opening one box tells you
about the other and transmits nothing.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Then what is spooky about it?">
Not the correlation. Classical shared randomness produces correlations too. The
spooky part is that the correlation persists in *every* measurement basis in a
way no assignment of pre-existing values can reproduce. Measure both halves of
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ in the $Z$ basis and the bits agree; measure both in the $X$ basis
and they agree again; measure both in the $Y$ basis and they disagree every
time. T1.M6.L1 shows that no list of predetermined answers can match all of
those at once.
</Callout>

## Collapse is a statement about your description

It is tempting to imagine something travelling from Alice's qubit to Bob's at
the instant of measurement. Nothing in the formalism supplies such a thing, and
the no-signalling result says nothing observable travels. What changes at the
moment Alice looks is her description of the joint system, and descriptions are
not localised objects. Bob's description does not change until he learns her
outcome.

This is also why the ordering of spacelike-separated measurements does not
matter. If Bob measures first, the same calculation gives him $(0.5, 0.5)$ and
leaves Alice with a conditional state; the joint statistics are identical either
way, and there is no fact about who collapsed whom.

<Check question="Alice measures her half of $\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{10})$ in the X basis and gets the outcome +. What is Bob's state, and what would Bob see if he never learned her result?">
Write the singlet in the $X$ basis: it equals
$-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{+}\ket{-} - \ket{-}\ket{+})$, the singlet being
invariant in form under any common basis change up to a phase. So Alice getting
$+$ projects Bob onto $\ket{-}$, perfectly anticorrelated as in every other
basis. Without her result Bob's distribution in any basis he chooses is
$(0.5, 0.5)$: his marginal is unchanged by her choice of basis and by her
outcome, which is exactly the no-signalling statement.
</Check>
