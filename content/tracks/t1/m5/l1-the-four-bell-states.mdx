Four states of two qubits appear in nearly every protocol in this curriculum:
teleportation, superdense coding, entanglement swapping, key distribution, and
the nonlocal games of T1.M6. They are the Bell states, and they are not four
unrelated curiosities. They are a single orthonormal basis, produced by a single
two-gate circuit run on the four computational basis states.

## The four states

$$
\ket{\Phi^+} = \frac{\ket{00} + \ket{11}}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\ket{\Phi^-} = \frac{\ket{00} - \ket{11}}{\sqrt2},
$$

$$
\ket{\Psi^+} = \frac{\ket{01} + \ket{10}}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\ket{\Psi^-} = \frac{\ket{01} - \ket{10}}{\sqrt2} .
$$

The $\Phi$ pair correlates the two bits and the $\Psi$ pair anticorrelates them.
Within each pair the sign is the only difference. All four are maximally
entangled: applying the factorisation test of T1.M4.L3, each has
$|a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2| = \tfrac12$, so the concurrence is 1 and none of them is a
product.

<Margin label="Names">
$\ket{\Psi^-}$ is the **singlet**, the unique two-qubit state invariant under
$U \otimes U$ for every one-qubit $U$. The other three span the **triplet**. The
singlet's anticorrelation therefore holds in every basis, which is what makes it
the state of choice in Bell-inequality experiments.
</Margin>

## One circuit, four outputs

Apply $H$ to qubit 0 and then a CNOT from qubit 0 to qubit 1. Run that on each
of the four computational basis states and you get the four Bell states, in
order.

$$
\ket{00} \mapsto \ket{\Phi^+}, \quad
\ket{01} \mapsto \ket{\Psi^+}, \quad
\ket{10} \mapsto \ket{\Phi^-}, \quad
\ket{11} \mapsto \ket{\Psi^-} .
$$

Trace the third of these by hand. Starting from $\ket{10}$, the Hadamard on
qubit 0 gives $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} - \ket{1}) \otimes \ket{0}$, which
expands to $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{10})$. The CNOT leaves $\ket{00}$
alone and turns $\ket{10}$ into $\ket{11}$, giving
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^-}$.

Because $H$ and CNOT are unitary and the four inputs are orthonormal, the four
outputs are orthonormal: unitaries preserve inner products, so
$\braket{\Phi^+}{\Psi^-} = \braket{00}{11} = 0$ and so on for all six pairs.
That is the whole proof that the Bell states are a basis, and it also shows the
circuit is invertible: running $\mathrm{CX}$ then $H$ backwards maps each Bell
state to its computational label, which is exactly a **Bell measurement** and is
the step teleportation needs in T1.M7.L2.

Check orthogonality directly on one pair to see it concretely:

$$
\braket{\Phi^+}{\Phi^-}
= \tfrac12 \left( \braket{00}{00} - \braket{00}{11} + \braket{11}{00} - \braket{11}{11} \right)
= \tfrac12 (1 - 0 + 0 - 1) = 0 .
$$

## Moving between them with one gate

Because each Bell state differs from $\ket{\Phi^+}$ by which of $\ket{00}$ or
$\ket{01}$ it starts from and by a sign, a single Pauli on **one** qubit
converts any of them into any other.

| target | operation on qubit 1 | effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ket{\Phi^+}$ | $I$ | leave alone |
| $\ket{\Psi^+}$ | $X$ | flip the second bit of each term |
| $\ket{\Phi^-}$ | $Z$ | negate the $\ket{11}$ term |
| $\ket{\Psi^-}$ | $Z$ then $X$ | both, giving $\ket{01} - \ket{10}$ |

Verify the second row: $X$ on qubit 1 sends $\ket{00} \mapsto \ket{01}$ and
$\ket{11} \mapsto \ket{10}$, so $\ket{\Phi^+} \mapsto \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} + \ket{10}) = \ket{\Psi^+}$.
Verify the third: $Z$ on qubit 1 leaves $\ket{00}$ alone and negates $\ket{11}$,
giving $\ket{\Phi^-}$.

This one-qubit reachability is the mechanism of superdense coding in T1.M7.L1.
Alice holds one half of a shared $\ket{\Phi^+}$, applies one of the four
operations in the table, and sends her qubit; Bob's Bell measurement then reads
two classical bits out of one transmitted qubit. The order matters in the last
row: applying $Z$ and then $X$ gives $\ket{\Psi^-}$ exactly, while $X$ and then
$Z$ gives $-\ket{\Psi^-}$, the same state up to a global phase.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Local operations move you around the Bell basis, not out of it">
$X$ and $Z$ on one qubit permute the four Bell states, and every one of them
stays maximally entangled. No sequence of single-qubit gates can reduce the
entanglement of a Bell pair or create it from a product state. Entanglement is
changed only by gates acting on both qubits, which is the content of the phrase
"local operations cannot create entanglement" and the reason T1.M5.L2 is about
what local operations *can* do.
</Callout>

## Correlation in every basis

Measuring $\ket{\Phi^+}$ in the computational basis gives $00$ or $11$, each with
probability $0.5$: the bits always agree. Now apply $H$ to both qubits first.
Since $(H \otimes H)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Phi^+}$, the outcomes still agree every
time. The same holds for the singlet, with anticorrelation in place of
correlation.

A classical shared random bit reproduces the first observation and fails the
second: copying a coin flip onto two cards makes the cards agree in the "which
face" basis and agree only half the time in any rotated one. The gap between
those two behaviours is measurable, and T1.M6.L1 turns it into an inequality
that no classical model can satisfy.

<Check question="Which Bell state does the circuit H on qubit 0, CNOT from 0 to 1, then Z on qubit 0 produce from $\ket{00}$? Compare with applying Z to qubit 1 instead.">
The circuit produces $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and then $Z$ on qubit 0 negates every term
whose first bit is 1, which is only $\ket{11}$, giving
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^-}$. Applying $Z$ to qubit 1
instead negates every term whose second bit is 1, again only $\ket{11}$, giving
the same state. So $Z \otimes I$ and $I \otimes Z$ act identically on
$\ket{\Phi^+}$: on a maximally entangled state a local operation on one qubit
can always be mimicked by an operation on the other, which is the first hint of
the reduced-state story in T1.M5.L3.
</Check>
