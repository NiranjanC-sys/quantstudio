T1.M4.L3 gave a yes-or-no test for entanglement and T1.M5.L3 observed that the
halves of an entangled state are mixed. Neither answers "how much". The Schmidt
decomposition supplies the missing structure, and one number computed from it,
the entanglement entropy, is the standard answer to the question.

## The decomposition

Every pure state of two qubits can be written

$$
\ket{\chi} = \sum_{k} \lambda_k \, \ket{u_k} \otimes \ket{v_k},
\qquad \lambda_k \ge 0, \quad \sum_k \lambda_k^2 = 1 ,
$$

where $\{\ket{u_k}\}$ and $\{\ket{v_k}\}$ are orthonormal bases of the two
qubits, chosen to suit the state. The $\lambda_k$ are the **Schmidt
coefficients** and the number of non-zero ones is the **Schmidt rank**.

Two features make this different from an arbitrary expansion. There is a single
sum, not a double sum: index $k$ appears on both sides. And the bases are
orthonormal but not the computational ones; they are whatever the state
requires.

Getting the decomposition is a singular value decomposition. Arrange the four
amplitudes in a matrix, rows indexed by qubit 0 and columns by qubit 1:

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} a_{00} & a_{01} \\ a_{10} & a_{11} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad M = U \Sigma V^{\dagger} ,
$$

and the singular values on the diagonal of $\Sigma$ are the Schmidt
coefficients, with $\ket{u_k}$ the columns of $U$ and $\ket{v_k}$ the conjugated
rows of $V^{\dagger}$.

<Margin label="Why the rank decides">
Schmidt rank 1 means one term, $\ket{\chi} = \ket{u_1}\otimes\ket{v_1}$, which is
a product state. Rank 2 or more means no product decomposition exists. So
"rank 1" and "$\det M = 0$" and "concurrence 0" are three names for the same
condition, and T1.M4.L3's test is the rank test in disguise.
</Margin>

## Worked, three states

**A product state.** For $\ket{+}\otimes\ket{+}$, every amplitude is $0.5$, so
$M = 0.5\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, which has rank 1. Its
singular values are $1$ and $0$: Schmidt rank 1, one term, no entanglement.

**A Bell state.** For $\ket{\Phi^+}$,
$M = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, whose singular
values are both $0.707107$. Schmidt rank 2 with equal coefficients, which is the
definition of maximally entangled.

**A partly entangled state.** For $0.5\ket{00} + 0.866025\ket{11}$, prepared by
$R_y(2\pi/3)$ followed by a CNOT, $M = \mathrm{diag}(0.5, 0.866025)$ is already
diagonal, so the Schmidt coefficients are $0.866025$ and $0.5$: rank 2, but
lopsided.

## The coefficients live in the reduced state

Squaring the Schmidt coefficients gives the eigenvalues of both reduced states:

$$
\rho_A = \sum_k \lambda_k^2 \, \ketbra{u_k}{u_k},
\qquad
\rho_B = \sum_k \lambda_k^2 \, \ketbra{v_k}{v_k} .
$$

The two halves therefore have the *same* spectrum, however different they look.
For the state above, $\lambda_1^2 = 0.75$ and $\lambda_2^2 = 0.25$, so each
reduced state has eigenvalues $0.75$ and $0.25$, and the corresponding Bloch
arrow has length $0.75 - 0.25 = 0.5$, which is what the figure reports.

This also explains why the Schmidt coefficients do not change under local gates.
A unitary on qubit 0 rotates $\ket{u_k}$ and leaves $\lambda_k$ alone, since
$\rho_A \mapsto U\rho_A U^{\dagger}$ has the same eigenvalues. Apply a Hadamard
to qubit 1 of a Bell pair and the amplitudes become
$0.5, 0.5, 0.5, -0.5$, an entirely different-looking vector, whose singular
values are still $0.707107$ and $0.707107$.

## Entanglement entropy

Feed the squared coefficients to the Shannon entropy of T0.M1.L5:

$$
S = -\sum_k \lambda_k^2 \log_2 \lambda_k^2 .
$$

This is the **entanglement entropy**, and it is the von Neumann entropy of
either reduced state. It measures, in bits, how much the local description had
to forget.

| state | $\lambda_k^2$ | $S$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ket{+}\otimes\ket{+}$ | $1,\ 0$ | $0$ |
| $0.5\ket{00} + 0.866025\ket{11}$ | $0.75,\ 0.25$ | $0.8112781$ |
| $0.6\ket{00} + 0.8\ket{11}$ | $0.64,\ 0.36$ | $0.9426832$ |
| $\ket{\Phi^+}$ | $0.5,\ 0.5$ | $1$ |

Check the third row by hand:

$$
S = -\left( 0.64 \log_2 0.64 + 0.36 \log_2 0.36 \right)
= -\left( 0.64 \times (-0.6438562) + 0.36 \times (-1.4739312) \right) ,
$$

which is $0.4120680 + 0.5306152 = 0.9426832$. Note that the more nearly balanced
state, $0.6$ and $0.8$, is more entangled than the $0.5$ and $0.866025$ one,
even though both have Schmidt rank 2. Rank says whether; entropy says how much.

<Callout tone="note" title="Zero, one, and the units">
$S = 0$ exactly when the state is a product, and $S = 1$ exactly when both
Schmidt coefficients are $1/\sqrt2$. The unit is the **ebit**, one Bell pair's
worth of entanglement, and it is the currency of T1.M7: teleporting one qubit
costs one ebit plus two classical bits, and superdense coding spends one ebit to
buy one extra bit of channel capacity.
</Callout>

## Beyond two qubits

For a split of $n$ qubits into a part of size $m$ and the rest, the coefficient
matrix is $2^m \times 2^{n-m}$ and the Schmidt rank can be as large as
$2^{\min(m,\, n-m)}$, so the maximum entropy across a balanced cut grows like
$n/2$. States whose entropy across every cut stays bounded by a constant are
exactly the ones a matrix product state can represent efficiently, which is the
loophole T1.M4.L2 mentioned: low entanglement entropy is what makes a large
circuit classically simulable. T5 makes this precise, and the area law is why
tensor networks work at all for physical Hamiltonians.

<Check question="A two-qubit state has amplitudes $0.5, 0.5, 0.5, -0.5$. What are its Schmidt coefficients and its entanglement entropy?">
The coefficient matrix is
$M = 0.5\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, so
$M M^{\dagger} = 0.25\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix} = 0.5 I$ and both
singular values are $\sqrt{0.5} = 0.707107$. Schmidt rank 2 with equal
coefficients, so $S = 1$: the state is maximally entangled. It is in fact
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ with a Hadamard applied to qubit 1, and the local gate could not
have changed the entropy.
</Check>
