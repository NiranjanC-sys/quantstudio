Copying is so cheap classically that no one counts it as an operation. Every
assignment statement is a copy, every network packet is a copy, and error
correction by majority vote is nothing but copying. None of that is available
here, and the proof that it is not takes three lines.

## The statement

A cloning machine would be a unitary $U$ acting on two qubits, a data qubit and
a blank, such that

$$
U \left( \ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{0} \right) = \ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\psi}
\qquad \text{for every one-qubit state } \ket{\psi} .
$$

The quantifier is the whole content. A machine that copies one known state is
trivial, and a machine that copies every state, including states nobody has told
it about, does not exist.

## The proof, from linearity

Suppose $U$ works on $\ket{0}$ and on $\ket{1}$:

$$
U\ket{00} = \ket{00}, \qquad U\ket{10} = \ket{11} .
$$

Now feed it $\ket{+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + \ket{1})$. Because $U$ is
linear, its action is forced:

$$
U \left( \ket{+} \otimes \ket{0} \right)
= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left( U\ket{00} + U\ket{10} \right)
= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left( \ket{00} + \ket{11} \right)
= \ket{\Phi^+} .
$$

But cloning demands the output be

$$
\ket{+} \otimes \ket{+} = \tfrac12 \left( \ket{00} + \ket{01} + \ket{10} + \ket{11} \right) ,
$$

and $\ket{\Phi^+} \ne \ket{+}\otimes\ket{+}$: the first has no amplitude on
$\ket{01}$ or $\ket{10}$ and the second has $0.5$ on each. So no linear $U$ does
both jobs. That is the whole theorem, and the only physics used was linearity.

<Margin label="The inner-product proof">
A second route: suppose $U$ clones both $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}$. Unitaries
preserve inner products, so
$\braket{\psi}{\phi} = \braket{\psi}{\phi}^2$, forcing
$\braket{\psi}{\phi} \in \{0, 1\}$. A cloner can therefore handle only sets of
states that are mutually orthogonal or identical, which is exactly a known
basis.
</Margin>

## Where the CNOT fools you

The CNOT looks like a copier, and on the computational basis it is one:
$\mathrm{CX}\ket{x0} = \ket{xx}$ for $x \in \{0, 1\}$. Many first circuits are
written on that basis and work. The failure is invisible until the input is a
superposition, at which point the CNOT produces entanglement instead of a copy.

That is the general pattern. Cloning is possible for states drawn from a known
orthonormal basis, because then "copy" means "measure and re-prepare", and
measurement in a basis you already know does not disturb the states. It is
impossible for an unknown state precisely because you do not know which basis to
measure in, and any guess destroys the information in the other bases.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Reading the Bell state as a copy">
It is tempting to say the Bell state is "both qubits copied, in superposition".
It is not. In $\ket{\Phi^+}$ neither qubit has a state of its own at all: the
reduced state of each is $I/2$ by T1.M5.L3, which is a fair coin in every basis.
A copy of $\ket{+}$ would be a qubit that reads $+$ with certainty in the $X$
basis, and neither half of a Bell pair does that.
</Callout>

## Auditing a proposed cloner

Proposals to beat the theorem recur, and they always break at one of three
places. Use this checklist on any circuit claiming to copy.

**Is the map linear?** If the circuit measures and then branches on the outcome,
the overall map is not linear, and it also is not a cloner: the measurement
collapses the input, so the "original" is destroyed and what is copied is a
classical bit, not the state.

**Is the map unitary on the full register?** A construction that produces the
right answer while leaving junk in an ancilla has cloned nothing, because the
ancilla is entangled with the output and the reduced state of each supposed copy
is mixed rather than equal to $\ket{\psi}$.

**Does it work for all inputs, or only for the ones tested?** Testing on
$\ket{0}$, $\ket{1}$ and maybe $\ket{+}$ proves nothing. By linearity a map is
determined by its action on a basis, so if it copies $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ it
*cannot* copy $\ket{+}$, and the test to run is exactly the one it will fail.

Approximate and probabilistic cloning are genuinely possible and are not
loopholes. The optimal universal cloner achieves fidelity $5/6$ per copy for
one-to-two cloning of an arbitrary qubit, which is well short of 1 and is
enough to keep the consequences below intact.

## What the theorem buys

No-cloning is not only an obstacle. It is the security assumption of quantum
key distribution: an eavesdropper who cannot copy the transmitted qubits must
measure them, and measuring in the wrong basis introduces detectable errors.
T1.M7.L5 builds BB84 on exactly this.

It also constrains error correction. The classical repetition code copies a bit
three times; the quantum version of T4 cannot copy, and instead spreads one
logical qubit across three physical ones without ever duplicating the state. And
it closes the loophole that would otherwise let entanglement signal: if Bob
could clone his half of a Bell pair many times, he could estimate its basis and
learn Alice's measurement choice, contradicting T1.M5.L2.

<Check question="A circuit takes $\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{0}$, measures the first qubit in the computational basis, and prepares the second qubit in the same basis state as the outcome. For which inputs does it produce two correct copies?">
Only for $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, and any state equal to one of them up to global
phase. For those the measurement is deterministic and non-disturbing, so the
first qubit survives and the second matches. For any other input, say $\ket{+}$,
the measurement collapses the first qubit to $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$ at random,
so the output is $\ket{00}$ or $\ket{11}$, each half the time. Neither qubit is
$\ket{+}$, and the original has been destroyed as well: the circuit produces two
copies of a classical bit rather than one copy of a quantum state.
</Check>
