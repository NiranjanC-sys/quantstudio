A Bloch sphere holds one qubit. Three qubits have eight amplitudes and no Bloch
sphere holds them, because the whole point of entanglement is that the parts do
not determine the whole. The Q-sphere is the standard answer: one sphere, one
node per basis state, and two visual channels carrying magnitude and phase.

## The encoding

Every one of the $2^n$ basis states gets a node on the sphere.

**Latitude is Hamming weight.** $\ket{00\cdots0}$ sits at the north pole,
$\ket{11\cdots1}$ at the south pole, and a string with $k$ ones sits on the
$k$-th ring down. On three qubits there are four rings holding 1, 3, 3 and 1
nodes.

**Node size is $|\text{amplitude}|^2$.** A basis state with zero amplitude has no
node at all, which is why a sparse state looks sparse.

**Node colour is $\arg(\text{amplitude})$**, on the cyclic phase colormap. A
global phase rotates every node's colour together and is therefore visible as no
difference at all once you learn to read relative colour rather than absolute.

<Margin label="What it cannot show">
The Q-sphere plots the amplitude vector, so it needs the state to be pure and it
needs you to have the statevector. It cannot be measured; it is a picture of the
simulator's memory, not of an experiment.
</Margin>

## Two states, two signatures

Here is GHZ on three qubits. Switch the panel between the amplitude table and the
Bloch view as you read.

Two nodes, $\ket{000}$ and $\ket{111}$, both with amplitude $0.7071$ and
probability $0.5$, both the same colour because both phases are zero. On the
Q-sphere that is a barbell through the poles, and it is the signature every GHZ
state has.

Now the product state $\ket{+}\ket{+}\ket{+}$.

<Circuit
  caption="Three Hadamards: all eight nodes present, all the same size, all the same colour."
  show={['amplitudes', 'bloch']}
  ir={{
    version: 1,
    name: "plus-plus-plus",
    qubits: 3,
    clbits: 0,
    ops: [
      { id: "h0", gate: "h", targets: [0] },
      { id: "h1", gate: "h", targets: [1] },
      { id: "h2", gate: "h", targets: [2] }
    ]
  }}
/>

All eight basis states present, each with amplitude $0.3536$ and probability
$0.125$. On the Q-sphere that is a full, evenly lit sphere.

The Bloch view separates them faster than the Q-sphere does. For $\ket{+++}$ each
of the three vectors has length 1 and points along $+x$: every qubit is in a pure
state of its own, which is the definition of a product state. For GHZ all three
vectors have length 0. A reduced state at the centre of the ball is maximally
mixed, and a qubit whose own state is maximally mixed while the global state is
pure is maximally entangled with the rest.

## Phase, made visible

Magnitudes alone do not identify a state. Put a $Z$ on qubit 1 of the product
state:

<Circuit
  caption="The same eight equal magnitudes, but the four states with q1 = 1 now carry a minus sign."
  show={['amplitudes', 'bloch']}
  ir={{
    version: 1,
    name: "plus-plus-plus-with-z",
    qubits: 3,
    clbits: 0,
    ops: [
      { id: "h0", gate: "h", targets: [0] },
      { id: "h1", gate: "h", targets: [1] },
      { id: "h2", gate: "h", targets: [2] },
      { id: "z", gate: "z", targets: [1] }
    ]
  }}
/>

Every probability is still $0.125$. Every measurement in the computational basis
gives the identical distribution. The amplitudes on $\ket{010}$, $\ket{011}$,
$\ket{110}$ and $\ket{111}$ are now $-0.3536$, and on the Q-sphere those four
nodes flip to the opposite colour. The Bloch view agrees: qubit 1's vector has
swung from $+x$ to $-x$, while qubits 0 and 2 are unmoved.

That is the picture of "a phase you cannot measure yet". T1.M9.L2 showed what to
do about it: rotate, and the colour pattern becomes a probability pattern.

Phases need not be signs. Insert an $S$ before the entangling gates of the GHZ
circuit and the south-pole node acquires a quarter turn of phase:

<Circuit
  caption="GHZ with a relative phase of i on the |111> branch. Same two probabilities, different colours."
  show={['amplitudes']}
  ir={{
    version: 1,
    name: "ghz-with-phase",
    qubits: 3,
    clbits: 0,
    ops: [
      { id: "h", gate: "h", targets: [0] },
      { id: "s", gate: "s", targets: [0] },
      { id: "a", gate: "x", targets: [1], controls: [0] },
      { id: "b", gate: "x", targets: [2], controls: [0] }
    ]
  }}
/>

The amplitude on $\ket{000}$ is $0.7071$ and on $\ket{111}$ it is $0.7071\,i$,
a phase of $\pi/2$. Both probabilities remain $0.5$. Two states, identical
measurement statistics in the computational basis, different physics.

<Check question="A Q-sphere shows four equally sized nodes on the middle rings of a three-qubit sphere and nothing at the poles. Is the state entangled?">
You cannot tell from that description alone, and the honest answer is to check the
reduced states. Four equal nodes at Hamming weights 1 and 2 is consistent with an
entangled state, and it is also consistent with a product state such as
$\ket{+}\ket{0}\ket{+}$ having only weight-0 and weight-1 nodes, so the pattern of
which strings are populated is suggestive rather than decisive. What is decisive
is the Bloch view: three unit-length vectors means product, and any vector shorter
than 1 means that qubit is entangled with the rest.
</Check>
