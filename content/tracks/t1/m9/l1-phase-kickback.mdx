In a controlled gate, the control decides and the target obeys. That is what the
name says and it is what the circuit diagram draws. It is also, in the case that
matters most, exactly backwards: there is a standard way to arrange a controlled
gate so that the target is left untouched and the control comes out changed.
Every algorithm in T1.M10 and the whole of phase estimation in T2 are built on
this one inversion.

## The general statement

Let $U$ be a unitary and $\ket{u}$ an eigenvector of it, so
$U\ket{u} = e^{i\varphi}\ket{u}$ for some real $\varphi$. Put the control in
$\ket{+}$ and the target in $\ket{u}$, and apply the controlled-$U$:

$$
\mathrm{C}U \, \frac{\ket{0} + \ket{1}}{\sqrt2}\ket{u}
= \frac{\ket{0}\ket{u} + \ket{1}U\ket{u}}{\sqrt2}
= \frac{\ket{0}\ket{u} + e^{i\varphi}\ket{1}\ket{u}}{\sqrt2}
= \left(\frac{\ket{0} + e^{i\varphi}\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}\right)\ket{u}.
$$

Three equalities. The first is the definition of a controlled gate acting on a
superposed control, by linearity. The second substitutes the eigenvalue equation.
The third factors $\ket{u}$ out, which is legal precisely because the same
$\ket{u}$ appears in both terms.

The output is a product state. The target is in $\ket{u}$, exactly as it started.
The control has picked up a relative phase $e^{i\varphi}$ between its two branches,
and a relative phase is measurable.

<Margin label="Global becomes relative">
$e^{i\varphi}$ multiplying $\ket{u}$ alone would be a global phase on the target
and unobservable. Sitting between the two branches of the control, the same
number is a relative phase and shows up in an $X$-basis measurement. The
controlled gate is a device for converting one into the other.
</Margin>

## The concrete case: CX with the target in $\ket{-}$

Take $U = X$. Its eigenvectors are $\ket{+}$ with eigenvalue $+1$ and $\ket{-}$
with eigenvalue $-1$, since $X\ket{-} = -\ket{-}$. So $\varphi = \pi$ and the
control should go from $\ket{+}$ to $\ket{-}$.

Read the amplitudes: $+0.5$ on $\ket{00}$, $-0.5$ on $\ket{01}$, $-0.5$ on
$\ket{10}$, $+0.5$ on $\ket{11}$. Factorise the sign pattern:

$$
\half\big(\ket{00} - \ket{01} - \ket{10} + \ket{11}\big)
= \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt2} \otimes \frac{\ket{0} - \ket{1}}{\sqrt2}
= \ket{-}\ket{-}.
$$

The target is still $\ket{-}$. The control was $\ket{+}$ and is now $\ket{-}$. A
CNOT whose control was in superposition normally entangles the two qubits; with
the target in $\ket{-}$ it does not entangle them at all, it rotates the control.
Switch the figure to the Bloch view and both vectors point along $-x$, each with
purity 1, which is the numerical signature of a product state.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Nothing was measured and nothing moved">
The eigenvalue $-1$ was a property of $X$ and $\ket{-}$ before the circuit ran.
The controlled gate did not compute it, look it up, or transmit it. It arranged
the state so that a number which was previously unobservable became observable on
a different qubit.
</Callout>

## Making the phase into a bit

A relative phase is not yet a measurement outcome. Measuring the control in the
computational basis gives $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12$ whether it is in $\ket{+}$ or
$\ket{-}$; that is what "the information is in the phase" means. One more
Hadamard converts it.

With the CNOT in place, the control ends in $\ket{-}$, and $H\ket{-} = \ket{1}$:

not". That is Deutsch's algorithm with the labels removed, and you will put the
labels back on in T1.M10.L1.

## Why the target is unchanged

The factorisation step is worth staring at, because it is the whole reason the
trick is useful. If the target had been in a general state $\ket{\psi}$ rather
than an eigenvector, the output would be

$$
\frac{\ket{0}\ket{\psi} + \ket{1}U\ket{\psi}}{\sqrt2},
$$

and $\ket{\psi}$ does not factor out of that unless $U\ket{\psi}$ is a multiple of
$\ket{\psi}$. Instead the two qubits come out entangled, the control's reduced
state is mixed rather than a clean superposition with a phase, and there is no
"kickback" to speak of.

So the recipe has a precondition, and it is the reason every oracle in T1.M10 is
drawn with an ancilla prepared in $\ket{-}$: for a bit-flip oracle
$U_f\ket{x}\ket{y} = \ket{x}\ket{y \oplus f(x)}$, the state $\ket{-}$ is the
eigenvector of the $X$ that gets applied, with eigenvalue $(-1)^{f(x)}$. The
ancilla comes out unchanged every time and the sign lands on $\ket{x}$:

$$
U_f \ket{x}\ket{-} = (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x}\ket{-}.
$$

That identity is the bridge from "an oracle that writes a bit" to "an oracle that
writes a sign", and phase is the thing quantum circuits can interfere.

<Check question="You apply a controlled-$S$ with the control in $\ket{+}$ and the target in $\ket{1}$. What is the control's state afterwards, and what would a computational-basis measurement of the control show?">
$S\ket{1} = i\ket{1}$, so $\ket{1}$ is an eigenvector with $\varphi = \pi/2$, and
the control becomes $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0} + i\ket{1})$. A computational-basis
measurement shows $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12$ and reveals nothing: the phase is
invisible in that basis. To see it you need a $Y$-basis measurement, which is
$S\dg$ then $H$ then a computational measurement, and that returns 0 with
certainty. The target is still $\ket{1}$ either way.
</Check>
