The popular explanation of quantum speedup is that a superposition tries every
answer at once. That explanation is worse than useless, because it predicts an
exponential speedup for every problem and there is no such thing. A measurement
returns one basis state, chosen with probability equal to a squared amplitude,
and "trying everything" buys nothing unless the wrong answers can be made to
cancel. Cancellation is what this lesson is about, and it is the only thing in
the box.

## Amplitudes add, probabilities do not

Here is the rule that separates quantum from randomised computation. To find the
amplitude of an outcome, sum the amplitudes of every path that reaches it, then
square:

$$
\Pr[y] = \Big| \sum_{\text{paths } p \to y} A_p \Big|^2 .
$$

A classical randomised computation has the same structure with one difference:
the path weights are probabilities, which are non-negative, so the sum can only
grow. Quantum path weights are complex, so two paths of equal magnitude and
opposite sign contribute nothing at all.

<Margin label="Back to T0.M1">
This is the $\ell_1$ against $\ell_2$ distinction from the very first lesson of
T0, cashed out. Non-negative weights summing to 1 can never cancel. Complex
weights whose squares sum to 1 can.
</Margin>

## Two paths, one gate apart

The smallest complete example is two Hadamards. Start at $\ket{0}$ and ask for
the amplitude of $\ket{1}$ at the end. There are exactly two paths:

$$
\ket{0} \to \ket{0} \to \ket{1}: \quad
\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = +\half,
$$

$$
\ket{0} \to \ket{1} \to \ket{1}: \quad
\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = -\half.
$$

They cancel exactly. The amplitude of $\ket{1}$ is $0$, so the probability is $0$.

Now put a $Z$ between them. It flips the sign of the $\ket{1}$ leg, which flips
the sign of the second path:

$$
+\half \;\longrightarrow\; +\half, \qquad
-\half \;\longrightarrow\; +\half,
$$

so the amplitude of $\ket{1}$ becomes $1$ and the probability becomes $1$.

<Circuit

One gate changed a sign on one path, and a certain outcome became the opposite
certain outcome. No classical randomised process does that, because there is no
classical operation that turns a probability negative.

## The same calculation on $2^n$ paths

Deutsch-Jozsa is the two-path calculation with $2^n$ paths. After the first
Hadamard layer and the phase oracle the state of the query register is

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x} (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x},
$$

and the second Hadamard layer sends the amplitude of the all-zeros outcome to

$$
A_{0\cdots0} = \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} (-1)^{f(x)} .
$$

Every one of the $2^n$ terms is $\pm 1$. If $f$ is constant they all have the same
sign, the sum is $\pm 2^n$, and $|A|^2 = 1$: the all-zeros string is certain. If
$f$ is balanced, exactly half are $+1$ and half are $-1$, the sum is $0$, and
$|A|^2 = 0$: the all-zeros string is impossible.

Only $\ket{0000}$ and $\ket{0001}$ appear, each at $0.5$, so the three measured
query bits are $000$ with probability 1. Seven of the eight possible query
outcomes have been cancelled to nothing.

## Delete the interference and watch the speedup disappear

Take a balanced oracle, $f(x) = x_0$, implemented as a CNOT from qubit 0 onto the
$\ket{-}$ ancilla. Measure the query register *before* the second Hadamard layer:

Sixteen basis states, each at probability $0.0625$. The query register is
uniform. The oracle ran, its answer is in the statevector as a sign pattern on
half the amplitudes, and measuring here throws all of it away. What you have
built is an expensive fair coin: exactly the sampling you could have done
classically, for the price of a cryogenic refrigerator.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The test to apply to any speedup claim">
Ask where the wrong answers cancel. If nothing in the circuit produces
destructive interference between paths leading to the same outcome, the output
distribution is one a classical sampler could match, and there is no speedup to
explain.
</Callout>

## Where the interference step lives

Three algorithms, three names for the same layer.

**Deutsch and Deutsch-Jozsa.** The final $H^{\otimes n}$. Before it the answer is
a sign pattern; after it the answer is an amplitude.

**Grover.** The diffuser, $2\ket{s}\bra{s} - I$, usually described as inversion
about the mean. The oracle marks the target with a minus sign, and the diffuser
converts that one sign into slightly more amplitude on the target and slightly
less everywhere else. The reason Grover needs $O(\sqrt{N})$ iterations rather than
one is that each round of interference moves the amplitude only a little.

**Phase estimation.** The inverse quantum Fourier transform. Phase kickback writes
$e^{2\pi i \theta k}$ onto the $k$-th basis state of the control register, and the
inverse QFT is precisely the operator whose interference pattern concentrates that
onto the basis state closest to $\theta$.

Different problems, different oracles, same third step: convert a phase pattern
into an amplitude pattern, so that the answers you do not want cancel.

<Check question="A colleague proposes an algorithm: prepare the uniform superposition over $2^n$ inputs, apply a phase oracle, and measure. What speedup does it give?">
None. The phase oracle is diagonal, so it changes no magnitude, and measuring
immediately afterwards returns a uniformly random $x$ from $\{0,1\}^n$, exactly as
in the figure above. Every phase the oracle wrote is squared away by the Born rule.
The proposal is missing the third step, and the third step is the algorithm; the
superposition and the oracle are only the setup that makes interference possible.
</Check>
