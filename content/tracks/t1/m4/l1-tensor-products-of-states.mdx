Two classical bits are described by two variables. Two qubits are described by
one vector of four numbers, and the reason is not that quantum states are
mysterious: it is that the space of states of a composite system is a tensor
product rather than a Cartesian product. This lesson does the arithmetic and
fixes the index convention that every later lesson depends on.

## The product, entry by entry

If qubit 0 is in $\ket{\psi} = (a_0, a_1)$ and qubit 1 is in
$\ket{\phi} = (b_0, b_1)$, the joint state is

$$
\ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\phi}
= \begin{pmatrix} a_0 b_0 \\ a_0 b_1 \\ a_1 b_0 \\ a_1 b_1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The pattern is: take each entry of the first vector in turn and multiply the
whole second vector by it. For three qubits the same rule gives 8 entries, and
for $n$ qubits it gives $2^n$.

The four rows are labelled by the four two-bit strings in the order
$\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$, and the labelling is what makes the
formula memorable: row $\ket{q_0 q_1}$ holds the product of the amplitude of
$q_0$ in the first factor and the amplitude of $q_1$ in the second.

<Margin label="Convention, stated once">
Qubit 0 is the most significant bit. Basis index $i$ assigns qubit $q$ the value
`(i >> (n - 1 - q)) & 1`, so $\ket{10}$ is index 2 and the leftmost symbol in a
ket is always qubit 0. Cirq and Nielsen and Chuang agree with this; Qiskit
reverses it. Every amplitude table in this track is printed in this order.
</Margin>

## A worked pair

Let qubit 0 be in $(0.6, 0.8)$, prepared by $R_y(1.8545904)$, and qubit 1 in
$\ket{+} = (0.707107, 0.707107)$. Then

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \otimes
\begin{pmatrix} 0.707107 \\ 0.707107 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}
0.6 \times 0.707107 \\ 0.6 \times 0.707107 \\
0.8 \times 0.707107 \\ 0.8 \times 0.707107
\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.424264 \\ 0.424264 \\ 0.565685 \\ 0.565685 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Squaring gives probabilities $0.18, 0.18, 0.32, 0.32$, which sum to 1. Notice
that each is a product of a single-qubit probability with another:
$0.18 = 0.36 \times 0.5$ and $0.32 = 0.64 \times 0.5$. Product states have
product distributions, and the two qubits are statistically independent.

## Ordering is not a detail

Put a Hadamard on qubit 1 alone and the state is
$\ket{0} \otimes \ket{+} = (0.707107, 0.707107, 0, 0)$: the non-zero amplitudes
sit on $\ket{00}$ and $\ket{01}$. Put it on qubit 0 alone instead and the state
is $\ket{+} \otimes \ket{0} = (0.707107, 0, 0.707107, 0)$, with the non-zero
amplitudes on $\ket{00}$ and $\ket{10}$. Same gate, same number of qubits,
different vector.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Where the SDKs disagree">
Qiskit prints the same two states as `|00>, |10>` and `|00>, |01>` respectively,
because it puts qubit 0 in the least significant position. Nothing physical
differs; the bitstrings are reversed. Any time you compare a printed
counts dictionary across tools, reverse the strings first. T0.M5.L3 is entirely
about this hazard, and it is the single most common source of wrong answers when
porting a circuit.
</Callout>

## Normalisation is automatic

If both factors are normalised, so is the product. Sum the squared moduli of the
four entries and factor:

$$
\sum_{j,k} |a_j b_k|^2
= \sum_{j,k} |a_j|^2 |b_k|^2
= \left( \sum_j |a_j|^2 \right)\left( \sum_k |b_k|^2 \right)
= 1 \times 1 = 1 .
$$

The double sum factored because every term is a product of one index with the
other, which is the defining feature of the tensor product. The argument does
not depend on the number of qubits, so a product of $n$ normalised states is
normalised, and the state $\ket{0}^{\otimes n}$ that every circuit starts in is
a legitimate state of the whole register.

The same factoring applies to inner products:

$$
\left( \bra{\psi_1} \otimes \bra{\phi_1} \right)
\left( \ket{\psi_2} \otimes \ket{\phi_2} \right)
= \braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2} \, \braket{\phi_1}{\phi_2} ,
$$

so orthogonality of either factor makes the products orthogonal. That is why the
four states $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$ form an orthonormal basis:
they are products of orthonormal pairs.

## Operators tensor too

A gate acting on qubit 0 only is really $A \otimes I$ on the pair, with

$$
A \otimes I = \begin{pmatrix} a_{00} I & a_{01} I \\ a_{10} I & a_{11} I \end{pmatrix}
$$

as a $4 \times 4$ matrix in $2 \times 2$ blocks. The two useful consequences:
$(A \otimes B)(\ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\phi}) = A\ket{\psi} \otimes B\ket{\phi}$,
so gates on different qubits never interfere with one another and can be applied
in either order; and $(A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = AC \otimes BD$, so products
factor as well. A circuit made only of single-qubit gates therefore keeps a
product state a product state forever, which is why entanglement in T1.M4.L3
requires a genuinely two-qubit gate.

<Check question="Write $\ket{-} \otimes \ket{1}$ as a length-4 vector and give its outcome probabilities. Which basis states have zero amplitude?">
$\ket{-} = (0.707107, -0.707107)$ and $\ket{1} = (0, 1)$, so the product is
$(0.707107 \times 0,\ 0.707107 \times 1,\ -0.707107 \times 0,\ -0.707107 \times 1)$,
that is $(0,\ 0.707107,\ 0,\ -0.707107)$. The probabilities are
$0, 0.5, 0, 0.5$: outcomes $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{11}$ are equally likely and
$\ket{00}$ and $\ket{10}$ never occur, which is qubit 1 being $\ket{1}$ with
certainty. The minus sign is invisible in the probabilities and would matter
after a Hadamard on qubit 0.
</Check>
