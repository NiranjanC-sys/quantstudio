Hardware gives you CNOT, or CZ, and single-qubit gates. Algorithms ask for
controlled-$R_z$, controlled-$H$, controlled-$U$ for an arbitrary $U$, and
controlled operations with five controls at once. Every one of those has to be
built, and the constructions are short enough to derive rather than look up.

## The easy case: conjugate the target

If $U = V W V^{\dagger}$ for some $V$, then controlled-$U$ is $V$ on the target,
controlled-$W$, then $V^{\dagger}$ on the target. The reason is that when the
control is $\ket{0}$ the two $V$ factors cancel, and when it is $\ket{1}$ they
sandwich $W$.

The workhorse instance uses $HZH = X$ from T1.M3.L1:

$$
\mathrm{CX}_{0\to1} = (I \otimes H)\,\mathrm{CZ}\,(I \otimes H) ,
$$

so a CNOT is a CZ with Hadamards on the target, and a CZ is a CNOT with
Hadamards on the target. Devices whose native entangler is CZ compile every
CNOT this way, at a cost of two single-qubit gates and no extra two-qubit gates.

<Margin label="Why this is free">
Single-qubit gates on superconducting hardware are roughly an order of magnitude
faster and less noisy than two-qubit gates. Any rewrite that trades two-qubit
gates for single-qubit gates is a win; any rewrite that goes the other way needs
justification.
</Margin>

## Controlled rotations in two CNOTs

For $U = R_z(\theta)$ there is a construction cheaper than the general one.
Split the angle in half and conjugate by CNOTs on the target:

$$
\mathrm{C}R_z(\theta):\quad
R_z(\theta/2),\ \mathrm{CX},\ R_z(-\theta/2),\ \mathrm{CX} .
$$

When the control is $\ket{0}$ the two CNOTs do nothing and the target sees
$R_z(\theta/2)R_z(-\theta/2) = I$. When the control is $\ket{1}$ the CNOTs flip
the target between the two rotations, and since $X R_z(-\theta/2) X = R_z(\theta/2)$
the target sees $R_z(\theta/2)R_z(\theta/2) = R_z(\theta)$. The same trick works
for $R_y$ and, with an extra basis change, for $R_x$.

## The general case: A, X, B, X, C

Any one-qubit $U$ can be controlled with two CNOTs and three single-qubit gates,
plus one phase gate on the control. Start from the Euler form of T1.M3.L5,

$$
U = e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta) ,
$$

and define

$$
A = R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma/2),
\qquad
B = R_y(-\gamma/2)R_z\!\left(-\tfrac{\delta + \beta}{2}\right),
\qquad
C = R_z\!\left(\tfrac{\delta - \beta}{2}\right) .
$$

Two facts make the construction work. First $ABC = I$: the $R_y$ halves cancel
and the three $R_z$ angles sum to
$\beta - \tfrac{\delta+\beta}{2} + \tfrac{\delta-\beta}{2} = 0$. Second
$AXBXC = e^{-i\alpha}U$, which follows from $XR_y(\phi)X = R_y(-\phi)$ and
$XR_z(\phi)X = R_z(-\phi)$: pushing the two $X$ gates outward flips the sign of
every angle inside $B$ and turns the product into the Euler form.

So the circuit is $C$, CNOT, $B$, CNOT, $A$ on the target, and $P(\alpha)$ on
the control to supply the phase. When the control is $\ket{0}$ the CNOTs vanish
and the target sees $ABC = I$; when it is $\ket{1}$ it sees $AXBXC$, and the
control picks up $e^{i\alpha}$ exactly on the branch that needs it.

Run it for $U = H$, whose Euler angles from T1.M3.L5 are $\alpha = \pi/2$,
$\beta = 0$, $\gamma = \pi/2$, $\delta = \pi$. That gives
$A = R_y(\pi/4)$, $B = R_y(-\pi/4)R_z(-\pi/2)$ and $C = R_z(\pi/2)$.

The control was in $\ket{+}$, so the $\ket{0}$ branch keeps the target at
$\ket{0}$ with amplitude $0.707107$, and the $\ket{1}$ branch splits it into
$0.5$ and $0.5$. That is exactly what a controlled Hadamard does, and the seven
gates above use two CNOTs, matching the bound.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Forgetting the phase gate gives the wrong gate">
Drop the $P(\alpha)$ on the control and you build controlled-$(e^{-i\alpha}U)$
instead of controlled-$U$. On the target alone the difference is a global phase
and invisible; under a control it is a relative phase between branches and
entirely visible. This is the concrete reason T1.M3.L5 insisted on tracking
$\alpha$ rather than discarding it.
</Callout>

## More than one control

For $k$ controls the standard construction spends ancillas to build the AND of
the controls one Toffoli at a time.

Take $k$ control qubits and $k-2$ clean ancillas initialised to $\ket{0}$.
Compute the running AND: a Toffoli from controls 1 and 2 into ancilla 1, then
from control 3 and ancilla 1 into ancilla 2, and so on, using $k-2$ Toffolis to
land the AND of the first $k-1$ controls in ancilla $k-2$. One more Toffoli, from
control $k$ and that ancilla into the target, performs the flip. Then run the
first $k-2$ Toffolis again in reverse to return the ancillas to $\ket{0}$, which
is the uncomputation step of T0.M6.L3 and is not optional: leaving the ancillas
entangled would destroy the interference the rest of the algorithm depends on.

$$
\text{Toffoli count} = 2(k-2) + 1 = 2k - 3 .
$$

Each Toffoli costs six CNOTs by the decomposition of T1.M4.L6, so a
$k$-controlled $X$ costs about $12k - 18$ two-qubit gates and needs $k-2$
ancillas.

| $k$ | ancillas | Toffolis | CNOTs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $3$ | $1$ | $3$ | $18$ |
| $4$ | $2$ | $5$ | $30$ |
| $5$ | $3$ | $7$ | $42$ |
| $10$ | $8$ | $17$ | $102$ |

The cost is linear in $k$, which is the good news, and the constant is 12, which
is the bad news: a five-controlled gate is 42 of the noisiest operations on the
device. Ancilla-free constructions exist and cost $O(k^2)$ gates instead, so
the choice is a genuine space-time trade, and it is the kind of decision T3
makes when compiling an oracle.

<Check question="You need a controlled-$S$ gate and the device offers only CNOT and single-qubit gates. Give a two-CNOT construction.">
$S = P(\pi/2) = e^{i\pi/4}R_z(\pi/2)$, so controlled-$S$ is controlled-$R_z(\pi/2)$
together with $P(\pi/4)$ on the control. Using the two-CNOT rotation
construction: $R_z(\pi/4)$ on the target, CNOT, $R_z(-\pi/4)$ on the target,
CNOT, then $P(\pi/4)$ on the control. Two CNOTs and three single-qubit gates.
Equivalently, and more symmetrically, controlled-$S$ is $T$ on the control, $T$
on the target, CNOT, $T^{\dagger}$ on the target, CNOT, which is the form used
inside the quantum Fourier transform in T2.
</Check>
