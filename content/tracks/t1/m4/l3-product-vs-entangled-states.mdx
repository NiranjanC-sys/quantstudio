Every two-qubit state is a vector of four amplitudes. Only some of them come
from a pair of one-qubit states. The ones that do not are entangled, and this
lesson gives a one-line test to tell which is which, plus a proof that the test
is not merely a failure of imagination.

## The test

A general two-qubit state is
$\ket{\chi} = a_0\ket{00} + a_1\ket{01} + a_2\ket{10} + a_3\ket{11}$. Suppose it
factors as $(\alpha_0, \alpha_1) \otimes (\beta_0, \beta_1)$. Then

$$
a_0 = \alpha_0\beta_0, \quad a_1 = \alpha_0\beta_1, \quad
a_2 = \alpha_1\beta_0, \quad a_3 = \alpha_1\beta_1 ,
$$

and multiplying the first and last against the middle two,

$$
a_0 a_3 = \alpha_0\beta_0\alpha_1\beta_1 = a_1 a_2 .
$$

So $a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2 = 0$ is necessary for a product state. It is also
sufficient: arrange the amplitudes in the matrix

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} a_0 & a_1 \\ a_2 & a_3 \end{pmatrix},
$$

and the condition says $\det M = 0$, that is $M$ has rank at most 1, which means
$M = \alpha \beta^{\mathsf T}$ for some vectors, which is exactly a product
decomposition. Normalising the two factors is then a matter of moving a scalar
between them.

<Margin label="Concurrence">
The quantity $C = 2|a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2|$ is the **concurrence** of a two-qubit
pure state. It runs from 0 for a product state to 1 for a maximally entangled
one, so the test above is the statement $C = 0$, and the size of $C$ measures
how far from product the state is. T1.M5.L5 relates it to entropy.
</Margin>

## Bell fails the test

The state
$\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left( \ket{00} + \ket{11} \right)$
has $a_0 = a_3 = 0.707107$ and $a_1 = a_2 = 0$. Then

$$
a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2 = 0.5 - 0 = 0.5 \ne 0 ,
$$

so it does not factor, and its concurrence is 1: it is maximally entangled.

The direct argument is worth seeing too, because it is the argument that
generalises. Suppose $\ket{\Phi^+} = (\alpha_0, \alpha_1) \otimes (\beta_0, \beta_1)$.
Matching the $\ket{01}$ coefficient gives $\alpha_0 \beta_1 = 0$, so
$\alpha_0 = 0$ or $\beta_1 = 0$. If $\alpha_0 = 0$ then the $\ket{00}$
coefficient $\alpha_0\beta_0$ is 0, contradicting $a_0 = 1/\sqrt2$. If
$\beta_1 = 0$ then the $\ket{11}$ coefficient $\alpha_1\beta_1$ is 0,
contradicting $a_3 = 1/\sqrt2$. Both branches fail, so no factorisation exists.

The Bell state is what a Hadamard and a CNOT produce from $\ket{00}$.

## Same marginals, different state

Now build a product state with identical single-qubit statistics. Put both
qubits in $\ket{+}$:

$$
\ket{+} \otimes \ket{+} = \tfrac12 \left( \ket{00} + \ket{01} + \ket{10} + \ket{11} \right) .
$$

Its test value is $0.25 - 0.25 = 0$, so it is a product state, as constructed.
Each qubit reads 0 or 1 with probability $0.5$, exactly as for the Bell state:
for $\ket{\Phi^+}$, $\Pr[q_0 = 0] = 0.5$ from the single term $\ket{00}$, and for
$\ket{+}\otimes\ket{+}$ it is $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$. The two states are
indistinguishable by any measurement on one qubit alone.

The joint distributions are completely different. The Bell state produces only
$00$ and $11$, so the two bits always agree. The product state produces all four
strings equally, so the bits agree half the time. Correlation, not marginals, is
where entanglement shows up.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Correlation alone is not entanglement">
A classical coin copied onto two cards also produces only $00$ and $11$. That
mixture is correlated and not entangled, and no amount of measuring in the
computational basis distinguishes it from $\ket{\Phi^+}$. What separates them is
what happens in another basis. Apply $H$ to both qubits of $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and the
state is $\ket{\Phi^+}$ again, so the outcomes still agree every time; do the
same to the classical mixture and the outcomes agree only half the time. T1.M6
turns this gap into a testable inequality.
</Callout>

Applying $H$ to both qubits of the product state $\ket{+}\otimes\ket{+}$ gives
$\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}$, a certain outcome of $00$. Applying it to
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ leaves $\ket{\Phi^+}$ unchanged, still a coin flip that agrees.
Three preparations, indistinguishable on one qubit, separated cleanly by a
two-qubit measurement in the right basis.

## Partly entangled states

Entanglement is not binary in degree, only in kind. Prepare qubit 0 in
$(0.6, 0.8)$ and then apply a CNOT:

$$
\ket{\chi} = 0.6\ket{00} + 0.8\ket{11},
\qquad a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2 = 0.48 ,
$$

so $C = 0.96$: entangled, but not maximally. Its marginals are $0.36$ and
$0.64$ on each qubit, and its outcomes still always agree.

<Check question="Is $\tfrac12\left(\ket{00} - \ket{01} + \ket{10} - \ket{11}\right)$ entangled? If not, factor it.">
The amplitudes are $a_0 = 0.5$, $a_1 = -0.5$, $a_2 = 0.5$, $a_3 = -0.5$, so
$a_0 a_3 - a_1 a_2 = -0.25 + 0.25 = 0$ and the state is a product. To factor,
group by qubit 0: the state is
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{-} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket{1}\otimes\ket{-}$,
which is $\ket{+} \otimes \ket{-}$. Both qubits are in an even superposition, so
the marginals are again $0.5$ and $0.5$, and the concurrence is 0.
</Check>
