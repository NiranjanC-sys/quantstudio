What is the state of one qubit of a Bell pair? Every tool T1 gave you says a
state is a vector, and there is no vector that answers the question. Half of
$\ket{\Phi^+}$ is not $\ket{0}$, not $\ket{1}$, not $\ket{+}$, and not any
superposition of them, yet the qubit exists and you can measure it. The object
that answers the question is the density matrix, and this module is built on it
because every statement about noise, every syndrome, and every fidelity in T4 is
a statement about density matrices.

## From a vector to a matrix

Start where you already are. A pure state $\ket{\psi}$ has a density matrix

$$
\rho = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi},
$$

the outer product of the vector with itself. For $\ket{+} = \isqrt(\ket 0 + \ket 1)$
that is

$$
\rho_+ = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Nothing has been gained yet: $\rho_+$ carries exactly the information $\ket{+}$
carried, minus the global phase, which was never physical anyway. That last
clause is the first small win. Replacing $\ket\psi$ by $e^{i\varphi}\ket\psi$
leaves $\rho$ untouched, because the phase multiplies the ket and its conjugate
cancels it in the bra. The density matrix is the state with the unphysical
degree of freedom already quotiented out.

<Margin label="Outer versus inner">
$\braket{\psi}{\psi}$ is a number, the norm squared. $\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$ is a
matrix, and for a normalised state it is the projector onto the line spanned by
$\ket\psi$. Both are written with the same two symbols in the opposite order,
which is why misreading one for the other is the standard first mistake.
</Margin>

The real win comes from what else the formalism can hold. A density matrix does
not have to be a single outer product. Any convex combination

$$
\rho = \sum_i p_i \ketbra{\psi_i}{\psi_i},
\qquad p_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_i p_i = 1,
$$

is also a legitimate state, and it describes a situation the vector formalism
cannot: an ensemble in which the system is $\ket{\psi_i}$ with probability $p_i$
and you do not know which. Reduced states of entangled systems, outputs of noisy
channels and the halves of Bell pairs all live here.

## The three conditions

A square matrix $\rho$ on $\C^d$ is a density matrix exactly when it satisfies
three conditions. They are the whole definition, and checking them is a
mechanical task you should be able to do on a $2 \times 2$ matrix in your head.

**Hermitian**: $\rho\dg = \rho$. This makes the eigenvalues real, which they must
be because they are going to be probabilities.

**Unit trace**: $\tr\rho = 1$. The probabilities in the ensemble sum to one.

**Positive semidefinite**: $\bra{\phi}\rho\ket{\phi} \ge 0$ for every $\ket\phi$,
equivalently every eigenvalue is at least zero. Without this you could produce a
negative probability for some measurement outcome.

The third is the one people skip and the one that actually bites. Consider

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

It is Hermitian and its trace is $1$, so two of three conditions hold. Its
eigenvalues are $1.3$ and $-0.3$, so it is not a state. Measuring the observable
whose eigenvector is $\ket{-}$ would return probability $-0.3$. Matrices like
this come out of real tomography fits, which is why T4.M2.L4 spends a section on
what to do when your reconstruction lands outside the set of states.

## Expectations without the Born rule

Given an observable $A$, the expected value in state $\rho$ is

$$
\expect{A}_\rho = \tr(\rho A).
$$

One formula, valid for pure and mixed states alike. Check it against what you
already know. For a pure state $\rho = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$,

$$
\tr\big(\ketbra{\psi}{\psi} A\big) = \bra{\psi} A \ket{\psi},
$$

using cyclicity of the trace. That is the T1 expression, recovered exactly.

Run the numbers on two states. For $\rho_+ = \ketbra{+}{+}$,

$$
\expect{X} = 1, \qquad \expect{Y} = 0, \qquad \expect{Z} = 0.
$$

For the classical mixture $\rho_m = 0.75\,\ketbra{0}{0} + 0.25\,\ketbra{1}{1}$,
which is the diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(0.75, 0.25)$,

$$
\expect{Z} = 0.75 - 0.25 = 0.5, \qquad \expect{X} = 0, \qquad \expect{Y} = 0.
$$

Outcome probabilities are the special case where $A$ is a projector. The chance
of outcome $m$ under a projective measurement $\{P_m\}$ is $\tr(P_m \rho)$, and
the post-measurement state is $P_m \rho P_m / \tr(P_m\rho)$. Every Born-rule
computation you did in T1 is this expression with $\rho$ a rank-one projector.

## Purity, in one number

The diagonal of $\rho_m$ above looks like a probability distribution over
$\ket 0$ and $\ket 1$, and it is. But $\rho_+$ also has diagonal entries that
look like a distribution, $(0.5, 0.5)$, and $\rho_+$ is a pure state. The
diagonal alone cannot tell them apart. The invariant that can is

$$
\gamma = \tr(\rho^2),
$$

the purity. It equals $1$ if and only if $\rho$ is a pure state, and it drops to
a minimum of $1/d$ for the maximally mixed state $I/d$.

| state | $\rho$ | $\tr(\rho^2)$ | pure? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\ket{+}$ | $\frac12\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ | $1.000000$ | yes |
| $\mathrm{diag}(0.75, 0.25)$ | $\frac14\big(\begin{smallmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ | $0.625000$ | no |
| $I/2$ | $\frac12\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ | $0.500000$ | no |

The two states with identical diagonals, $\rho_+$ and $I/2$, are separated by
purity $1$ against $0.5$. The off-diagonal entries are what distinguishes them,
and those entries are called coherences for exactly that reason. The next lesson
makes their disappearance the definition of decoherence.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The trace is not the norm">
For a pure state $\tr\rho = \braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1$, so trace and norm agree and
it is easy to conflate them. They come apart on mixed states: $\tr\rho = 1$
always, while $\tr(\rho^2) < 1$. If a calculation gives $\tr\rho \ne 1$ you have
lost normalisation; if it gives $\tr(\rho^2) > 1$ you have made an arithmetic
error, because no state can exceed one.
</Callout>

## The Bloch parametrisation, previewed

On one qubit the three conditions have a geometric solution. Any Hermitian
$2\times 2$ matrix with unit trace can be written

$$
\rho = \frac{1}{2}\big(I + r_x X + r_y Y + r_z Z\big),
$$

and positivity is exactly the condition $\lvert \vec r\rvert \le 1$. The three
components are the three expectations, $r_k = \tr(\rho \sigma_k)$, so the vector
is not an abstraction: it is what you measure. T4.M1.L3 works in this picture
throughout. For now note the two entries in the table: $\ket{+}$ has
$\vec r = (1,0,0)$, on the surface, and $I/2$ has $\vec r = (0,0,0)$, at the
centre.

<Check question="A one-qubit state has $\expect{X} = 0.3$, $\expect{Y} = 0$ and $\expect{Z} = 0.4$. Write its density matrix, give its eigenvalues and purity, and say whether it is pure.">
Substituting into the Bloch form,

$$
\rho = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 + 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.3 & 1 - 0.4 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.15 \\ 0.15 & 0.3 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The Bloch radius is $\lvert \vec r\rvert = \sqrt{0.09 + 0.16} = 0.5$, which is
inside the ball, so the state is legitimate and mixed. The eigenvalues are
$(1 \pm \lvert \vec r \rvert)/2 = 0.75$ and $0.25$, both non-negative as
required, and the purity is

$$
\tr(\rho^2) = \frac{1 + \lvert\vec r\rvert^2}{2} = \frac{1 + 0.25}{2} = 0.625,
$$

below $1$, so not pure. Notice this is the same purity as
$\mathrm{diag}(0.75,0.25)$ in the table, and the same eigenvalues: the two states
differ only by a rotation, which purity cannot see.
</Check>
