What is the square root of $\big(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix} \big)$, and what does it mean to raise $e$ to a matrix? Both questions have the
same answer, and it is the spectral theorem: apply the ordinary scalar function
to each eigenvalue and leave the eigenvectors alone. Every gate in T1.M3 is an
exponential of a Pauli, so this is the lesson where observables turn into
operations.

## Functions through the spectrum

If $A = \sum_k \lambda_k \ketbra{v_k}{v_k}$ is Hermitian and $f$ is any function
defined on the eigenvalues, define

$$
f(A) = \sum_k f(\lambda_k)\, \ketbra{v_k}{v_k} .
$$

This is consistent with the obvious cases. For $f(x) = x^2$, the cross terms
vanish because $\braket{v_j}{v_k} = \delta_{jk}$, leaving
$\sum_k \lambda_k^2 \ketbra{v_k}{v_k} = A^2$. The same argument extends to every
power, hence to every polynomial, hence to anything with a power series.

Take $A = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\big) = 3\ketbra{+}{+} + 1\ketbra{-}{-}$ from the previous lesson. Its square root is

$$
\sqrt{A} = \sqrt3\,\ketbra{+}{+} + 1\,\ketbra{-}{-}
= \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{2} \\[2pt]
\frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2}\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1.366025 & 0.366025 \\ 0.366025 & 1.366025\end{pmatrix} .
$$

Squaring it returns $A$: the diagonal gives $1.366025^2 + 0.366025^2 = 1.866025 + 0.133975 = 2$ and the off-diagonal gives
$2 \cdot 1.366025 \cdot 0.366025 = 1$. Its exponential is

$$
e^{A} = e^{3}\ketbra{+}{+} + e^{1}\ketbra{-}{-}
= \begin{pmatrix} 11.401909 & 8.683628 \\ 8.683628 & 11.401909\end{pmatrix},
$$

whose entries are $\tfrac{e^3 \pm e}{2}$.

<Margin label="Not the entrywise function">
$f(A)$ is emphatically not $f$ applied to each entry. The $(1,1)$ entry of
$e^{A}$ above is $11.401909$, not $e^2 = 7.389056$. The only matrices for which
the entrywise recipe is right are diagonal ones, which is exactly the case where
the eigenvectors are the standard basis.
</Margin>

The exponential also has a series definition,
$e^{M} = \sum_{k\ge 0} M^k / k!$, which converges for every square $M$ and
agrees with the spectral definition whenever both apply. The series is the more
general of the two, since it needs no diagonalisability.

## The Pauli exponential

Let $\hat n = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$ be a real unit vector and write

$$
\hat n \cdot \vec\sigma = n_x X + n_y Y + n_z Z .
$$

The key algebraic fact is that this matrix squares to the identity:

$$
(\hat n \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = \sum_i n_i^2 \sigma_i^2
+ \sum_{i < j} n_i n_j (\sigma_i \sigma_j + \sigma_j \sigma_i)
= \left(\textstyle\sum_i n_i^2\right) I = I,
$$

using $\sigma_i^2 = I$ and the anticommutation
$\sigma_i\sigma_j = -\sigma_j\sigma_i$ for $i \ne j$, which kills the cross
terms. Now feed that into the series for $\exp(-i\theta\, \hat n \cdot \vec\sigma)$. Even powers give $I$, odd powers give $\hat n \cdot \vec\sigma$:

$$
e^{-i\theta \hat n\cdot\vec\sigma}
= \sum_{m}\frac{(-i\theta)^{2m}}{(2m)!}I
+ \sum_{m}\frac{(-i\theta)^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma .
$$

Since $(-i)^{2m} = (-1)^m$ and $(-i)^{2m+1} = -i(-1)^m$, the two sums are the
cosine and sine series of T0.M2.L2:

$$
\boxed{\;e^{-i\theta\, \hat n\cdot\vec\sigma} = \cos\theta\, I
- i\sin\theta\, (\hat n \cdot \vec\sigma). \;}
$$

Set $\hat n = (1,0,0)$ and $\theta = \varphi/2$ to get the standard rotation
gate,

$$
R_x(\varphi) = e^{-i\varphi X/2} = \cos\tfrac{\varphi}{2} I
- i \sin\tfrac{\varphi}{2} X
= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\varphi}{2} & -i\sin\frac{\varphi}{2} \\
-i\sin\frac{\varphi}{2} & \cos\frac{\varphi}{2}\end{pmatrix} .
$$

At $\varphi = \pi/2$ this is
$\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0.707107 & -0.707107i\\ -0.707107i & 0.707107 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$, which is what the simulator's `rx` gate contains. The
half-angle is a convention chosen so that $\varphi$ is the rotation angle on the
Bloch sphere, and it is why $R_x(2\pi) = -I$ rather than $I$.

<Callout tone="note" title="Hermitian in, unitary out">
If $A\dg = A$ then $\left(e^{-iA}\right)\dg = e^{iA\dg} = e^{iA} = \left(e^{-iA}\right)^{-1}$, so $e^{-iA}$ is unitary. Every observable generates
a family of gates, and every gate is generated by some observable. In T4 the
observable is a Hamiltonian and the parameter is time.
</Callout>

## When the exponents do not commute

For scalars, $e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a+b}$. For matrices this holds only when
$AB = BA$. The proof of the scalar identity multiplies two series and collects
terms using the binomial theorem, and the binomial theorem for $(A+B)^n$ needs
commutativity: already at $n = 2$,

$$
(A+B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2 \ne A^2 + 2AB + B^2 \quad\text{unless } AB = BA .
$$

Take $A = -i\tfrac{\pi}{4}X$ and $B = -i\tfrac{\pi}{4}Z$, so
$e^{A} = R_x(\pi/2)$ and $e^{B} = R_z(\pi/2)$ exactly. Then

$$
e^{A}e^{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 - 0.5i & 0.5 - 0.5i \\ -0.5 - 0.5i & 0.5 + 0.5i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e^{A+B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.444016 - 0.633581i & -0.633581i \\ -0.633581i & 0.444016 + 0.633581i\end{pmatrix} .
$$

These differ by $0.760029$ in Frobenius norm, which is not a rounding error. The
second matrix is itself a Pauli exponential: $A + B = -i\tfrac{\pi}{4}(X+Z) = -i\tfrac{\pi\sqrt2}{4}\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$ with
$\hat n = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,1)$, so the boxed formula applies with
$\theta = \pi\sqrt2/4 = 1.110721$, giving $\cos\theta = 0.444016$ and
$\sin\theta \cdot \isqrt = 0.633581$. Every digit above is that formula.

The failure is measured by the commutator $[A,B] = AB - BA$. Here
$[X, Z] = -2iY \ne 0$, and the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion begins

$$
e^{A}e^{B} = \exp\left(A + B + \half[A,B] + \cdots\right).
$$

Trotterisation in T4 is the art of making the neglected terms small by
chopping the exponent into many short pieces.

abstraction; it is the reason a compiler may not reorder those two instructions.

<Check question="Compute $e^{-i\theta Z}$ from the boxed formula and check it against the spectral definition.">
With $\hat n = (0,0,1)$ the formula gives
$e^{-i\theta Z} = \cos\theta\, I - i\sin\theta\, Z = \operatorname{diag}(\cos\theta - i \sin\theta,\; \cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = \operatorname{diag}\left(e^{-i\theta}, e^{i\theta}\right)$.

Spectrally, $Z = (+1)\ketbra{0}{0} + (-1)\ketbra{1}{1}$, so applying
$f(x) = e^{-i\theta x}$ to the eigenvalues gives
$e^{-i\theta}\ketbra{0}{0} + e^{+i\theta}\ketbra{1}{1}$, the same diagonal
matrix. Note that $R_z(\varphi) = e^{-i\varphi Z/2}$ carries the phases
$e^{\mp i\varphi/2}$, differing from the gate
$P(\varphi) = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$ by the global phase
$e^{-i\varphi/2}$.
</Check>
