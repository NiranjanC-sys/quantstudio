Most vectors get rotated by a matrix. A few get only stretched. Those few are
the eigenvectors, and for the operator classes of the previous lesson they are
not a curiosity but a complete description: a Hermitian matrix is nothing more
than a list of real numbers attached to an orthonormal basis. Once you can write
an operator that way, you can measure it, exponentiate it, and take its square
root, all by doing arithmetic on the numbers in the list.

## Eigenvalues by hand

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $\ket{v} \ne 0$ when
$A\ket{v} = \lambda\ket{v}$, equivalently when $(A - \lambda I)\ket{v} = 0$,
equivalently when

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0 .
$$

Take the real symmetric matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det\begin{pmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda\end{pmatrix}
= (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 .
$$

The roots are $\lambda = 3$ and $\lambda = 1$. For $\lambda = 3$, the equation
$(A - 3I)v = 0$ reads $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = v_2$ and the normalised
eigenvector is $\ket{+}$. For $\lambda = 1$ it reads $v_1 + v_2 = 0$, giving
$\ket{-}$. The two eigenvectors are orthogonal, which is not luck.

<Margin label="Orthogonality is automatic">
If $A\dg = A$ and $A\ket{v} = \lambda\ket{v}$, $A\ket{w} = \mu\ket{w}$ with
$\lambda \ne \mu$, then $\lambda\braket{w}{v} = \bra{w}A\ket{v} = \mu\braket{w}{v}$, using reality of $\mu$. So
$(\lambda - \mu)\braket{w}{v} = 0$ and the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
</Margin>

## Eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are real

Let $A\dg = A$ and $A\ket{v} = \lambda\ket{v}$ with $\braket{v}{v} = 1$.
Sandwich:

$$
\lambda = \lambda\braket{v}{v} = \bra{v}A\ket{v} .
$$

Now take the conjugate of that number. Conjugating a scalar of the form
$\bra{v}A\ket{v}$ is the same as daggering it, and by the reversal rule
$\left(\bra{v}A\ket{v}\right)\dg = \bra{v}A\dg\ket{v} = \bra{v}A\ket{v}$. So
$\bar\lambda = \lambda$, and $\lambda$ is real. $\blacksquare$

The unitary case is the same trick with norms. If $U\dg U = I$ and
$U\ket{v} = \lambda\ket{v}$ with $\ket{v}$ a unit vector, then

$$
1 = \lVert v \rVert = \lVert Uv\rVert = \lVert \lambda v \rVert = |\lambda| ,
$$

so every eigenvalue of a unitary has modulus 1 and can be written
$e^{i\theta}$. Real numbers for observables, points on the unit circle for
gates: that is the whole distinction, and T0.M4.L4 will turn one into the other
with an exponential.

## The spectral theorem

**Statement.** Every Hermitian $A$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ has an orthonormal basis of
eigenvectors $\ket{v_1},\dots,\ket{v_n}$ with real eigenvalues, and

$$
A = \sum_k \lambda_k \ketbra{v_k}{v_k} .
$$

The right-hand side is a weighted sum of the projectors of T0.M4.L2, so a
Hermitian operator is literally a list of outcomes with a projector attached to
each. Verify the decomposition for the example:

$$
3\ketbra{+}{+} + 1\ketbra{-}{-}
= \tfrac32\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} + \half\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} = A .
$$

A complex example. Let

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1\end{pmatrix} = I + Y .
$$

It is Hermitian, since transposing gives $\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&i\\-i&1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ and conjugating returns $B$. Its characteristic
polynomial is

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda & -i \\ i & 1 - \lambda\end{pmatrix}
= (1-\lambda)^2 - (-i)(i) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda(\lambda - 2),
$$

so the eigenvalues are $0$ and $2$, both real as promised. For $\lambda = 2$ the
first row gives $-v_1 - iv_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = -iv_2$; taking $v_2 = i$ gives
$v_1 = 1$ and the normalised eigenvector

$$
\ket{y_+} = \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\ i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ket{y_-} = \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\ -i\end{pmatrix} \text{ for } \lambda = 0 .
$$

Since one eigenvalue is zero, the spectral sum has one surviving term:

$$
B = 2\ketbra{y_+}{y_+} = 2 \cdot \half \begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1 & -i\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}1 & -i \\ i & 1\end{pmatrix} .
$$

## The Paulis, decomposed

$$
Z = (+1)\ketbra{0}{0} + (-1)\ketbra{1}{1},
\qquad
X = (+1)\ketbra{+}{+} + (-1)\ketbra{-}{-},
$$

and $Y = (+1)\ketbra{y_+}{y_+} + (-1)\ketbra{y_-}{y_-}$. Each is Hermitian with
eigenvalues $\pm 1$, and each is also unitary, consistent with both rules at
once: $\pm 1$ are real and have modulus 1.

## Reading an eigenvalue off a machine

The spectral decomposition is how an expectation value is computed. For a
Hermitian $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ and projectors $P_k$,

$$
\expect{A} = \bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi} = \sum_k \lambda_k \bra{\psi}P_k\ket{\psi}
= \sum_k \lambda_k \, p_k ,
$$

so the average of $A$ is the average of its eigenvalues weighted by the
probability of landing in each eigenspace. Measuring $X$ means measuring in the
$\pm$ basis, which on hardware means applying $H$ and reading the computational
basis.

Take $\ket{\psi} = R_y(0.8)\ket{0} = \cos(0.4)\ket{0} + \sin(0.4)\ket{1} = 0.921061\ket{0} + 0.389418\ket{1}$.

Weighting the eigenvalues by those probabilities,

$$
\expect{X} = (+1)(0.858678) + (-1)(0.141322) = 0.717356 .
$$

Compare the direct sandwich: $\bra{\psi}X\ket{\psi} = 2\cos(0.4)\sin(0.4) = \sin(0.8) = 0.717356$. The two routes agree to six decimals, which is the
spectral theorem doing its job.

<Callout tone="note" title="Not every matrix is diagonalisable">
The spectral theorem needs $A\dg A = AA\dg$, the normality condition, which
Hermitian and unitary matrices both satisfy. The matrix
$\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ is not normal: its only
eigenvalue is 1 and it has just one eigenvector direction, so no basis of
eigenvectors exists. Nothing in quantum mechanics is built from such matrices,
which is why the theorem can be used so freely here.
</Callout>

<Check question="Find the eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors of $C = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&2\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\big)$, and write its spectral decomposition.">
$\det(C - \lambda I) = -\lambda(3-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 4 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda + 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $4$ and $-1$, both real
because $C$ is symmetric.

For $\lambda = 4$: $-4v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$ gives $v_2 = 2v_1$, so
$\ket{v_4} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1,2)^{T}$. For $\lambda = -1$:
$v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$ gives $\ket{v_{-1}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2,-1)^{T}$. The two are
orthogonal, as Hermiticity requires.

$$
C = 4 \cdot \tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}
  - 1 \cdot \tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}
  = \tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}0&10\\10&15\end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
</Check>
