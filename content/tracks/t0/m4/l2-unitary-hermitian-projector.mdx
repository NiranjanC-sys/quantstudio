Three classes of matrix carry the whole of quantum mechanics: unitaries are what
you may do to a state, Hermitian matrices are what you may measure, and
projectors are what a measurement outcome does. Each is defined by a one-line
equation in the adjoint, and the three definitions overlap in interesting ways.
Knowing which class a matrix belongs to tells you what it is allowed to mean.

## The three definitions

$$
U\dg U = U U\dg = I \quad \text{(unitary)},
\qquad
A\dg = A \quad \text{(Hermitian)},
\qquad
P^2 = P, \; P\dg = P \quad \text{(projector)} .
$$

The first says the inverse is the adjoint. The second says the matrix is its own
adjoint. The third says applying it twice is the same as applying it once, and
that it is Hermitian, which is what makes it an *orthogonal* projector rather
than a skewed one.

<Margin label="Real special cases">
Over the reals the dagger is just the transpose, so unitary becomes orthogonal
and Hermitian becomes symmetric. The rotation matrix of T0.M3.L2 is orthogonal;
the matrix $\big(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ is
symmetric. All the statements below specialise correctly.
</Margin>

## Testing concrete matrices

**Hadamard.** $H = \isqrt\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$
has real symmetric entries, so $H\dg = H$ and it is Hermitian. Squaring,

$$
H^2 = \half \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}
= \half\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = I,
$$

so $H\dg H = H^2 = I$ and it is unitary too. It is not a projector, since
$H^2 = I \ne H$.

**Pauli $Y$.** $Y = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{smallmatrix}\big)$.
Transposing swaps the off-diagonal entries to $i$ and $-i$, and conjugating
swaps them back, so $Y\dg = Y$. And $Y^2 = I$, so $Y$ is Hermitian and unitary.
All three Paulis share this: they are both observables and gates.

**$T$ gate.** $T = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})$. Its adjoint is
$\operatorname{diag}(1, e^{-i\pi/4}) \ne T$, so it is not Hermitian. But
$T\dg T = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{-i\pi/4}e^{i\pi/4}) = I$, so it is unitary.
Unitary without Hermitian is the common case.

**A non-example.** $N = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$
is neither: $N\dg = \big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)\ne N$,
and

$$
N\dg N = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} \ne I .
$$

No quantum circuit implements $N$, because it stretches some vectors and not
others.

## Unitaries preserve inner products

**Claim.** If $U\dg U = I$ then $\langle Uu, Uv\rangle = \langle u,v\rangle$ for
all $u, v$.

**Proof.** Using the reversal rule of the previous lesson,

$$
\langle Uu, Uv \rangle = (Uu)\dg (Uv) = u\dg U\dg U v = u\dg I v
= u\dg v = \langle u, v\rangle . \;\blacksquare
$$

Setting $v = u$ gives $\lVert Uu\rVert = \lVert u \rVert$, so normalisation
survives every gate and total probability stays 1. The converse also holds: a
matrix preserving all inner products must satisfy $U\dg U = I$, because
$u\dg(U\dg U - I)v = 0$ for all $u,v$ forces the bracketed matrix to be zero.
"Unitary" and "preserves lengths and angles" are the same condition.

Geometrically the columns of $U$ are an orthonormal basis, since
$\left(U\dg U\right)_{jk}$ is the inner product of columns $j$ and $k$. A gate
is a change of orthonormal basis, which is why T0.M3.L2 and this lesson are the
same subject twice.

## Rank-1 projectors

Given a unit vector $\ket{a}$, define $P = \ketbra{a}{a}$. The reversal rule
gives $P\dg = \left(\bra{a}\right)\dg\left(\ket{a}\right)\dg = \ketbra{a}{a} = P$, since daggering a ket produces a bra and vice versa. And

$$
P^2 = \ket{a}\braket{a}{a}\bra{a} = \ket{a}(1)\bra{a} = P ,
$$

which uses normalisation of $\ket{a}$ in an essential way. If $\ket{a}$ were not
a unit vector, $P^2 = \lVert a\rVert^2 P$ and idempotence fails.

Take $\ket{+}$:

$$
P_+ = \ketbra{+}{+} = \half\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P_+^2 = \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix} = P_+ .
$$

Its eigenvalues are 0 and 1, and its determinant is 0, so it is not invertible
and certainly not unitary. It also fails to preserve norms:
$P_+\ket{0} = (0.5, 0.5)^{T}$ has norm $0.707107$, not 1. That shortfall is not
a defect, it is the Born rule: the squared norm of $P_x\ket{\psi}$ is exactly
the probability of outcome $x$, here $0.707107^2 = 0.5$.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Projectors are not gates">
Because a projector shrinks vectors, no unitary circuit implements one. The only
way to apply a projector to a physical qubit is to measure and condition on the
outcome, which is why measurement is a separate primitive in the circuit model
and why it cannot be undone.
</Callout>

## Idempotence you can watch

Unitarity has a directly observable consequence: $H\dg = H$ and $H^2 = I$
together say that two Hadamards restore any state exactly.

The amplitude of $\ket{0}$ is exactly 1. Compare the classical fair coin of
T0.M1.L2, which is idempotent in the opposite sense, $F^2 = F$. That is no
coincidence: as a matrix, $F = \half\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ is precisely the projector $\ketbra{+}{+}$. Applying it
twice leaves you no better informed than once, because it has already thrown the
information away. $H$ keeps everything and gives it back.

## The three classes overlap

| Matrix | Hermitian | Unitary | Projector |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $I$ | yes | yes | yes |
| $X, Y, Z, H$ | yes | yes | no |
| $S, T$ | no | yes | no |
| $\ketbra{+}{+}$ | yes | no | yes |
| $\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ | no | no | no |

A matrix that is both unitary and a projector must satisfy $P^2 = P$ and
$P^{-1} = P\dg = P$, hence $P = I$. That is why the identity is the only entry
with three yeses.

<Check question="Is $M = \isqrt\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ unitary? Is it Hermitian? What is the projector onto $\ket{\psi} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1, 2)^{T}$?">
$M\dg = \isqrt\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{smallmatrix}\big) \ne M$,
so it is not Hermitian. For unitarity,

$$
M\dg M = \half\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}
= \half\begin{pmatrix}1 - i^2 & i - i\\ -i + i & -i^2 + 1\end{pmatrix} = I,
$$

so yes, it is unitary. It is exactly $R_x(-\pi/2) = \exp(i\pi X/4)$.

The projector is $\ketbra{\psi}{\psi} = \tfrac15\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$. Check idempotence: squaring gives
$\tfrac{1}{25}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}5&10\\10&20\end{smallmatrix}\big) = \tfrac15\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\big)$, as required.
</Check>
