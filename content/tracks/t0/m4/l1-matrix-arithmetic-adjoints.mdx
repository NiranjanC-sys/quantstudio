A circuit is a list of gates in an order, and running it is multiplying their
matrices in the reverse of that order. Two facts about matrix multiplication
therefore decide what a circuit can be: it is associative, so gates may be
grouped and fused into blocks without changing the result, and it is not
commutative, so the order can never be shuffled. Everything a compiler is
allowed to do to your circuit lives in the gap between those two sentences.

## The product

For $A$ of shape $m \times k$ and $B$ of shape $k \times n$, the product is the
$m \times n$ matrix

$$
(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{k} A_{i\ell} B_{\ell j} .
$$

Entry $(i,j)$ is row $i$ of $A$ paired against column $j$ of $B$. Work an
example that will not let you get away with sign errors:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 + i & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Entry by entry, using $i^2 = -1$:

$$
(AB)_{11} = 1 \cdot 0 + i(1+i) = i + i^2 = -1 + i,
\qquad
(AB)_{12} = 1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot 1 = 1 + i,
$$
$$
(AB)_{21} = 0 \cdot 0 + 2(1+i) = 2 + 2i,
\qquad
(AB)_{22} = 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 = 2 ,
$$

giving

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix} -1 + i & 1 + i \\ 2 + 2i & 2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 + i & 1 + i \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The second product is a different matrix, not a rearrangement of the first.

## Associative but not commutative

**Associativity.** $(AB)C = A(BC)$, because both sides have entry

$$
\sum_{\ell}\sum_{m} A_{i\ell} B_{\ell m} C_{mj},
$$

and the only difference between the two groupings is which sum is performed
first. Finite sums may be reordered, so the entries agree.

**Non-commutativity.** Nothing in the formula for $(AB)_{ij}$ is symmetric in
$A$ and $B$: the index $\ell$ runs along rows of $A$ and columns of $B$. Even
when both shapes allow both products, the two answers pair up different numbers.
The example above is one witness; the cleanest quantum witness is
$XZ = -ZX$, since

$$
XZ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
ZX = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

<Margin label="Why the order reverses">
A circuit that applies $U_1$, then $U_2$, then $U_3$ acts on a state as
$U_3 U_2 U_1 \ket{\psi}$. Diagrams read left to right in time, matrix products
read right to left in application order. Getting this backwards is the single
most common bug in hand-written simulator code.
</Margin>

## The adjoint

The **adjoint** (or conjugate transpose, or dagger) of $A$ is
$\left(A\dg\right)_{ij} = \overline{A_{ji}}$: transpose, then conjugate every
entry. For the matrices above,

$$
A\dg = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B\dg = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 - i \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The adjoint is what makes the inner product of T0.M3.L1 behave, because it is
defined by the property

$$
\langle A u, v \rangle = \langle u, A\dg v\rangle
$$

for all $u, v$. Reading that as bras and kets, $A\dg$ is the matrix that acts on
the bra side the way $A$ acts on the ket side.

## The product rule reverses

**Claim.** $(AB)\dg = B\dg A\dg$.

**Proof.** Compare entries. On the left,

$$
\left((AB)\dg\right)_{ij} = \overline{(AB)_{ji}}
= \overline{\sum_{\ell} A_{j\ell}B_{\ell i}}
= \sum_{\ell} \overline{A_{j\ell}}\; \overline{B_{\ell i}},
$$

using that conjugation is additive and multiplicative. On the right,

$$
\left(B\dg A\dg\right)_{ij} = \sum_{\ell} \left(B\dg\right)_{i\ell}\left(A\dg\right)_{\ell j}
= \sum_{\ell} \overline{B_{\ell i}}\; \overline{A_{j \ell}} .
$$

The two sums have the same terms, since complex multiplication commutes.
$\blacksquare$

Confirm on the example. Direct:

$$
(AB)\dg = \begin{pmatrix} -1 - i & 2 - 2i \\ 1 - i & 2\end{pmatrix} .
$$

Reversed product:

$$
B\dg A\dg = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1-i \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 2\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} (1-i)(-i) & 2(1-i) \\ 1 - i & 2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -1 - i & 2 - 2i \\ 1 - i & 2\end{pmatrix},
$$

using $(1-i)(-i) = -i + i^2 = -1 - i$. They match.

<Callout tone="note" title="Reversal is why inverting a circuit is easy">
For unitary gates $U\dg = U^{-1}$, so the inverse of a circuit
$U_n \cdots U_2 U_1$ is $U_1\dg U_2\dg \cdots U_n\dg$: reverse the gate list and
dagger each gate. Uncomputation in T0.M6.L3 and the diffusion operator in T2 are
both applications of exactly this.
</Callout>

## Order you can measure

Non-commutativity is not a bookkeeping detail, it is observable. Compare $H$
after $X$ against $X$ after $H$, both applied to $\ket{0}$, then rotate back
with a final $H$ and measure. The first circuit computes $HHX\ket{0} = X\ket{0} = \ket{1}$, the second computes $HXH\ket{0} = Z\ket{0} = \ket{0}$.

<Check question="Let $C = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ and $D = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\big)$. Compute $CD$, $DC$, $C\dg$ and $(CD)\dg$.">
$CD = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ and
$DC = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$. Both
products are projectors, and they are different ones, which is a strong form of
non-commutativity: here $CD$ and $DC$ do not even share a single non-zero entry.

$C\dg = D$, since transposing swaps the two off-diagonal positions and the
entries are real. Finally $(CD)\dg = CD$, because $CD = \ketbra{0}{0}$ is
Hermitian, and the reversal rule agrees:
$D\dg C\dg = C D$.
</Check>
