T0.M1.L4 found a two-bit distribution that could not be written as a product of
its own marginals, and called it correlated. T0.M5.L1 built the product
operation properly. Putting the two together gives the definition that the rest
of the curriculum turns on: a composite state is a product when it factorises,
and everything else is correlation, which in the quantum case gets the name
entanglement. The useful skill is deciding which case you are in from the
numbers alone.

## Independence is a tensor product

Two probabilistic bits with distributions $p = (p_0, p_1)$ and $q = (q_0, q_1)$,
prepared independently, have joint distribution

$$
p \otimes q = \begin{pmatrix} p_0 q_0 \\ p_0 q_1 \\ p_1 q_0 \\ p_1 q_1\end{pmatrix},
$$

which is the Kronecker product with the leading bit as the left factor. The
entries are the four products $p_x q_y$, which is exactly the independence
condition $p_{xy} = p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ written as one vector equation. Normalisation
survives automatically:

$$
\sum_{x,y} p_x q_y = \left(\sum_x p_x\right)\left(\sum_y q_y\right) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 .
$$

With $p = (0.75, 0.25)$ and $q = (0.40, 0.60)$,

$$
p \otimes q = (0.30,\; 0.45,\; 0.10,\; 0.15)^{T},
$$

the table from T0.M1.L4.

## The factorisation test

Given a length-4 distribution $(p_{00}, p_{01}, p_{10}, p_{11})$, is it a
product? Arrange it as a $2\times 2$ matrix and take the determinant.

**Claim.** With all marginals non-zero, the distribution factorises if and only
if

$$
p_{00}\,p_{11} - p_{01}\,p_{10} = 0 .
$$

**Proof.** If $p_{xy} = a_x b_y$ then
$p_{00}p_{11} = a_0b_0a_1b_1 = p_{01}p_{10}$, so the determinant vanishes.
Conversely, suppose it vanishes and set $a = (p_{00}+p_{01},\; p_{10}+p_{11})$
and $b = (p_{00}+p_{10},\; p_{01}+p_{11})$, the two marginals. Expanding
$a_0 b_0$ and using $p_{00}p_{11} = p_{01}p_{10}$ together with
$\sum p_{xy} = 1$ gives

$$
a_0 b_0 = (p_{00}+p_{01})(p_{00}+p_{10})
= p_{00}\big(p_{00}+p_{01}+p_{10}\big) + p_{01}p_{10}
= p_{00}\big(p_{00}+p_{01}+p_{10}+p_{11}\big) = p_{00},
$$

and the other three entries follow the same way. $\blacksquare$

Apply it. For $(0.30, 0.45, 0.10, 0.15)$ the two cross products are
$0.30 \times 0.15 = 0.045$ and $0.45 \times 0.10 = 0.045$: equal, so it
factorises, as we already knew. For the perfectly correlated
$(0.5, 0, 0, 0.5)$ they are $0.25$ and $0$: unequal, so no factorisation exists.
For the partially correlated $(0.4, 0.1, 0.1, 0.4)$ they are $0.16$ and $0.01$,
also unequal.

<Margin label="The same test on amplitudes">
For a pure two-qubit state $a\ket{00} + b\ket{01} + c\ket{10} + d\ket{11}$, the
product condition is $ad = bc$ on the amplitudes rather than the probabilities.
Failing it is the definition of an entangled state, and the quantity
$|ad - bc|$ is (up to a factor) the concurrence that T1.M5 uses to measure how
entangled it is.
</Margin>

## Products and non-products, side by side

Three qubits prepared independently, one with $H$, one with $R_y(\pi/3)$, one
with $X$, give a state that is a triple Kronecker product:

$$
\isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \otimes
\begin{pmatrix}0.866025\\0.5\end{pmatrix} \otimes
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} .
$$

The amplitude of $\ket{001}$ is $0.707107 \times 0.866025 \times 1 = 0.612372$
and that of $\ket{011}$ is $0.707107 \times 0.5 \times 1 = 0.353553$, both of
which the simulator reproduces. Every amplitude is a product of one number per
qubit, which is what "no correlation" means.

Now the contrast. A Hadamard followed by a CNOT produces amplitudes
$a = d = 0.707107$ and $b = c = 0$, so $ad = 0.5$ while $bc = 0$. The state does
not factorise, and no pair of single-qubit states can reproduce it.

## Counting parameters

Here is why the tensor product is the whole story of quantum hardness. A
classical distribution over $n$ bits has $2^n$ entries constrained by one
normalisation equation, so $2^n - 1$ free numbers. If the $n$ bits are
independent, you need one number each, so $n$. At $n = 10$ that is $1023$
against $10$.

A pure state of $n$ qubits is $2^n$ complex amplitudes, so $2^{n+1}$ real
numbers, less one for normalisation and one for the unobservable global phase:
$2^{n+1} - 2$. A product state needs 2 real parameters per qubit, so $2n$. At
$n = 10$: $2046$ against $20$.

| $n$ | product states | general states |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 | 6 |
| 3 | 6 | 14 |
| 10 | 20 | 2046 |

<Callout tone="note" title="Where the gap comes from">
At $n = 1$ the two columns agree, because a single system cannot be correlated
with anything. At $n=2$ the gap is exactly 2, and those two parameters are what
a Bell state uses. The gap grows exponentially, and simulating a general state
on a classical computer means storing all $2^{n+1}-2$ of those numbers.
</Callout>

<Check question="Does $(0.24, 0.36, 0.16, 0.24)$ factorise? If so, into which two distributions?">
Cross products: $0.24 \times 0.24 = 0.0576$ and $0.36 \times 0.16 = 0.0576$.
They are equal, so it factorises.

The marginals are $p_X = (0.24 + 0.36,\; 0.16 + 0.24) = (0.6, 0.4)$ and
$p_Y = (0.24 + 0.16,\; 0.36 + 0.24) = (0.4, 0.6)$. Their Kronecker product is
$(0.24, 0.36, 0.16, 0.24)$, matching entry for entry.
</Check>
