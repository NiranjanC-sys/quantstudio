A quantum computer never hands you a probability. It hands you counts, and you
divide. Every bar in every histogram in this field is an estimate with an error
that nobody drew, and the difference between two bars is routinely smaller than
the error on either of them. Knowing how big that error is turns a picture into
a measurement.

## Where the error comes from

Fix a circuit and an outcome $x$ with true probability $p$. Running $N$ shots
and counting how many land on $x$ is $N$ independent Bernoulli trials, so the
count $k$ is binomial with mean $Np$ and variance $Np(1-p)$. The estimator
$\hat p = k/N$ therefore has

$$
\expect{\hat p} = p,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{N},
\qquad
\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} .
$$

The estimate is unbiased and its error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$. That square
root is brutal: a tenfold improvement in precision costs a hundredfold more
shots.

Since $p$ is unknown, plug in $\hat p$:

```python
import numpy as np

def std_error(counts, N):
    p = np.asarray(counts) / N
    return np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / N)
```

Some concrete values, with the interval that covers the truth 95 percent of the
time being roughly $\hat p \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$:

| $p$ | $N$ | SE | 95 percent half-width |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.5$ | $1{,}000$ | $0.015811$ | $0.030990$ |
| $0.5$ | $8{,}192$ | $0.005524$ | $0.010828$ |
| $0.75$ | $1{,}000$ | $0.013693$ | $0.026838$ |

<Margin label="Worst case is a fair coin">
$p(1-p)$ is maximised at $p = 0.5$, so an unbiased outcome is the hardest to
pin down and $\mathrm{SE} \le 1/(2\sqrt N)$ always. At $N = 8192$, the default
shot count on much IBM hardware, no probability estimate is better than about
$0.0055$.
</Margin>

## Sizing the shot budget

Turn the formula around. To resolve a difference $\Delta$ between your estimate
and a reference value at $z$ standard errors, you need

$$
N \ge \frac{z^2\,p(1-p)}{\Delta^2}.
$$

Suppose you want to distinguish $p = 0.50$ from $p = 0.52$ at two standard
errors. With $\Delta = 0.02$, $z = 2$ and the worst-case $p(1-p) = 0.25$,

$$
N \ge \frac{4 \times 0.25}{0.0004} = 2500 .
$$

Comparing two *measured* probabilities rather than one against a constant costs
more, because both carry error: the standard error of the difference is
$\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_1^2 + \mathrm{SE}_2^2}$, which for equal $N$ is $\sqrt2$
times one of them, so the shot count doubles.

## Drawing it

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

labels = list(counts)
p = np.array([counts[k] for k in labels]) / N
err = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / N)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.bar(labels, p, yerr=1.96 * err, capsize=4)
ax.set_ylabel("probability")
ax.set_xlabel("outcome")
```

Two rules for these plots. Always label the error bars with what they mean, one
standard error or a 95 percent interval, because readers cannot tell them apart
by looking. And never start the $y$ axis anywhere but zero: a truncated axis can
make a difference of one standard error look decisive.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The formula breaks at the edges">
If an outcome never occurs, $\hat p = 0$ and the formula returns
$\mathrm{SE} = 0$, claiming certainty from no evidence at all. The rule of
three is the fix: seeing 0 occurrences in $N$ shots means the true probability
is below roughly $3/N$ with 95 percent confidence, so $N = 1000$ gives an upper
bound of $0.003$, not a proof of impossibility. For serious work near 0 or 1,
use a Wilson interval instead of the normal approximation.
</Callout>

## Why the picture without bars is not evidence

A histogram of raw counts invites the reader to compare bar heights, and the eye
is very good at seeing differences of a few pixels that are well inside the
noise. Take a four-outcome distribution near uniform on 1024 shots. Each bar has
$\mathrm{SE} = 0.013532$, so the difference of two bars has standard error
$\sqrt2 \times 0.013532 = 0.019137$. Two outcomes separated by 30 counts differ
by $0.029297$ in probability, which is $1.53$ standard errors: below the usual
two-sigma threshold, and therefore not a result. You would need a gap of about
39 counts before that plot said anything.
This matters most in exactly the places quantum results are reported: comparing
a noisy device against a simulator, or a mitigated result against an unmitigated
one, where the effect sizes are genuinely small.

<Check question="You measure an outcome 412 times in 1024 shots. Report the probability with a 95 percent interval, and say how many shots you would need to halve the interval.">
$\hat p = 412/1024 = 0.402344$. The standard error is

$$
\sqrt{\frac{0.402344 \times 0.597656}{1024}} = 0.015324,
$$

so the 95 percent interval is $0.402344 \pm 1.96 \times 0.015324$, that is
$0.402344 \pm 0.030035$, or roughly $0.372$ to $0.432$.

Halving the interval means halving the standard error, and since it scales as
$1/\sqrt N$ that costs four times the shots: $4096$ instead of $1024$.
</Check>
