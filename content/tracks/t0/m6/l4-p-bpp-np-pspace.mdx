"Quantum computers are faster" is a claim about complexity classes, and it is
routinely stated against the wrong baseline. The honest comparison is not
against what a deterministic algorithm can do, because classical computers have
had access to randomness for as long as they have existed. Getting the four
classical classes straight is what lets you read a speedup claim and know what
was actually claimed.

## Four definitions

Fix a decision problem: an infinite family of yes-or-no questions, one per input
string $x$, with $n = |x|$.

**P.** There is a deterministic algorithm that answers correctly on every input,
running in time polynomial in $n$. This is the usual informal meaning of
"efficiently solvable".

**BPP.** Bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time. There is a randomised
algorithm running in polynomial time that answers correctly with probability at
least $2/3$ on every input. The constant $2/3$ is arbitrary, as the next section
shows.

**NP.** There is a polynomial-time deterministic *verifier* and, for every yes
instance, a certificate of polynomial length that the verifier accepts. For a no
instance, no certificate is accepted. NP is about checking, not finding.

**PSPACE.** There is a deterministic algorithm using memory polynomial in $n$,
with no limit on time.

<Margin label="Deciding versus computing">
All four classes are about decision problems, which makes them comparable. A
search problem like factoring is turned into a decision problem in the usual
way, for instance "does $N$ have a factor less than $k$", and a polynomial
number of such questions recovers the factorisation by binary search.
</Margin>

## Why the constant in BPP does not matter

An algorithm that is right with probability $2/3$ can be turned into one that is
right with probability $1 - \varepsilon$ for any $\varepsilon$ you name, by
running it many times and taking the majority answer. Each run is an independent
biased coin, so the probability the majority is wrong is a binomial tail:

| runs | probability the majority is wrong |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.333333$ |
| 3 | $0.259259$ |
| 11 | $0.122085$ |
| 101 | $0.000272$ |

The three-run figure is worth doing by hand. The majority of 3 is wrong when 0
or 1 runs are correct:

$$
\left(\tfrac13\right)^3 + 3\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\left(\tfrac13\right)^2
= \tfrac{1}{27} + \tfrac{6}{27} = \tfrac{7}{27} = 0.259259 .
$$

The tail shrinks exponentially in the number of runs, by the Chernoff bound, so
polynomially many repetitions drive the error below $2^{-n}$. That is why BPP
with threshold $2/3$, $0.51$ or $1 - 2^{-n}$ are the same class.

A single-shot machine with success probability exactly $2/3$ is easy to build.

## The containments that are actually proved

$$
\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{BPP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE},
\qquad
\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE} .
$$

Each is easy to see. A deterministic algorithm is a randomised one that ignores
its coins, giving the first. A randomised polynomial-time algorithm can be
simulated in polynomial space by trying every coin sequence one at a time and
counting, reusing the same memory, giving the second. A verifier can be run on
every candidate certificate the same way, giving the fourth.

What is *not* known is any separation among them. Both of these are open:

$$
\mathrm{P} \stackrel{?}{=} \mathrm{NP},
\qquad
\mathrm{P} \stackrel{?}{=} \mathrm{PSPACE} .
$$

Most researchers expect that $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{BPP}$, on the strength of
derandomisation results
that trade hardness assumptions for pseudorandom generators. BPP and NP are not
known to be comparable in either direction.

Only one separation in the neighbourhood is unconditional: the time hierarchy
theorem gives $\mathrm{P} \subsetneq \mathrm{EXP}$, and since
$\mathrm{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathrm{EXP}$, at least one of the containments in
the chain from P to EXP is strict. Nobody knows which.

## Where BQP sits

BQP is the quantum analogue of BPP: polynomial-size quantum circuits, correct
with probability at least $2/3$. The proved relations are

$$
\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{BPP} \subseteq \mathrm{BQP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE}.
$$

The first quantum containment holds because a quantum circuit can simulate coin
flips with Hadamards, as the figure above does. The last holds because the
amplitude of any basis state is a sum over exponentially many paths, and that
sum can be accumulated one path at a time in polynomial space.

This is why BPP, not P, is the baseline. A quantum algorithm that beats the best
known deterministic algorithm but ties the best known randomised one has
demonstrated nothing about quantum advantage. It is also why no proof of
quantum advantage exists: separating BQP from BPP would separate P from PSPACE,
which is a famous open problem.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Speedup claims to read carefully">
Shor's algorithm is not proved to be faster than every classical factoring
algorithm; it is faster than every *known* one, and factoring is not known to be
outside BPP. Grover's algorithm gives a proved quadratic speedup, but only in
the black-box query model, where the input is an oracle rather than a circuit.
The catalogue in T2 labels every algorithm with which of these two kinds of
claim it makes.
</Callout>

<Check question="A randomised algorithm answers correctly with probability $0.9$. How does the majority of 3 runs compare, and what is the general shape of the improvement?">
The majority of 3 is wrong when 0 or 1 runs are correct:

$$
(0.1)^3 + 3(0.9)(0.1)^2 = 0.001 + 0.027 = 0.028 .
$$

So the error drops from $0.1$ to $0.028$, better than a factor of 3. In general
the error after $k$ runs decays like $e^{-ck}$ for a constant $c$ depending on
the gap between the success probability and $1/2$, which is the Chernoff bound.
The closer the single-run probability is to $1/2$, the smaller $c$ is and the
more repetitions you need.
</Check>
