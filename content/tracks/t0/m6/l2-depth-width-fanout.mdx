Two circuits computing the same function can differ by orders of magnitude in
how long they take and how much hardware they occupy. Counting gates measures
one of those and not the other, because gates on independent wires run at the
same time. The two numbers that matter are depth and width, and on quantum
hardware depth is the one that kills you, because every layer of gates is more
time for the state to decohere.

## Three cost measures

For a circuit written as a directed acyclic graph of gates:

- **Size** is the number of gates. It is the total work.
- **Depth** is the number of gates on the longest path from an input to an
  output. It is the time, assuming unlimited parallelism.
- **Width** is the maximum number of wires alive at any one point. It is the
  memory, and on quantum hardware it is the qubit count.

Consider computing $a_1 \land a_2 \land a_3 \land a_4$ with 2-input ANDs. The
left-associated chain has size 3 and depth 3. The balanced tree has size 3 and
depth 2. Same gate count, one fewer layer, and the gap grows: for $n$ inputs the
chain has depth $n-1$ while the tree has depth $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$.

<Margin label="Depth is not fungible with size">
A circuit of size $s$ has depth at most $s$ and at least $\log_2 s$ when gates
have bounded fan-in, because each gate can at most double the number of inputs
influencing a wire. Optimising for one measure routinely worsens the other, so a
compiler always needs to know which one you are paying for.
</Margin>

## Fan-out

A classical wire can be split and fed to as many gates as you like. Physically
that split has a cost, and if each buffer drives at most 2 loads, delivering one
bit to $n$ places needs a tree of $n - 1$ buffers and depth
$\lceil \log_2 n\rceil$. For $n = 8$ that is 7 buffers and 3 levels.

Quantum circuits cannot do this at all for unknown states. The no-cloning
theorem, proved in T1.M5, says no unitary $U$ satisfies
$U(\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{0}) = \ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\psi}$ for every
$\ket{\psi}$. The CNOT gate looks like it should: it maps $\ket{00}$ to
$\ket{00}$ and $\ket{10}$ to $\ket{11}$, copying a classical bit perfectly. Feed
it a superposition and watch what happens instead.

The result has amplitude $0.707107$ on $\ket{00}$ and on $\ket{11}$, and zero
elsewhere. Two genuine copies of $\ket{+}$ would be the product state
$\ket{+}\ket{+}$, which has amplitude $0.5$ on all four basis states, as the
$H \otimes H$ figure in T0.M5.L1 shows. CNOT produced entanglement where you
asked for duplication. Fan-out, free in every classical design, is simply not
available.

## Adders, two ways

The classic depth-versus-size trade lives in addition. A **full adder** takes
$a$, $b$ and a carry-in and produces

$$
s = a \oplus b \oplus c_{\text{in}},
\qquad
c_{\text{out}} = (a \land b) \lor \big(c_{\text{in}} \land (a \oplus b)\big).
$$

With 2-input gates that is two XORs, two ANDs and one OR: 5 gates. The path from
$c_{\text{in}}$ to $c_{\text{out}}$ passes an AND and then an OR, so 2 gate
delays.

A **ripple-carry adder** chains $n$ of these. Size is $5n$ gates and the carry
must propagate through every stage, giving depth about $2n$. For $n = 4$ that is
20 gates and carry depth 8; for $n = 64$ it is 320 gates and carry depth 128.
Cheap in hardware, and linear in time.

A **carry-lookahead adder** computes the carries in parallel instead. Each
position generates a carry when $g_i = a_i \land b_i$ and propagates one when
$p_i = a_i \oplus b_i$, and the carry into position $i$ is a prefix computation
over those pairs. Prefix sums over an associative operator can be done in
$\Theta(\log n)$ depth using $\Theta(n \log n)$ cells. For $n = 64$ that is
6 prefix levels instead of a 128-deep carry chain, bought with several times as
many gates.

| adder | size | depth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ripple-carry, $n$ bits | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ |
| carry-lookahead, $n$ bits | $\Theta(n \log n)$ | $\Theta(\log n)$ |

<Callout tone="note" title="The same trade appears in T2">
Quantum arithmetic faces exactly this choice, with an extra constraint: the
lookahead version needs ancilla qubits to hold the intermediate prefixes, so
buying depth costs width as well as size. Resource estimates for Shor's
algorithm are dominated by which adder you pick.
</Callout>

## Reading the numbers off a circuit

The lesson figures on this platform report qubits, depth and gate count in the
footer of every panel. Depth there is computed the standard way: a gate goes in
the earliest layer after every gate it depends on, so gates on disjoint qubits
share a layer. The two-qubit-gate count is reported separately because on real
hardware a two-qubit gate is roughly an order of magnitude noisier and slower
than a one-qubit gate, so it is the number that actually predicts fidelity.

Three gates, three qubits, depth 1. Chain the same three gates onto one qubit
and the depth becomes 3 while the width drops to 1. Nothing about the gate list
tells you which you have; only the dependency structure does.

<Check question="A circuit computes the parity of 16 bits using 2-input XOR gates. What are its minimum size and minimum depth, and what is its width if it is built as a balanced tree?">
Parity of $n$ bits needs $n - 1$ two-input XORs, so 15 gates, and no fewer:
each gate reduces the count of live values by one, from 16 to 1.

Minimum depth is $\lceil \log_2 16 \rceil = 4$, achieved by the balanced tree.
The chain would also use 15 gates but have depth 15.

Width at the widest point is 16, the inputs themselves. The tree halves the live
count each level: 16, then 8, 4, 2, 1.
</Check>
