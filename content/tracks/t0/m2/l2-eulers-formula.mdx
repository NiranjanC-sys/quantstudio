Multiplying complex numbers multiplies moduli and adds arguments. Addition in
the exponent, multiplication outside: that is the signature of the exponential
function, and it is a strong hint that $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is secretly
$e^{\text{something}}$. It is, the something is $i\theta$, and once you believe
it every phase in the rest of this curriculum becomes a single symbol you can
multiply and differentiate without thinking about triangles.

## The three series

Start from the real power series, each convergent for every argument:

$$
e^{x} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!}, \qquad
\cos x = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^m x^{2m}}{(2m)!}, \qquad
\sin x = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^m x^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!} .
$$

Define $e^{z}$ for complex $z$ by the same series. That is a definition, not a
theorem, and it is the right one because the series converges absolutely for
every complex $z$, so terms may be reordered freely.

Now substitute $z = i\theta$ with $\theta$ real. The only new ingredient is that
powers of $i$ cycle with period four:

$$
i^0 = 1, \quad i^1 = i, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad i^3 = -i, \quad i^4 = 1, \dots
$$

so $i^{2m} = (-1)^m$ and $i^{2m+1} = (-1)^m i$. Split the sum into even and odd
$k$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{(i\theta)^k}{k!}
= \sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{i^{2m}\theta^{2m}}{(2m)!}
  + \sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{i^{2m+1}\theta^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!} .
$$

Substituting the powers of $i$,

$$
e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{\sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{(-1)^m \theta^{2m}}{(2m)!}}_{\cos\theta}
 \;+\; i \underbrace{\sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{(-1)^m \theta^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}}_{\sin\theta}
 \;=\; \cos\theta + i \sin\theta .
$$

That is **Euler's formula**.

<Margin label="A proof without series">
Let $f(\theta) = e^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta + i \sin\theta)$. Differentiating,
$f'(\theta) = e^{-i\theta}\big[-i(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) + (-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta)\big] = 0$. So $f$ is constant, and $f(0) = 1$, giving
$\cos\theta + i\sin\theta = e^{i\theta}$. Shorter, but it assumes the derivative
rule for $e^{i\theta}$ that the series proves.
</Margin>

Truncating the series is a fair numerical check. Summing $\sum_{k<30} (i)^k/k!$ at $\theta = 1$ gives $0.540302 + 0.841471 i$, and
$\cos 1 = 0.540302$, $\sin 1 = 0.841471$.

## Immediate consequences

Setting $\theta$ to the obvious angles:

$$
e^{i\pi/2} = i, \qquad e^{i\pi} = -1, \qquad e^{3i\pi/2} = -i, \qquad
e^{2\pi i} = 1 .
$$

The modulus is always 1, because

$$
\left|e^{i\theta}\right|^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 .
$$

So $\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta}$ traces the unit circle, at unit speed, starting
at 1 and going anticlockwise. It is $2\pi$-periodic, which is the same
statement as the argument being defined only modulo $2\pi$.

Conjugation flips the angle: $\overline{e^{i\theta}} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta = e^{-i\theta}$. Combined with $z\bar z = |z|^2$, this gives the tidy inverse
$1/e^{i\theta} = e^{-i\theta}$.

Any non-zero complex number now has the compact **polar form**

$$
z = r e^{i\theta}, \qquad r = |z| > 0, \quad \theta = \arg z .
$$

For example $2 + 2i$ has $r = 2\sqrt2 = 2.828427$ and $\theta = \pi/4$, so
$2 + 2i = 2\sqrt{2}\, e^{i\pi/4}$. Going the other way,
$e^{i\pi/4} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(1 + i) = 0.707107 + 0.707107 i$.

## Multiplication is rotation

In polar form the product rule of the previous lesson becomes a single
exponent law:

$$
\left(r_1 e^{i\theta_1}\right)\left(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}\right)
= r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)} .
$$

Fix $r_1 = 1$. Then multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$ leaves every modulus alone
and adds $\theta$ to every argument: it is a rotation of the plane about the
origin by angle $\theta$. Writing $z = x + iy$ as the real vector
$(x, y)^{T}$ and expanding
$e^{i\theta}(x+iy) = (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta) + i(x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta)$
shows the same map as a matrix,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix},
$$

the standard rotation matrix. The complex numbers are the plane with rotation
built into the arithmetic, which is why they are the natural home for
amplitudes.

Repeated multiplication gives **de Moivre's theorem**:
$\left(e^{i\theta}\right)^n = e^{in\theta}$, so
$(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta$. That single line
replaces a page of trigonometric identities and is the tool the next lesson uses
on roots of unity.

## Phases you can measure

The gate $P(\lambda) = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\lambda})$ multiplies the
amplitude of $\ket{1}$ by a point on the unit circle and leaves $\ket{0}$ alone.
The $T$ gate is $P(\pi/4)$. Applied after a Hadamard, it turns the state
$\isqrt(\ket{0} + \ket{1})$ into $\isqrt\left(\ket{0} + e^{i\pi/4}\ket{1}\right)$,
so the second amplitude should be $0.707107 \times (0.707107 + 0.707107 i) = 0.5 + 0.5i$.

Both amplitudes still have modulus $0.707107$, so both outcomes remain equally
likely. What changed is an angle, and on the Bloch sphere view the state vector
has swung $45$ degrees around the equator. The unit circle in the complex plane
and the equator of the Bloch sphere are the same circle, seen twice.

<Callout tone="note" title="Relative phase is the only kind that matters">
Multiplying the *whole* state by $e^{i\varphi}$ changes no probability in any
basis and no expectation value of any observable, so it is unobservable and
called a global phase. Multiplying only one amplitude, as $T$ does, is a
relative phase, and it is physical. T1.M1 makes this precise; the arithmetic
you need for it is on this page.
</Callout>

<Check question="Write $-3$ and $-2i$ in the form $re^{i\theta}$ with the principal argument, then use exponentials to compute $(-2i)^3$.">
$-3 = 3e^{i\pi}$, since the modulus is 3 and the point lies on the negative real
axis. $-2i = 2e^{-i\pi/2}$, modulus 2, pointing straight down, and the principal
argument convention $(-\pi, \pi]$ prefers $-\pi/2$ to $3\pi/2$.

Then $(-2i)^3 = \left(2e^{-i\pi/2}\right)^3 = 8 e^{-3i\pi/2} = 8 e^{i\pi/2} = 8i$, using $2\pi$-periodicity to bring the angle back into range. Direct
expansion agrees: $(-2i)^3 = -8i^3 = -8(-i) = 8i$.
</Check>
