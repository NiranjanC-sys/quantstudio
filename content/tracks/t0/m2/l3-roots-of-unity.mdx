How many solutions does $z^N = 1$ have? Over the reals the answer is one or two
and it depends awkwardly on the parity of $N$. Over the complex numbers it is
always exactly $N$, they are evenly spaced around the unit circle, and they sum
to zero. That last fact is not decoration: it is the cancellation that makes the
quantum Fourier transform work, and every phase-estimation argument in T2 comes
back to it.

## Solving the equation

Write $z = re^{i\theta}$. Then $z^N = r^N e^{iN\theta}$, and this equals
$1 = 1 \cdot e^{i \cdot 0}$ exactly when the moduli match and the arguments
match modulo $2\pi$:

$$
r^N = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 1, \qquad
N\theta \equiv 0 \pmod{2\pi} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = \frac{2\pi k}{N},
\; k \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

The angles $2\pi k/N$ repeat with period $N$ in $k$, so there are exactly $N$
distinct solutions. Name the first one after zero:

$$
\omega = \omega_N = e^{2\pi i / N},
\qquad \text{roots} = \left\{ 1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{N-1} \right\}.
$$

<Margin label="Two useful reflexes">
$\omega^{-1} = \omega^{N-1} = \overline{\omega}$, because inverting a
unit-modulus number is conjugating it. And $\omega^{j}$ depends only on
$j \bmod N$, so exponents on $\omega$ live in $\mathbb{Z}_N$, not $\mathbb{Z}$.
</Margin>

They are the vertices of a regular $N$-gon inscribed in the unit circle, with
one vertex nailed to $1$.

| $N$ | roots |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | $1,\; -1$ |
| 3 | $1,\; -0.5 + 0.866025i,\; -0.5 - 0.866025i$ |
| 4 | $1,\; i,\; -1,\; -i$ |
| 6 | $1,\; 0.5 + 0.866025i,\; -0.5 + 0.866025i,\; -1,\; -0.5 - 0.866025i,\; 0.5 - 0.866025i$ |

The value $0.866025$ is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sin(2\pi/3)$, and the $N = 6$ list
contains the $N = 3$ and $N = 2$ lists inside it, because a sixth root of unity
raised to the sixth power is 1 and so is a cube root raised to the sixth. In
general the $d$-th roots of unity sit inside the $N$-th roots whenever $d$
divides $N$.

## They sum to zero

**Claim.** For $N \ge 2$, $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \omega^{k} = 0$.

**Proof.** This is a geometric series with ratio $\omega \ne 1$, so the standard
formula applies:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \omega^{k} = \frac{\omega^{N} - 1}{\omega - 1}.
$$

But $\omega^N = e^{2\pi i} = 1$, so the numerator is 0 while the denominator is
not, and the sum is 0. $\blacksquare$

Sanity check at $N = 4$: $1 + i + (-1) + (-i) = 0$. At $N = 3$:
$1 + (-0.5 + 0.866025i) + (-0.5 - 0.866025i) = 0$, the imaginary parts cancelling
in pairs and the real parts summing to $1 - 1 = 0$.

There is a proof without algebra worth carrying in your head. The roots are the
vertices of a regular polygon centred at the origin, and the centroid of a
regular polygon is its centre. The sum is $N$ times the centroid, hence zero.
Symmetry, not cancellation of digits.

## The general orthogonality sum

Almost every use of roots of unity in this curriculum is one identity:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \omega^{jk} =
\begin{cases}
N, & j \equiv 0 \pmod N, \\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

**Proof.** If $j \equiv 0 \pmod N$ then $\omega^{j} = 1$ and every term is 1,
giving $N$. Otherwise $\omega^{j} \ne 1$, and the same geometric formula applies
with ratio $\omega^{j}$:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left(\omega^{j}\right)^{k}
= \frac{\left(\omega^{j}\right)^{N} - 1}{\omega^{j} - 1}
= \frac{\left(\omega^{N}\right)^{j} - 1}{\omega^{j} - 1}
= \frac{1 - 1}{\omega^{j} - 1} = 0 . \;\blacksquare
$$

Read it as an interference statement. Each term $\omega^{jk}$ is a unit vector
in the plane; the sum either has every arrow pointing the same way, giving
maximal constructive interference of size $N$, or the arrows are spread evenly
around the circle and cancel exactly. There is no intermediate case. The Fourier
transform is the machine that arranges for the phases you want to be in the
first case and everything else in the second.

Partial sums do not vanish, and the same formula gives them. With $N = 4$,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{2} i^{k} = \frac{i^3 - 1}{i - 1} = \frac{-1 - i}{-1 + i} = i,
$$

which you can confirm directly: $1 + i - 1 = i$.

## Roots of unity as gates

The gate $P(\lambda) = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\lambda})$ with
$\lambda = 2\pi/N$ multiplies the $\ket{1}$ amplitude by $\omega_N$. Applying it
$N$ times multiplies by $\omega_N^{N} = 1$, so the circuit is the identity: the
gate has order exactly $N$. Take $N = 3$, so $\lambda = 2\pi/3 = 2.094395$.

$0.707107 \times (-0.5 + 0.866025i) = 0.707107\,\omega_3$, exactly the second
cube root of unity scaled by $\isqrt$. Now apply the gate three times.

of unity applied in succession, and their product returned to the start.

<Callout tone="note" title="Where you will meet these again">
The $N$-point quantum Fourier transform has matrix entries
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt N}\omega_N^{jk}$, and it is unitary precisely because of the
orthogonality sum above. Phase estimation reads an eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i \phi}$
by arranging for the wrong answers to be spread around the circle and cancel.
Both live in T2.
</Callout>

<Check question="List the fifth roots of unity in exponential form, then evaluate $\sum_{k=0}^{4}\omega_5^{2k}$ and $\sum_{k=0}^{4}\omega_5^{5k}$.">
The fifth roots are $e^{2\pi i k/5}$ for $k = 0,1,2,3,4$, that is
$1, e^{2\pi i/5}, e^{4\pi i/5}, e^{6\pi i/5}, e^{8\pi i/5}$, evenly spaced every
$72$ degrees.

For the first sum, $j = 2$ is not a multiple of 5, so the orthogonality identity
gives 0. Concretely the exponents $2k \bmod 5$ run through $0, 2, 4, 1, 3$, a
permutation of all five roots, and those sum to zero.

For the second sum, $j = 5 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, so every term is
$\omega_5^{5k} = 1$ and the sum is $5$.
</Check>
