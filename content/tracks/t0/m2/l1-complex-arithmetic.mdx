T0.M1 ended on a minus sign. A stochastic matrix cannot produce one, a unitary
can, and that single difference turned an idempotent fair coin into a gate that
undoes itself. So the amplitudes a qubit carries are not probabilities, they are
signed. Why stop at signed, and why is the answer the complex numbers rather
than the reals with a sign attached? Because the operation you actually need is
*rotation of a plane*, and $\mathbb{C}$ is precisely the number system in which
rotation is multiplication.

## Rectangular form and the one new rule

A complex number is a pair of reals written $z = a + bi$, with $a = \operatorname{Re} z$ and $b = \operatorname{Im} z$. Addition is componentwise.
Multiplication is ordinary expansion plus one substitution, $i^2 = -1$:

$$
(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i .
$$

Take $z = 3 + 4i$ and $w = 1 - 2i$:

$$
zw = (3 \cdot 1 - 4 \cdot (-2)) + (3 \cdot (-2) + 4 \cdot 1)i
   = (3 + 8) + (-6 + 4)i = 11 - 2i .
$$

<Margin label="Why not just signs">
Signed reals give you two rotations of the line, by $0$ and by $\pi$. The
gates in T1.M3 need every angle in between, and a general rotation of the plane
is exactly what multiplication by a unit-modulus complex number does. The
minus sign is the special case $\theta = \pi$.
</Margin>

## Conjugate, modulus, and division

The **conjugate** $\bar{z} = a - bi$ flips the sign of the imaginary part. It is
useful because the product of a number with its own conjugate is real and
non-negative:

$$
z\bar{z} = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 + b^2 .
$$

Define the **modulus** $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, the distance from the origin in
the plane. Then $z \bar z = |z|^2$, always. For our two numbers,
$|z| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ and $|w| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5} = 2.236068$.

Division follows immediately: to divide by $w$, multiply top and bottom by
$\bar w$ and the denominator becomes real.

$$
\frac{z}{w} = \frac{(3+4i)(1+2i)}{(1-2i)(1+2i)}
= \frac{3 + 6i + 4i + 8i^2}{1 + 4}
= \frac{-5 + 10i}{5}
= -1 + 2i .
$$

Check by multiplying back: $(-1+2i)(1-2i) = -1 + 2i + 2i - 4i^2 = 3 + 4i$. It
is $z$.

## Polar form: modulus and argument

Every non-zero $z$ is determined by how far it is from the origin and in which
direction. The direction is the **argument** $\arg z$, the angle from the
positive real axis, computed with the two-argument arctangent so that the
quadrant is right:

$$
\arg z = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a) .
$$

For $z = 3 + 4i$, $\arg z = \operatorname{atan2}(4,3) = 0.927295$ rad, which is
$53.130102$ degrees. For $w = 1 - 2i$, the point is in the fourth quadrant and
$\arg w = -1.107149$ rad, or $-63.434949$ degrees.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="atan2, not atan">
$\arctan(b/a)$ loses the quadrant: it gives the same answer for $1+i$ and
$-1-i$, which point in opposite directions. Every language has a two-argument
version (`atan2`, `np.angle`, `Math.atan2`); use it. The argument is also only
defined up to adding $2\pi$, and conventions differ on whether the principal
value lives in $(-\pi, \pi]$ or $[0, 2\pi)$. NumPy and this curriculum use
$(-\pi, \pi]$.
</Callout>

With $r = |z|$ and $\theta = \arg z$, the polar form is
$z = r(\cos\theta + i \sin\theta)$. Multiplying two numbers in polar form uses
the angle-addition identities:

$$
r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1) \cdot r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i \sin\theta_2)
= r_1 r_2 \big[\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) + i \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\big].
$$

Moduli multiply, arguments add. Check it on our example: $\arg z + \arg w = 0.927295 - 1.107149 = -0.179853$, and $\arg(11 - 2i) = \operatorname{atan2}(-2, 11) = -0.179853$. They agree to every digit shown.

## The multiplicative property of the modulus

The claim $|zw| = |z||w|$ deserves a proof, because everything about
normalisation in T1 depends on it.

**Proof.** Conjugation distributes over products, $\overline{zw} = \bar z \bar w$, which you can check by expanding both sides. Then

$$
|zw|^2 = (zw)\overline{(zw)} = z w \bar z \bar w = (z \bar z)(w \bar w)
= |z|^2 |w|^2 ,
$$

using commutativity to reorder. Both $|zw|$ and $|z||w|$ are non-negative reals
with equal squares, so they are equal. $\blacksquare$

Expanded in coordinates the same statement is a classical identity,

$$
(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2 ,
$$

which is worth expanding once by hand: the cross terms $-2abcd$ and $+2abcd$
cancel, leaving $a^2c^2 + b^2d^2 + a^2d^2 + b^2c^2$, which factors as claimed.
Numerically, $|zw| = \sqrt{121 + 4} = \sqrt{125} = 11.180340$ and
$|z||w| = 5\sqrt{5} = 11.180340$.

## What this buys a qubit

A qubit state is $\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}$ with $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ and $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. Measurement probabilities are
squared moduli, so multiplying an amplitude by any number of modulus 1 leaves
every probability untouched while changing the state. The $S$ gate does exactly
that to the $\ket{1}$ amplitude, multiplying it by $i$.

Both amplitudes have modulus $\isqrt = 0.707107$, so both probabilities are
$0.5$, exactly as before the $S$ gate. The state has moved, and no single
measurement in this basis can tell. T1.M9 is the story of how a later gate
converts that invisible argument into a visible probability, which is what
interference means.

<Check question="Let $u = 2 + 2i$. Compute $|u|$, $\arg u$, $u^2$ and $1/u$.">
$|u| = \sqrt{4+4} = \sqrt{8} = 2.828427$. The point is on the diagonal of the
first quadrant, so $\arg u = \pi/4 = 0.785398$ rad.

$u^2 = (2+2i)^2 = 4 + 8i + 4i^2 = 8i$. Consistent with polar form: the modulus
should be $8$ and the argument $\pi/2$, and $8i$ has exactly those.

$1/u = \bar u / |u|^2 = (2 - 2i)/8 = 0.25 - 0.25i$. Its modulus is
$\sqrt{0.125} = 0.353553 = 1/2.828427$, as reciprocals require, and its argument
is $-\pi/4$.
</Check>
