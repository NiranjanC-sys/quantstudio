Two bits, four outcomes. Why is that not simply two copies of the one-bit story?
Because a distribution over two bits needs three free numbers while two separate
one-bit distributions need only two, and the missing number is correlation. That
one extra parameter is where every interesting phenomenon lives, classical and
quantum alike, so it is worth being exact about how to extract it and how to
detect its absence.

## The joint table

A state of two bits $X$ and $Y$ is a distribution over the four outcomes $00$,
$01$, $10$, $11$. Written as a column vector with $X$ as the leading bit,

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} p_{00} \\ p_{01} \\ p_{10} \\ p_{11} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad p_{xy} \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{x,y} p_{xy} = 1 .
$$

<Margin label="Ordering">
The leading bit is the one written first, so index $xy$ in binary is the row
number: $00 \to 0$, $01 \to 1$, $10 \to 2$, $11 \to 3$. RedQuant keeps this
convention everywhere: qubit 0 is the most significant bit. T0.M5.L3 is entirely
about what breaks when an SDK disagrees.
</Margin>

The same four numbers arranged as a $2 \times 2$ table make the sums easier to
see, with $x$ indexing rows and $y$ indexing columns:

|  | $y=0$ | $y=1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x=0$ | $p_{00}$ | $p_{01}$ |
| $x=1$ | $p_{10}$ | $p_{11}$ |

## Marginals are sums along an axis

If you only ever look at $X$, the probability that $X = 0$ is the probability of
$00$ plus the probability of $01$, because those are the disjoint ways it can
happen. In general,

$$
p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{xy}, \qquad p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_{xy} .
$$

Marginalising over $Y$ is summing each row; marginalising over $X$ is summing
each column. Both results are legitimate distributions: the entries are
non-negative, and $\sum_x p_X(x) = \sum_{x,y} p_{xy} = 1$.

Take the concrete joint distribution

$$
p = (0.30,\; 0.45,\; 0.10,\; 0.15)^{T} .
$$

Row sums give $p_X = (0.30 + 0.45,\; 0.10 + 0.15) = (0.75,\; 0.25)$. Column sums
give $p_Y = (0.30 + 0.10,\; 0.45 + 0.15) = (0.40,\; 0.60)$.

## Factorisation is a testable claim

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** when the joint table is the outer product of its
own marginals:

$$
p_{xy} = p_X(x)\, p_Y(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y .
$$

Test it on the example. The outer product of $(0.75, 0.25)$ and $(0.40, 0.60)$
is

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0.75 \cdot 0.40 & 0.75 \cdot 0.60 \\
0.25 \cdot 0.40 & 0.25 \cdot 0.60 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.30 & 0.45 \\ 0.10 & 0.15 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which is the original table entry for entry. These two bits are independent, and
the joint state is the tensor product $p_X \otimes p_Y$, the operation T0.M5
builds properly.

An independent pair is exactly as expensive to prepare as two separate bits.

between them:

## Uniform marginals, maximal correlation

Now the case that matters. Consider

$$
p = (0.5,\; 0,\; 0,\; 0.5)^{T},
$$

the state "both bits are equal, and which value is a fair coin". Row sums give
$p_X = (0.5, 0.5)$. Column sums give $p_Y = (0.5, 0.5)$. Each bit on its own is
a perfect fair coin, with no bias to detect by any amount of looking at it
alone. Yet the outer product of the marginals is

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0.25 & 0.25 \\ 0.25 & 0.25 \end{pmatrix}
\ne
\begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix},
$$

so the bits are not independent. All of the structure is in the relationship,
none of it in the parts. This is the classical fact that makes entanglement
comprehensible when it arrives in T1.M5: a Bell pair also has uniform marginals
and all its content in the correlation.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Same marginals is not same state">
The independent pair with $p_X = p_Y = (0.5, 0.5)$ and this correlated pair have
identical single-bit statistics. No experiment on one bit can tell them apart.
Only a joint measurement, comparing the two outcomes shot by shot, exposes the
difference.
</Callout>

## Putting a number on it

Treat the bits as the numbers 0 and 1 and compute the covariance,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \expect{XY} - \expect{X}\expect{Y} .
$$

For the correlated state, $\expect{X} = \expect{Y} = 0.5$ and
$\expect{XY} = \sum_{xy} p_{xy}\, x y = 0.5$, since only the $11$ term survives.
So $\operatorname{Cov} = 0.5 - 0.25 = 0.25$. The standard deviation of a fair
bit is $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$, giving a correlation coefficient of

$$
\rho = \frac{0.25}{0.5 \cdot 0.5} = 1 ,
$$

the maximum. For an independent pair, $\expect{XY} = \expect{X}\expect{Y}$ by
construction, so the covariance is 0.

Correlation can be partial. The table

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & 0.1 \\ 0.1 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

still has uniform marginals, but now $\expect{XY} = 0.4$, so
$\operatorname{Cov} = 0.4 - 0.25 = 0.15$ and $\rho = 0.6$. The three free
parameters of a two-bit state can be taken to be the two marginals plus this one
correlation number, which is why "three, not two" was the opening claim.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Zero covariance is weaker than independence">
Covariance only sees a linear relationship. For two bits the two notions happen
to coincide, but on larger alphabets a distribution can have zero covariance and
still be far from a product. Independence is the factorisation test, not the
covariance test.
</Callout>

<Check question="For the joint table with $p_{00} = 0.1$, $p_{01} = 0.2$, $p_{10} = 0.3$, $p_{11} = 0.4$, find both marginals and decide whether the bits are independent.">
Row sums: $p_X = (0.3, 0.7)$. Column sums: $p_Y = (0.4, 0.6)$. The outer product
would be $(0.12, 0.18, 0.28, 0.42)$, and the actual table starts with $0.1$, not
$0.12$, so the bits are dependent. Covariance confirms it:
$\expect{X} = 0.7$, $\expect{Y} = 0.6$, $\expect{XY} = 0.4$, so
$\operatorname{Cov} = 0.4 - 0.42 = -0.02$, a weak negative correlation.
</Check>
