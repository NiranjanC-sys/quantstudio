You are holding a bit you are not certain about, and someone is about to act on
it: they will flip a biased coin, and if it lands heads they will invert your
bit. What should you believe afterwards? And if they do it again, and again,
where does your belief end up? The answers are a matrix-vector product and an
eigenvalue, in that order, and the whole of quantum dynamics is built on the
same two moves.

## One round of the game

Write the state before the round as $p = (p_0, p_1)^{T}$. The coin lands heads
with probability $q$, and heads means "invert". The bit ends up as 0 in two
disjoint ways: it was 0 and the coin said leave it alone, or it was 1 and the
coin said invert. Add the two:

$$
p_0' = (1-q)\,p_0 + q\,p_1, \qquad
p_1' = q\,p_0 + (1-q)\,p_1 .
$$

Both new coordinates are linear in the old ones, so the round is a matrix:

$$
B_q = \begin{pmatrix} 1-q & q \\ q & 1-q \end{pmatrix},
\qquad p' = B_q\, p .
$$

<Margin label="Reading a column">
Column $j$ of $B_q$ is the distribution you end up with if you *start* certain
that the bit is $j$. That is the rule for every operator in this module: columns
are conditional distributions, so they are non-negative and each sums to 1.
</Margin>

Take $q = 0.3$ and start from the certain state $\ket{0} = (1,0)^{T}$. One round
gives

$$
B_{0.3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which is just the first column. A second round is the same matrix again:

$$
B_{0.3}\begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \cdot 0.7 + 0.3 \cdot 0.3 \\ 0.3 \cdot 0.7 + 0.7 \cdot 0.3 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.58 \\ 0.42 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

A third gives $(0.532, 0.468)$, a fourth $(0.5128, 0.4872)$, a fifth
$(0.50512, 0.49488)$. The state is crawling toward $(0.5, 0.5)$ and the size of
each step is shrinking by a constant factor. That factor is worth naming.

## The bias is what decays

Instead of tracking two numbers subject to a constraint, track the one number
that is actually free. Let

$$
d = p_0 - p_1 .
$$

Since $p_0 + p_1 = 1$, the pair $(p_0, p_1)$ is recovered from $d$ by
$p_0 = \tfrac{1+d}{2}$ and $p_1 = \tfrac{1-d}{2}$. Now subtract the two update
equations:

$$
d' = p_0' - p_1'
   = \big[(1-q)p_0 + q p_1\big] - \big[q p_0 + (1-q) p_1\big]
   = (1 - 2q)\,(p_0 - p_1)
   = (1-2q)\, d .
$$

One line, and the dynamics are solved. After $k$ rounds,

$$
d_k = (1-2q)^k d_0,
\qquad
p^{(k)} = \left( \tfrac{1 + (1-2q)^k d_0}{2}, \; \tfrac{1 - (1-2q)^k d_0}{2} \right).
$$

Check it against the arithmetic above. With $q = 0.3$ the factor is
$1 - 2q = 0.4$, and starting from $d_0 = 1$ we get $d_1 = 0.4$ so
$p_0 = 0.7$; $d_2 = 0.16$ so $p_0 = 0.58$; $d_3 = 0.064$ so $p_0 = 0.532$;
$d_4 = 0.0256$ so $p_0 = 0.5128$. Every value matches.

The number $1-2q$ is an eigenvalue of $B_q$, with eigenvector $(1,-1)^{T}$. The
other eigenvalue is 1, with eigenvector $(1,1)^{T}$, which is twice the uniform
distribution. Any honest starting state decomposes into "uniform, which never
moves" plus "bias, which is multiplied by $1-2q$ every round". For
$0 < q < 1$ we have $|1-2q| < 1$, the bias dies, and the uniform distribution is
the unique fixed point.

## The fair coin is not gentle, it is total

Set $q = \tfrac12$. Then $1 - 2q = 0$, and the bias is not merely damped, it is
annihilated in a single application:

$$
F = \begin{pmatrix} \half & \half \\ \half & \half \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
F \begin{pmatrix} p_0 \\ p_1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \half(p_0+p_1) \\ \half(p_0+p_1) \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \half \\ \half \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The output does not depend on the input at all. So $F^2 = F$: flipping a fair
coin twice is exactly as informative as flipping it once, which is to say, not
at all. That idempotence is the classical fact that the next lesson's quantum
analogue violates.

## The same experiment on hardware

The Hadamard gate is often introduced as "the quantum fair coin", and one
application does look like one: measure and you get 0 or 1 with probability
$\half$ each. Apply it twice and the analogy collapses.

| $0$ | $1.000000$ |
| $1$ | $0.000000$ |

</Simulate>

Two fair coins in a row leave you at $(0.5, 0.5)$. Two Hadamards in a row leave
you at $(1, 0)$, certain again. The reason is exactly the quantity we tracked:
after one Hadamard the two entries are $+\isqrt$ and $+\isqrt$, and the second
Hadamard produces $\isqrt(\isqrt + \isqrt) = 1$ for the 0 entry and
$\isqrt(\isqrt - \isqrt) = 0$ for the 1 entry. The minus sign is the whole
story. A stochastic matrix has no minus signs available, so the bias can only
shrink. A unitary matrix does, so the bias can be destroyed and then rebuilt.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Half and half is not the same state twice">
After one fair coin the bit is genuinely random. After one Hadamard the qubit is
in a definite state that merely *measures* as random. Two systems can agree on
every single-shot probability and still behave differently under further
operations, which is why the state, not the measurement statistics, is the thing
you carry forward.
</Callout>

## An unfair game with an unfair fixed point

Nothing forces the two conditional distributions to be mirror images. Suppose a
0 flips to 1 with probability $a = 0.3$, and a 1 flips to 0 with probability
$b = 0.2$. The operator is

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 1-a & b \\ a & 1-b \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 \\ 0.3 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Track $p_1$ alone, using $p_0 = 1 - p_1$:

$$
p_1' = a\,p_0 + (1-b)\,p_1 = a(1 - p_1) + (1-b)p_1 = a + (1 - a - b)\,p_1 .
$$

A fixed point $\pi_1$ satisfies $\pi_1 = a + (1-a-b)\pi_1$, so
$\pi_1 (a + b) = a$ and

$$
\pi = \left( \frac{b}{a+b}, \; \frac{a}{a+b} \right) = (0.4, \; 0.6).
$$

Subtracting the fixed-point equation from the update equation gives
$p_1' - \pi_1 = (1-a-b)(p_1 - \pi_1)$, so the deviation decays by
$1 - a - b = 0.5$ per round. Starting from $\ket{0}$, the deviation is
$-0.6$, and the sequence of $p_1$ values is $0.3$, $0.45$, $0.525$, $0.5625$,
$0.58125$, converging on $0.6$ by halving the gap each time. The uniform
distribution has no special status here; the fixed point is whatever the
asymmetry says it is.

<Check question="A biased coin with $q = 0.1$ acts on the certain state $\ket{1}$. What is $p_0$ after three rounds, and what is the limit as the number of rounds grows?">
Start with $d_0 = p_0 - p_1 = -1$ and multiply by $1 - 2q = 0.8$ three times:
$d_3 = -0.512$, so $p_0 = \tfrac{1 - 0.512}{2} = 0.244$ and $p_1 = 0.756$.
Since $|0.8| < 1$, $d_k \to 0$ and the state converges to $(0.5, 0.5)$. A weak
coin takes longer, but as long as $0 < q < 1$ the destination is the same.
</Check>
