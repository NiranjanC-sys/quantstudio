A bit is either 0 or 1, and that is the whole of it. So why does every quantum
textbook start by writing a bit as a *vector*? Because the interesting question
is not what a bit is when you know its value, it is what you can honestly say
about a bit when you do not. The answer to that question is a probability
distribution, a distribution is a vector, and once you are working with vectors
the step to a qubit is a change of one constraint rather than a change of
subject.

## The deterministic case

Write the two possible values of a bit as two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$
\ket{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ket{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

<Margin label="Notation">
The bracket $\ket{\cdot}$ is a column vector. It is read "ket". Nothing quantum
has happened yet: these are the standard basis vectors $e_1$ and $e_2$ wearing a
different hat.
</Margin>

The first coordinate records how much of the state sits on the outcome 0, the
second how much sits on the outcome 1. For a bit you are certain about, all of
it sits on one outcome and none on the other. That is a wasteful way to store one
bit of information, and it buys nothing until the coordinates are allowed to be
something other than 0 and 1.

## The probabilistic case

Now suppose someone has flipped a biased coin and hidden the result. You know the
coin lands heads with probability $\tfrac{3}{4}$, but you do not know this
particular outcome. The honest description of the bit is

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} p_0 \\ p_1 \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 0.75 \\ 0.25 \end{pmatrix},
$$

where $p_0$ is the probability the bit is 0 and $p_1$ the probability it is 1.
This is a *probabilistic state*. It is not a claim about the coin, which has
already landed. It is a claim about you.

Two conditions make a vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ a legitimate probabilistic state:

$$
p_0 \ge 0, \quad p_1 \ge 0, \qquad p_0 + p_1 = 1.
$$

The first condition is non-negativity, the second is normalisation. Together they
carve out a line segment: the set of points $(p_0, p_1)$ with both coordinates
non-negative and summing to one is exactly the straight segment joining
$\ket{0} = (1,0)$ to $\ket{1} = (0,1)$. Every probabilistic bit in the universe
is a point on that segment, and the deterministic bits are its two endpoints.

<Margin label="Definition">
The **probability simplex** on $n$ outcomes is the set of non-negative vectors in
$\mathbb{R}^n$ whose entries sum to 1. For $n = 2$ it is a segment, for $n = 3$ a
triangle, for $n = 4$ a tetrahedron.
</Margin>

It is worth being concrete about what fails. Consider four candidate vectors:

$$
a = \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 \\ 0.7 \end{pmatrix},\quad
b = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.6 \end{pmatrix},\quad
c = \begin{pmatrix} -0.2 \\ 1.2 \end{pmatrix},\quad
d = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7071 \\ 0.7071 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Only $a$ is a probabilistic state. Vector $b$ has entries summing to $1.1$, so it
over-counts certainty. Vector $c$ sums correctly but asks for a negative
probability, which has no operational meaning: there is no experiment whose
outcome happens minus one fifth of the time. Vector $d$ sums to
$0.7071 + 0.7071 = 1.4142$, which is $\sqrt{2}$, so it fails normalisation too.

Hold on to $d$. Its entries do not sum to one, but their *squares* do:
$0.7071^2 + 0.7071^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1$. That vector is not a probabilistic bit,
and it is not nothing either. It is a qubit, and the whole of T1 is the
consequence of replacing "the entries sum to 1" with "the squares of the entries
sum to 1".

## The constraint is the geometry

Say the same thing with norms. The probabilistic constraint is that the
$\ell_1$ norm equals one,

$$
\lVert p \rVert_1 = |p_0| + |p_1| = 1,
$$

together with non-negativity. The qubit constraint, which you will meet in T1,
is that the $\ell_2$ norm equals one,

$$
\lVert \psi \rVert_2 = \sqrt{|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2} = 1,
$$

with no sign restriction at all. One is a segment, the other is a circle. Almost
every difference between classical and quantum computing traces back to which
of those two shapes you are moving points around on.

## A bit you can actually run

A probabilistic bit is not only a bookkeeping device. Here is a one-qubit
circuit that produces one, physically, on any quantum computer: prepare
$\ket{0}$, apply a Hadamard gate, and measure.

The gate itself is the subject of T1.M1. What matters here is the output: the

probabilistic state $(0.5, 0.5)$, the midpoint of the segment.

## Reading a number off a state

The reason to carry the state as a vector is that questions about it become
linear algebra. Suppose you win one pound when the bit is 0 and lose three when
it is 1. Collect the payoffs in a row vector $f = (1, -3)$. Your expected
winnings are the matrix product

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] = f \, p = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0.75 \\ 0.25 \end{pmatrix}
= 1 \cdot 0.75 + (-3) \cdot 0.25 = 0.75 - 0.75 = 0.
$$

A fair bet, as it happens. The pattern generalises without change: an observable
is a row vector, a state is a column vector, and the expectation is their
product. In T1 the row vector becomes a matrix and the product picks up a
conjugate, but the shape of the calculation survives intact.

<Check question="Which of $(0.4, 0.6)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0.6, 0.6)$ and $(0.5, -0.5)$ are valid probabilistic states of one bit?">
The first two. $(0.4, 0.6)$ is an interior point of the segment and $(1, 0)$ is
the endpoint $\ket{0}$, which is deterministic but still a perfectly good
distribution. $(0.6, 0.6)$ sums to $1.2$, so it fails normalisation. $(0.5, -0.5)$
sums to $0$ and has a negative entry, so it fails both conditions. Note that
$(0.6, 0.6)$ would not be rescued by squaring either, since
$0.36 + 0.36 = 0.72 \ne 1$: it is not a qubit in disguise.
</Check>
