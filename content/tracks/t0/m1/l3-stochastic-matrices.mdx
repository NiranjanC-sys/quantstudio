The last lesson wrote one particular game as a matrix. Which matrices are
allowed? If a probabilistic state is a point in the simplex, an operation on it
must be a map that never leaves the simplex, because there is no such thing as a
bit that is 0 with probability $1.3$ or $-0.2$. That requirement has a clean
answer, it is closed under composition, and the shape of the argument is the one
you will reuse when unitarity replaces it.

## The two conditions

A real matrix $M$ is **column-stochastic** when

1. every entry is non-negative, $M_{ij} \ge 0$, and
2. every column sums to 1, $\sum_i M_{ij} = 1$ for each $j$.

The second condition has a compact form. Let $\mathbf{1}^{T} = (1, 1, \dots, 1)$
be the all-ones row vector. Then column sums are the entries of
$\mathbf{1}^{T} M$, so condition 2 says

$$
\mathbf{1}^{T} M = \mathbf{1}^{T}.
$$

<Margin label="Why columns">
Whether the sums live on rows or columns is a convention, set by whether states
are columns or rows. RedQuant writes states as columns and acts on the left,
$p' = Mp$, which is the convention every quantum text uses, so our stochastic
matrices are column-stochastic. Much of the Markov chain literature writes rows
on the left and calls the transpose stochastic.
</Margin>

Column $j$ of $M$ is the vector $M e_j$, the state you get from the certain
state $j$. The two conditions say exactly that each such column is itself a
probability distribution. A stochastic matrix is a table of conditional
distributions, one per input.

## It maps the simplex into the simplex

Let $p$ be a probability vector and set $p' = Mp$. Two things to check.

**Non-negativity.** Each entry is $p'_i = \sum_j M_{ij} p_j$, a sum of products
of non-negative numbers, so $p'_i \ge 0$.

**Normalisation.** Sum the entries and reorder the double sum:

$$
\sum_i p'_i
= \sum_i \sum_j M_{ij} p_j
= \sum_j \left( \sum_i M_{ij} \right) p_j
= \sum_j 1 \cdot p_j
= 1 .
$$

In the compact notation the same computation is one associativity step:
$\mathbf{1}^{T}(Mp) = (\mathbf{1}^{T} M) p = \mathbf{1}^{T} p = 1$. Both
conditions are needed. Drop non-negativity and $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ has unit column sums and sends $(1,0)^{T}$ to
$(2,-1)^{T}$, which is not a distribution. Drop normalisation and the matrix
$2I$ sends $(0.5, 0.5)^{T}$ to $(1,1)^{T}$, which claims total probability 2.

## Composition preserves the property

If $A$ and $B$ are column-stochastic, so is $AB$. Non-negativity again comes for
free: $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$ is a sum of products of non-negative
numbers. For the column sums,

$$
\mathbf{1}^{T} (AB) = (\mathbf{1}^{T} A) B = \mathbf{1}^{T} B = \mathbf{1}^{T} .
$$

So "run $B$, then run $A$" is a legal single-step operation, which is what lets
you compress a whole circuit into one matrix. Concretely, with
$A = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 \\ 0.3 & 0.8 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ and
the fair coin $F = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$,

$$
AF = \begin{pmatrix} 0.45 & 0.45 \\ 0.55 & 0.55 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
FA = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = F .
$$

Both are column-stochastic, and they are not equal. Order matters: a fair coin
after the biased map still erases everything, a fair coin before it does not.

## A zoo, and what is missing from it

Four operators on one bit, all column-stochastic:

$$
I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
F = \begin{pmatrix} \half & \half \\ \half & \half \end{pmatrix},
\quad
R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$I$ does nothing, $N$ is the NOT gate, $F$ is the fair coin, and $R$ resets the
bit to 0 whatever it was. Only two of them are invertible, and only those two
have a stochastic inverse: $I^{-1} = I$ and $N^{-1} = N$. $F$ and $R$ both have
rank 1, so they destroy information outright, and no matrix can undo them.

That is not an accident of small size. Suppose $M$ is stochastic, invertible,
and $M^{-1}$ is stochastic too. Then $M$ maps the simplex onto the simplex
bijectively. An affine bijection of a polytope onto itself must send extreme
points to extreme points, and the extreme points of the simplex are exactly the
deterministic states $e_1, \dots, e_n$. So every column $Me_j$ is some $e_i$,
and invertibility forces distinct columns, making $M$ a permutation matrix.

<Callout tone="note" title="The classical shadow of reversibility">
Reversible classical computing (T0.M6.L3) is the study of permutation matrices,
and unitary evolution is the quantum generalisation. The stochastic matrices
that are *not* permutations are exactly the ones that lose information, which is
why erasure has a thermodynamic cost and a NOT gate does not.
</Callout>

## Bigger alphabets, same rules

Nothing above used $n = 2$. A three-outcome system evolves under a $3 \times 3$
column-stochastic matrix in the same way:

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix}
0.5 & 0.25 & 0 \\
0.5 & 0.5 & 0.5 \\
0 & 0.25 & 0.5
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M^2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.375 \\ 0.5 \\ 0.125 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Its columns sum to 1, its eigenvalues are $1$, $0.5$ and $0$, and the
eigenvector for 1, normalised to be a distribution, is $(0.25, 0.5, 0.25)$. That
is the stationary state, and repeated application converges to it: after six
steps the state is $(0.2578, 0.5, 0.2422)$ to four places. Every column-stochastic matrix has 1 in its spectrum,
because $\mathbf{1}^{T}M = \mathbf{1}^{T}$ makes $\mathbf{1}$ a left
eigenvector, and a matrix and its transpose share eigenvalues.

## Doubly stochastic, and the bridge to quantum

If the rows sum to 1 as well, $M$ is **doubly stochastic**. Then
$M \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}$ for the uniform distribution
$\mathbf{u} = (1/n, \dots, 1/n)^{T}$, since each entry of $M\mathbf{u}$ is a row
sum divided by $n$. Among the four one-bit operators above, $I$, $N$ and $F$ are
doubly stochastic and $R$ is not: $R$ has row sums 2 and 0, and it is precisely
$R$ that can drive the uniform state somewhere else.

Here is the connection to everything that follows. Take any unitary $U$ and form
the matrix of squared magnitudes, $S_{ij} = |U_{ij}|^2$. Unitarity says the
columns of $U$ are unit vectors, so the columns of $S$ sum to 1, and the rows of
$U$ are unit vectors too, so the rows of $S$ also sum to 1. Every unitary casts a
doubly stochastic shadow. For the Hadamard gate,

$$
H = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
S = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = F .
$$

Measure a single Hadamard and you cannot tell it from a fair coin, because the
shadow is all a single measurement sees.

<Check question="Is $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 0.6 & 0.1 \\ 0.4 & 0.9 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ column-stochastic? Is it doubly stochastic? What is its stationary distribution?">
Column-stochastic: yes, all entries are non-negative and both columns sum to 1.
Doubly stochastic: no, the row sums are $0.7$ and $1.3$. For the stationary
distribution use the fixed-point formula from the previous lesson with
$a = 0.4$ and $b = 0.1$: $\pi = (b, a)/(a+b) = (0.1, 0.4)/0.5 = (0.2, 0.8)$.
Check it: $0.6 \cdot 0.2 + 0.1 \cdot 0.8 = 0.12 + 0.08 = 0.2$.
</Check>
