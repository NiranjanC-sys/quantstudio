A bit stores one bit. A bit that is 0 with probability $0.99$ stores rather less
than that, and if you have a million of them you can write them down in far
fewer than a million bits. How much less, exactly? Shannon's answer is a single
number computed from the distribution, and it is not a definition chosen for
elegance: it is forced by the compression question, and it is the quantity every
later resource count in this curriculum reduces to.

## The definition

For a distribution $p$ over a finite alphabet, the **Shannon entropy** in bits
is

$$
H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{1}{p_i},
$$

with the convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$, justified because
$t \log_2 t \to 0$ as $t \to 0^{+}$.

<Margin label="Units">
The base of the logarithm is the unit. Base 2 gives bits, base $e$ gives nats,
base $d$ gives dits. Everything in RedQuant is in bits, so every $\log$ in this
lesson is $\log_2$ unless written otherwise.
</Margin>

Read $\log_2(1/p_i)$ as the **surprise** of outcome $i$. An outcome of
probability 1 has surprise 0, an outcome of probability $\tfrac{1}{8}$ has
surprise 3, and an impossible outcome has infinite surprise but zero weight.
Entropy is the average surprise.

Four evaluations, all worth memorising:

$$
H(0.5, 0.5) = 1, \qquad
H(1, 0) = 0, \qquad
H(0.75, 0.25) = 0.811278, \qquad
H\!\left(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\right) = 2 .
$$

The third is the only one needing work:

$$
H(0.75, 0.25) = -0.75 \log_2 0.75 - 0.25 \log_2 0.25
= 0.75 \cdot 0.415037 + 0.25 \cdot 2 = 0.311278 + 0.5 = 0.811278 .
$$

A fair bit carries a full bit. A certain bit carries nothing. A three-to-one bit
carries about four fifths of a bit, and that shortfall is exactly what a
compressor gets to keep.

## The uniform distribution is the maximum

Claim: for any distribution on $n$ outcomes, $H(p) \le \log_2 n$, with equality
only for the uniform distribution.

The proof rests on one inequality, $\ln t \le t - 1$ for all $t > 0$, with
equality only at $t = 1$. That is immediate from calculus: the function
$g(t) = t - 1 - \ln t$ has $g'(t) = 1 - 1/t$, so $g$ decreases on $(0,1)$,
increases on $(1,\infty)$, and $g(1) = 0$ is its unique minimum.

Now compute the gap, working in nats and converting at the end. Write
$\ln$ throughout and note $H(p) = \frac{1}{\ln 2}\sum_i p_i \ln(1/p_i)$.

$$
\sum_i p_i \ln \frac{1}{p_i} - \ln n
= \sum_i p_i \ln \frac{1}{p_i} - \sum_i p_i \ln n
= \sum_i p_i \ln \frac{1}{n\, p_i} ,
$$

where the second step used $\sum_i p_i = 1$ to turn the constant into a weighted
sum. Apply $\ln t \le t - 1$ with $t = \frac{1}{n p_i}$ on each term for which
$p_i > 0$:

$$
\sum_i p_i \ln \frac{1}{n p_i}
\;\le\; \sum_i p_i \left( \frac{1}{n p_i} - 1 \right)
= \sum_i \left( \frac{1}{n} - p_i \right)
= 1 - 1 = 0 .
$$

Dividing by $\ln 2$ gives $H(p) \le \log_2 n$. Equality needs $t = 1$ in every
term, that is $p_i = 1/n$ for all $i$, the uniform distribution. Any bias at all
strictly reduces the entropy.

<Callout tone="note" title="This inequality has a name">
The middle expression is $-D(p \,\|\, u)$ where $D$ is the Kullback-Leibler
divergence and $u$ is uniform. The argument just given is the proof that
$D \ge 0$, and it reappears in T4 as the proof that quantum relative entropy is
non-negative, with density matrices in place of distributions.
</Callout>

## What the number buys you

Entropy is not a mood, it is a compression rate. Shannon's source coding theorem
says that a stream of independent symbols drawn from $p$ can be encoded at
$H(p)$ bits per symbol on average and no lower, in the limit of long blocks.

You can see the mechanism on a small case. Take the biased bit $p = (0.75,
0.25)$, with $H = 0.811278$. A single symbol cannot be coded in less than one
bit, since the shortest possible codeword is one bit long. So code symbols in
pairs. The pair probabilities are products:

| pair | probability | codeword length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $00$ | $0.5625$ | 1 |
| $01$ | $0.1875$ | 2 |
| $10$ | $0.1875$ | 3 |
| $11$ | $0.0625$ | 3 |

Those lengths come from running Huffman's algorithm on the four probabilities:
merge the two smallest repeatedly, $0.0625 + 0.1875 = 0.25$, then
$0.25 + 0.1875 = 0.4375$, then $0.4375 + 0.5625 = 1$. The expected length is

$$
0.5625 \cdot 1 + 0.1875 \cdot 2 + 0.1875 \cdot 3 + 0.0625 \cdot 3
= 1.6875 \text{ bits per pair},
$$

which is $0.84375$ bits per symbol. Against the naive 1 bit per symbol that is
already a 15.6 percent saving, and it sits $0.0325$ bits above the entropy
bound. Longer blocks close that gap; nothing closes it below $0.811278$.

<Margin label="Why blocks help">
Huffman codes are optimal among codes that assign a whole number of bits to each
symbol, and integrality is what costs you. Blocking $k$ symbols together spreads
the rounding error over $k$ symbols, so the overhead falls like $1/k$.
</Margin>

## Entropy of something you can run

A quantum circuit is a source. Prepare a qubit with a rotation, measure it, and
the outcome distribution has whatever entropy the angle dictates. The rotation
$R_y(\pi/3)$ gives amplitudes $\cos(\pi/6) = 0.866025$ and $\sin(\pi/6) = 0.5$,
hence probabilities $0.75$ and $0.25$: the biased bit from the previous section,
made physically.

Contrast the two-bit states of the previous lesson. The independent pair
$(0.30, 0.45, 0.10, 0.15)$ has joint entropy

$$
H = 1.782229 = 0.811278 + 0.970951 = H(p_X) + H(p_Y),
$$

the sum of its parts, because independence makes the logarithm of a product a
sum of logarithms. The correlated pair $(0.5, 0, 0, 0.5)$ has joint entropy
$H = 1$ while its marginals have entropy 1 each. The difference,

$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 \text{ bit},
$$

is the **mutual information**: the number of bits that learning $Y$ saves you
when you then have to describe $X$. For an independent pair it is 0. For the
perfectly correlated pair it is a full bit, which is right, because once you
know $Y$ you know $X$ exactly.

<Check question="A four-symbol source has $p = (0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125)$. What is its entropy, and can a whole-number-of-bits code achieve it exactly?">
$H = 0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.25 \cdot 2 + 0.125 \cdot 3 + 0.125 \cdot 3 = 0.5 + 0.5 +
0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75$ bits. Every probability is a power of $\tfrac12$, so the
ideal codeword lengths $\log_2(1/p_i)$ are the whole numbers $1, 2, 3, 3$, and
the code $0$, $10$, $110$, $111$ achieves an expected length of exactly $1.75$
with no blocking needed. That is the special case where the entropy bound is
tight symbol by symbol.
</Check>
