Everything in the last two lessons can be written with transposes, daggers and
explicit sums. So why does every paper in the field use angle brackets instead?
Because Dirac notation makes the type of each object visible in its shape. A ket
is a column, a bra is a row, and whether a product is a number or a matrix is
readable off the direction the brackets face. Once you can see types, whole
derivations become mechanical.

## Three objects, three shapes

A **ket** $\ket{a}$ is a column vector in $\mathbb{C}^n$. A **bra** $\bra{a}$ is
its conjugate transpose, a row vector: $\bra{a} = \ket{a}\dg$. For

$$
\ket{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i \\ 2 - i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\bra{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - i & 2 + i \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Two ways to multiply a row and a column, and the shapes decide which:

$$
\underbrace{\braket{a}{b}}_{1 \times n \,\cdot\, n \times 1}
= \text{a scalar},
\qquad
\underbrace{\ketbra{a}{b}}_{n \times 1 \,\cdot\, 1 \times n}
= \text{an } n \times n \text{ matrix} .
$$

<Margin label="Reading direction">
The bracket $\braket{a}{b}$ closes, so it collapses to a number. The outer
product $\ketbra{a}{b}$ opens outward, so it expands to a matrix. Dirac's
notation is one of the few in mathematics where the picture is the type
signature.
</Margin>

The bracket is the inner product from T0.M3.L1, with the conjugation already
built into the bra: $\braket{a}{b} = \sum_k \overline{a_k} b_k$. Conjugate
symmetry reads $\braket{b}{a} = \overline{\braket{a}{b}}$, and the norm is
$\lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{\braket{a}{a}}$.

## Outer products are matrices you can read off

To expand $\ketbra{a}{b}$, multiply every entry of the column by every entry of
the conjugated row. The computational basis makes this concrete:

$$
\ketbra{0}{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ketbra{0}{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

In general $\ketbra{j}{k}$ is the matrix with a single 1 in row $j$, column $k$.
That gives a decomposition of any matrix,

$$
A = \sum_{j,k} A_{jk} \ketbra{j}{k},
$$

which is worth reading in both directions. Left to right it says the matrix is
the sum of its entries. Right to left it says any operator you can describe by
"send $\ket{k}$ to something" is a sum of outer products. The Pauli gates:

$$
X = \ketbra{0}{1} + \ketbra{1}{0},
\qquad
Z = \ketbra{0}{0} - \ketbra{1}{1} .
$$

And the Hadamard, described by what it does to a basis, is
$H = \ketbra{+}{0} + \ketbra{-}{1}$. Expanding,

$$
\isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}
+ \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\end{pmatrix}
= \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
+ \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
= \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

the usual matrix. The general pattern is $U = \sum_k \ketbra{u_k}{k}$ whenever
$U$ sends basis vector $k$ to $\ket{u_k}$.

## Projectors

The special case $b = a$ with $\ket{a}$ normalised gives a **projector**,
$P = \ketbra{a}{a}$. It is idempotent,

$$
P^2 = \ket{a}\braket{a}{a}\bra{a} = \ket{a}\cdot 1 \cdot \bra{a} = P,
$$

because the inner bracket collapses to the number 1. Applying it to any vector
gives $P\ket{v} = \ket{a}\braket{a}{v}$, that is, the component of $\ket{v}$
along $\ket{a}$. For the $\pm$ basis,

$$
\ketbra{+}{+} = \half\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ketbra{-}{-} = \half\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix} .
$$

## The resolution of the identity

For any orthonormal basis $\{\ket{e_k}\}$,

$$
\sum_k \ketbra{e_k}{e_k} = I .
$$

This is the expansion rule of the previous lesson, wearing a better notation.
Apply the left side to any $\ket{v}$:

$$
\left(\sum_k \ketbra{e_k}{e_k}\right)\ket{v} = \sum_k \ket{e_k}\braket{e_k}{v}
= \sum_k c_k \ket{e_k} = \ket{v},
$$

using $c_k = \braket{e_k}{v}$. Check the two one-qubit cases numerically:
$\ketbra{0}{0} + \ketbra{1}{1} = I$ by inspection, and
$\ketbra{+}{+} + \ketbra{-}{-} = \half\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)
+ \half\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big) = I$ because
the off-diagonal halves cancel.

The practical use is inserting $I$ wherever a derivation needs a basis. For
instance,

$$
\braket{a}{b} = \bra{a} I \ket{b} = \sum_k \braket{a}{e_k}\braket{e_k}{b},
$$

which converts an abstract overlap into a sum over any basis you like. Almost
every manipulation in T2 is an insertion of the identity in a well-chosen basis.

## Probabilities are sandwiches

The Born rule in this notation says the probability of outcome $x$ on state
$\ket{\psi}$ is

$$
p(x) = \left|\braket{x}{\psi}\right|^2 = \bra{\psi} \ketbra{x}{x} \ket{\psi}
= \bra{\psi} P_x \ket{\psi},
$$

using $|z|^2 = \bar z z$ and $\overline{\braket{x}{\psi}} = \braket{\psi}{x}$.
Take the two-qubit state $\isqrt(\ket{01} + \ket{11})$, produced by a Hadamard
on qubit 0 and an $X$ on qubit 1.

The projector $P_{01} = \ketbra{01}{01}$ is the $4 \times 4$ matrix whose only
non-zero entry is a 1 in row 1, column 1, counting from zero. Sandwiching,

$$
\bra{\psi}P_{01}\ket{\psi} = \left|\braket{01}{\psi}\right|^2
= \left(\isqrt\right)^2 = 0.5 ,
$$

matching the simulated probability exactly. The two zero amplitudes are
$\braket{00}{\psi} = 0$ and $\braket{10}{\psi} = 0$, which is why those rows are
absent from the table.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Kets are not variables">
$\ket{\psi}$ is one symbol whose name happens to be $\psi$; the label inside the
ket is a name, not a quantity you can do arithmetic on. $\ket{2\psi}$ means
nothing, while $2\ket{\psi}$ means a vector twice as long. Similarly
$\ket{01}$ names a basis state, and the 01 inside it is a bitstring, not the
number one.
</Callout>

<Check question="Expand $\ketbra{+}{0}$ as an explicit matrix, then compute $\bra{1}\ketbra{+}{0}\ket{0}$.">
$\ket{+} = \isqrt(1,1)^{T}$ and $\bra{0} = (1, 0)$, so

$$
\ketbra{+}{0} = \isqrt\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}
= \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

For the sandwich, group the brackets rather than multiplying matrices:
$\braket{1}{+}\braket{0}{0} = \braket{1}{+} \cdot 1 = \isqrt = 0.707107$. It is
also the entry in row 1, column 0 of the matrix above, as it must be.
</Check>
