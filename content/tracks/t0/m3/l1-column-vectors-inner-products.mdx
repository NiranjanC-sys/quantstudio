A probabilistic state was normalised by summing its entries. A quantum state is
normalised by summing the squares of the magnitudes of its entries. Both are
statements that a vector has "length one", but under two different notions of
length, and only the second one comes from an inner product. That difference is
why quantum states live on a sphere and probabilistic ones live on a simplex,
and it is why this lesson is about a single operation: the inner product.

## Column vectors and the transpose that is not enough

A vector in $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a column of $n$ complex numbers. For real vectors,
the inner product is the familiar dot product,

$$
\langle u, v\rangle = u^{T} v = \sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k v_k .
$$

Take $u = (3, 4)^{T}$ and $v = (1, -2)^{T}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$\langle u, v\rangle = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot (-2) = -5$.

Length comes from the inner product of a vector with itself:
$\langle u, u \rangle = 9 + 16 = 25$, so $\lVert u \rVert = 5$. That works
because a real square is non-negative.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ it stops working. For $z = (i, 0)^{T}$ the plain sum of squares
gives $i^2 = -1$, and a vector of length $\sqrt{-1}$ is not a useful object. The
fix is the one T0.M2.L1 already supplied: $z \bar z = |z|^2 \ge 0$. So conjugate
the left argument.

$$
\langle u, v \rangle = u\dg v = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \overline{u_k}\, v_k .
$$

<Margin label="Which side gets conjugated">
Physics conjugates the left argument, mathematics often conjugates the right.
RedQuant follows physics, because it makes $\braket{\psi}{\phi}$ agree with
Dirac notation in T0.M3.L3 and with every quantum text. The only thing you must
never do is mix the two inside one derivation.
</Margin>

## The three properties

The complex inner product satisfies:

1. **Conjugate symmetry.** $\langle v, u \rangle = \overline{\langle u, v\rangle}$.
2. **Linearity in the second argument.** $\langle u, \alpha v + \beta w\rangle = \alpha\langle u, v\rangle + \beta\langle u, w\rangle$, and conjugate-linearity
   in the first.
3. **Positive definiteness.** $\langle u, u\rangle \ge 0$, with equality only for
   $u = 0$.

Property 3 follows from property 1 plus the definition. Setting $v = u$ in
conjugate symmetry gives $\langle u, u\rangle = \overline{\langle u, u\rangle}$, and a number equal to its own conjugate has zero imaginary part, so
$\langle u,u \rangle$ is real. Being explicitly
$\sum_k \overline{u_k} u_k = \sum_k |u_k|^2$, it is also non-negative, and it
vanishes only when every $|u_k| = 0$.

That real non-negative number defines the **norm**,

$$
\lVert u \rVert = \sqrt{\langle u, u\rangle} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_k |u_k|^2},
$$

which is the $\ell_2$ norm that T0.M1.L1 contrasted with the $\ell_1$ norm of a
probability vector.

## A worked complex pair

Let

$$
a = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i \\ 2 - i \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ i \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Conjugate the entries of $a$, then pair them off with $b$:

$$
\langle a, b\rangle = \overline{(1+i)}\cdot 3 + \overline{(2-i)}\cdot i
= (1-i)\cdot 3 + (2+i)\cdot i
= 3 - 3i + 2i + i^2
= 2 - i .
$$

Swapping the arguments should conjugate the answer:

$$
\langle b, a\rangle = \bar 3 (1+i) + \bar i (2-i) = 3 + 3i - i(2 - i)
= 3 + 3i - 2i - 1 = 2 + i,
$$

which is $\overline{2-i}$, as conjugate symmetry demands. The norms are

$$
\lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{|1+i|^2 + |2-i|^2} = \sqrt{2 + 5} = \sqrt{7} = 2.645751,
$$
$$
\lVert b \rVert = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10} = 3.162278 .
$$

## Cauchy-Schwarz

For any $u, v$,

$$
\left| \langle u, v \rangle \right| \le \lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert,
$$

with equality exactly when one vector is a scalar multiple of the other.

**Proof.** If $v = 0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise set
$\lambda = \langle v, u\rangle / \lVert v \rVert^2$ and expand the non-negative
quantity $\lVert u - \lambda v\rVert^2$:

$$
0 \le \langle u - \lambda v,\, u - \lambda v\rangle
= \lVert u \rVert^2 - \bar\lambda \langle v, u\rangle - \lambda \langle u, v\rangle
+ |\lambda|^2 \lVert v\rVert^2 .
$$

Substituting $\lambda$, the last three terms collapse to
$-|\langle u,v\rangle|^2 / \lVert v\rVert^2$, giving
$0 \le \lVert u\rVert^2 - |\langle u, v\rangle|^2/\lVert v\rVert^2$. Multiply by
$\lVert v \rVert^2$ and take square roots. $\blacksquare$

Check it on the two examples. Real case: $|\langle u,v\rangle| = 5$ against
$\lVert u\rVert \lVert v\rVert = 5 \cdot \sqrt5 = 11.180340$. Complex case:
$|\langle a, b\rangle| = |2 - i| = \sqrt5 = 2.236068$ against
$\sqrt7 \cdot \sqrt{10} = \sqrt{70} = 8.366600$. Both comfortably satisfied,
because neither pair is parallel.

<Callout tone="note" title="What the inequality is for">
Cauchy-Schwarz is what guarantees $|\braket{\psi}{\phi}| \le 1$ for two
normalised states, so the overlap can be read as a probability amplitude. It is
also the reason the angle $\cos\theta = \langle u,v\rangle / (\lVert u\rVert \lVert v\rVert)$ between two real vectors is well defined: the right-hand side
never leaves $[-1,1]$.
</Callout>

## Amplitudes are inner products

Every number a simulator prints is an inner product. If the state of $n$ qubits
is the unit vector $\ket{\psi} \in \mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ and $\ket{x}$ is a
computational basis vector, the amplitude of $x$ is $\braket{x}{\psi}$, and the
probability is $|\braket{x}{\psi}|^2$. Here is a two-qubit state built from a
rotation on qubit 0 and a Hadamard on qubit 1:

The four amplitudes are $0.612372$, $0.612372$, $0.353553$, $0.353553$. Their
squares are $0.375$, $0.375$, $0.125$, $0.125$, which sum to exactly 1. That sum
is $\lVert \psi \rVert^2$, and normalisation of a quantum state is nothing more
than the statement that this inner product equals one.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Two different normalisations, one word">
"Normalised" means $\sum_i p_i = 1$ for a distribution and
$\sum_i |\alpha_i|^2 = 1$ for a state. The words are the same and the
constraints are not. A vector can satisfy one and fail the other, as
$(0.7071, 0.7071)$ does.
</Callout>

<Check question="Let $p = (2, -1, i)^{T}$ and $q = (1, i, 1)^{T}$ in $\mathbb{C}^3$. Compute $\langle p, q\rangle$, $\lVert p \rVert$, $\lVert q\rVert$, and check Cauchy-Schwarz.">
Conjugating $p$ gives $(2, -1, -i)$, so

$$
\langle p, q\rangle = 2 \cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot i + (-i)\cdot 1 = 2 - 2i .
$$

Norms: $\lVert p \rVert = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt6 = 2.449490$ and
$\lVert q\rVert = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt3 = 1.732051$.

Cauchy-Schwarz: $|\langle p, q\rangle| = |2 - 2i| = \sqrt8 = 2.828427$, and
$\sqrt6 \sqrt3 = \sqrt{18} = 4.242641$. The inequality holds and is strict, so
the two vectors are not parallel.
</Check>
