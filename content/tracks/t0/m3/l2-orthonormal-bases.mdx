A vector does not know what its coordinates are. It has coordinates only once
you name a basis, and naming a different basis gives different numbers for the
same arrow. In quantum computing this is not pedantry: measuring in the
computational basis and measuring in the Hadamard basis are physically different
experiments on the same state, and the numbers you get are the coordinates in
one basis or the other. So the question is how to move between them, and what
survives the move.

## Orthonormal means two conditions

A set $\{e_1, \dots, e_n\}$ in $\mathbb{C}^n$ is **orthonormal** when

$$
\langle e_j, e_k \rangle = \delta_{jk} =
\begin{cases} 1, & j = k, \\ 0, & j \ne k. \end{cases}
$$

Unit length on the diagonal, mutual orthogonality off it. The standard basis
$\{(1,0)^{T}, (0,1)^{T}\}$ qualifies trivially. So does

$$
\ket{+} = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\ket{-} = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

since $\langle +, + \rangle = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$, likewise for $\ket{-}$, and
$\langle +, - \rangle = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$.

## Coordinates are inner products

The point of orthonormality is that expansion coefficients require no solving.
Suppose $v = \sum_k c_k e_k$. Take the inner product with $e_j$ and use
linearity in the second argument:

$$
\langle e_j, v\rangle = \left\langle e_j, \sum_k c_k e_k \right\rangle
= \sum_k c_k \langle e_j, e_k \rangle = \sum_k c_k \delta_{jk} = c_j .
$$

So $c_j = \langle e_j, v \rangle$ and

$$
v = \sum_k \langle e_k, v\rangle \, e_k .
$$

<Margin label="Contrast with a general basis">
For a basis that is merely linearly independent, recovering coordinates means
solving an $n \times n$ system. Orthonormality replaces that solve with $n$
independent inner products, which is why every basis in quantum computing is
orthonormal by construction.
</Margin>

Concretely, expand $w = (3,4)^{T}$ in the $\pm$ basis:

$$
\langle +, w\rangle = \isqrt(3 + 4) = \frac{7}{\sqrt2} = 4.949747,
\qquad
\langle -, w\rangle = \isqrt(3 - 4) = \frac{-1}{\sqrt2} = -0.707107 .
$$

So $w = 4.949747\ket{+} - 0.707107\ket{-}$. Check the length:
$4.949747^2 + 0.707107^2 = 24.5 + 0.5 = 25$, and $\lVert w\rVert = 5$ as in the
standard basis. The coordinates changed completely; the length did not.

## The change-of-basis matrix

Stack the new basis vectors as the **rows** of a matrix $U$, so that row $k$ is
$e_k\dg$. Then $(Uv)_k = e_k\dg v = \langle e_k, v\rangle = c_k$, and $U$ maps
old coordinates to new ones in a single product. For the $\pm$ basis,

$$
U = \isqrt \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = H,
$$

the Hadamard matrix, and $Hw = (4.949747,\; -0.707107)^{T}$, exactly the
coefficients above.

Orthonormality of the rows says precisely $UU\dg = I$: entry $(j,k)$ of
$UU\dg$ is $e_j\dg e_k = \delta_{jk}$. A square matrix with $UU\dg = I$ also has
$U\dg U = I$, so $U^{-1} = U\dg$ and the columns are orthonormal too. Such a
matrix is **unitary**, the subject of T0.M4.L2. Going back is therefore free:
$v = U\dg c$. For the Hadamard, $H\dg = H$, so $H$ is its own inverse.

A second example in $\mathbb{R}^2$: rotate the axes by 30 degrees, taking
$e_1' = (\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ)^{T} = (0.866025, 0.5)^{T}$ and
$e_2' = (-0.5, 0.866025)^{T}$. Then

$$
U = \begin{pmatrix} 0.866025 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.866025 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Uw = \begin{pmatrix} 4.598076 \\ 1.964102 \end{pmatrix},
$$

and $4.598076^2 + 1.964102^2 = 21.142303 + 3.857697 = 25$. Same vector, a third
set of coordinates, same length again.

## Why the norm survives

The general statement: if $U\dg U = I$ then $\lVert Uv \rVert = \lVert v \rVert$
for every $v$. The proof is one line of adjoint arithmetic,

$$
\langle Uv, Uv\rangle = (Uv)\dg (Uv) = v\dg U\dg U v = v\dg v = \langle v, v\rangle .
$$

Slightly more is true: the whole inner product survives, since
$\langle Uu, Uv\rangle = u\dg U\dg U v = \langle u, v\rangle$. Angles and
lengths are both preserved, which is what "change of basis" ought to mean. If
the coefficients are amplitudes, this is the statement that changing measurement
basis cannot change the total probability away from 1.

## Measuring in another basis

Physically, measuring in the $\pm$ basis is done by applying $H$ and then
measuring in the computational basis, because $H$ is exactly the matrix that
rewrites $\pm$ coordinates as computational ones. Take the state
$R_y(\pi/3)\ket{0} = 0.866025\ket{0} + 0.5\ket{1}$. Its $\pm$ coefficients are

$$
\langle +, \psi\rangle = \isqrt(0.866025 + 0.5) = 0.965926,
\qquad
\langle -, \psi\rangle = \isqrt(0.866025 - 0.5) = 0.258819,
$$

so the outcome probabilities should be $0.965926^2 = 0.933013$ and
$0.258819^2 = 0.066987$.

Without the $H$, the same state measures $0$ with probability
$0.866025^2 = 0.75$. Same state, two bases, two completely different
distributions, and both are honest descriptions of it.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The basis is part of the experiment">
There is no such thing as "the" probability of a state. There is a probability
per basis. When a paper quotes a distribution without naming a basis, the
computational basis is meant, and that is a convention rather than a fact about
the state.
</Callout>

<Check question="Expand $\ket{0}$ in the $\pm$ basis, then in the basis $\ket{y_\pm} = \isqrt(\ket{0} \pm i \ket{1})$. What outcome probabilities does each basis predict?">
In the $\pm$ basis, $\langle +, 0\rangle = \isqrt$ and
$\langle -, 0\rangle = \isqrt$, so $\ket{0} = \isqrt(\ket{+} + \ket{-})$ and
both outcomes have probability $0.5$.

In the $y$ basis, conjugate the left vector first. Its entries are
$\isqrt$ and $\pm i \isqrt$, whose conjugates are $\isqrt$ and $\mp i\isqrt$.
Pairing against $\ket{0} = (1,0)^{T}$, only the first entry survives, so
$\langle y_+, 0\rangle = \langle y_-, 0\rangle = \isqrt$ and again both
probabilities are $0.5$.

$\ket{0}$ is unbiased in both of these bases and completely certain in the
computational basis. Three mutually unbiased bases is exactly what T1.M2 uses
for single-qubit tomography.
</Check>
