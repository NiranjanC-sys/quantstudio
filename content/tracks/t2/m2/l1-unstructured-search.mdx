What is the cheapest way to find a needle in a haystack when the haystack has no
structure at all? Classically the answer is depressing and exactly known: look at
the straws. The quantum answer saves a square root, and understanding *where* the
saving comes from matters more than the algorithm, because the same mechanism is
the only one anybody has found that works on a problem with no structure to
exploit.

## The problem

You are given oracle access to $f: \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$ and told that exactly
$M$ of the $N = 2^n$ inputs satisfy $f(x) = 1$. Find one. Nothing is promised
about *which* inputs, and no two queries are related: the function is a lookup
table with no pattern in it, which is what "unstructured" means.

Classically, with $M = 1$ and the marked index uniformly random, query indices in
a random order. The marked index is equally likely to be the first, second, up to
the $N$-th you try, so the expected number of queries is

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} k = \frac{N+1}{2} .
$$

The worst case is $N - 1$, since after $N-1$ misses the answer is forced.
Randomisation buys nothing: any algorithm making $T$ queries has seen at most $T$
of the $N$ cells, so it finds the marked index with probability at most $T/N$.

<Margin label="Structure is what algorithms eat">
Sorted data gives binary search, $\log_2 N$ queries. A hash table gives $O(1)$.
Unstructured search is the problem you are left with once every such handle is
removed, which is why its complexity is the cleanest possible measure of what
raw quantum interference is worth. The answer, quadratic and no better, is
Module 9's punchline as much as this module's.
</Margin>

## Superposition alone is worth nothing

The obvious quantum move is to prepare the uniform superposition and query it in
one shot:

$$
\ket{s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N} \sum_{x} \ket{x},
\qquad
O_f \ket{s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N} \sum_x (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x} .
$$

Now measure. Every amplitude still has magnitude $1/\sqrt N$, so every outcome
still has probability $1/N$, and the chance of reading the marked index is
exactly the chance of guessing it. One query has bought a uniformly random guess,
which is what one classical query buys.

The amplitude of $\ket{101}$ is $-0.353553$ and every other amplitude is
$+0.353553$. The information is there, sitting in a relative phase, and
measurement in the computational basis cannot see it. This is the single most
important thing to internalise about the query model: the oracle is generous, and
the difficulty is entirely in the readout.

## What has to happen instead

Amplitudes are not probabilities, and the gap between them is the whole
opportunity. Write the state after the oracle as

$$
O_f \ket{s} = \ket{s} - \frac{2}{\sqrt N} \ket{w} ,
$$

where $\ket{w}$ is the marked basis state. The oracle has subtracted a small
correction along $\ket{w}$: a component of size $2/\sqrt N$, sitting on top of a
state whose overlap with $\ket{w}$ was only $1/\sqrt N$ to begin with. The
correction is *larger than the thing it corrects*. All that is missing is an
operation that turns "differs from the average" into "has large amplitude".

That operation is a reflection about $\ket{s}$, and it is the subject of the next
lesson. Its effect on a set of amplitudes $a_x$ is

$$
a_x \;\longmapsto\; 2\bar{a} - a_x, \qquad \bar{a} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_x a_x ,
$$

an inversion about the mean. Every unmarked amplitude sits at $+1/\sqrt N$ and
barely moves. The marked one sits at $-1/\sqrt N$, is nearly $2/\sqrt N$ below the
mean, and comes back nearly $2/\sqrt N$ above it. One query plus one inversion
roughly *triples* the marked amplitude, and the amplitude is what gets squared at
the end.

<Callout tone="note" title="Why the saving is a square root and not more">
Each iteration adds about $2/\sqrt N$ to the marked amplitude, so it takes about
$\sqrt N / 2$ iterations for that amplitude to reach 1. The additive step size is
set by the overlap $\braket{w}{s} = 1/\sqrt N$, and no choice of oracle or
diffuser changes it. The precise constant, $\pi/4$ rather than $1/2$, comes from
the fact that the growth is sinusoidal rather than linear, which is T2.M2.L3.
The impossibility of doing better than $\sqrt N$ is T2.M2.L9.
</Callout>

Preview the payoff on the same three qubits. One oracle call and one inversion
about the mean, and the marked string already carries most of the probability.

<Check question="Suppose the promise is changed to at most one marked item, so the input may have none. Does a single measurement of $O_f\ket{s}$ distinguish the two cases?">
No. If there is no marked item then $O_f = I$ and the state is $\ket{s}$; if there
is one then the state is $\ket{s}$ with one sign flipped. Both give the uniform
distribution on measurement, so one query and one measurement cannot tell them
apart at all. You need the interference step in both cases: run the full
algorithm, measure, and check the returned candidate with one extra query. That
final verification is why search and OR have the same query complexity up to a
constant.
</Check>
