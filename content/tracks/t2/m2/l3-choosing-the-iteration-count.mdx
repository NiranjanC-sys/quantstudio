Grover is a rotation, and a rotation does not stop when it reaches the target. It
keeps going. So the algorithm has one free parameter, the number of iterations,
and getting it wrong costs you the answer entirely rather than a few percent. The
rule is a floor, not a round, and this lesson shows where the floor comes from and
what it guarantees.

## The success probability, exactly

From the previous lesson, $k$ iterations leave the state at angle $(2k+1)\theta$
from $\ket{r}$, so measuring returns a marked item with probability

$$
P(k) = \sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big), \qquad \sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N} .
$$

Set the argument to $\pi/2$ and solve for $k$ to find the ideal real-valued count:

$$
(2k^\star + 1)\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k^\star = \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac{1}{2} .
$$

$k$ has to be an integer, and the standard choice is

$$
k_{\mathrm{opt}} = \left\lfloor \frac{\pi}{4\theta} \right\rfloor .
$$

<Margin label="Floor, not round">
Taking the floor of $\pi/(4\theta)$ is the same as rounding $k^\star$ to the
nearest integer whenever the fractional part of $\pi/(4\theta)$ is not exactly
$1/2$, and it never overshoots by more than one step. Rounding $\pi/(4\theta)$
*up* is the classic bug: it can push $(2k+1)\theta$ past $\pi/2$ by more than
$\theta$ and cost real probability. For small $M/N$, $\theta \approx \sqrt{M/N}$,
which is where the familiar $\lfloor \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N/M} \rfloor$ comes from.
</Margin>

## Two worked counts

**$N = 8$, $M = 1$.** $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt8 = 0.353553$, so
$\theta = 0.3613671$ and $\pi/(4\theta) = 2.173408$. The floor is
$k_{\mathrm{opt}} = 2$, and

$$
P(2) = \sin^2(5\theta) = \left(\frac{11\sqrt2}{16}\right)^{2} = \frac{121}{128} = 0.9453125 .
$$

The other seven amplitudes are $-0.088388 = -\sqrt2/16$ each, for
$1/128 = 0.0078125$ apiece. Two queries, and the answer is wrong once in about
nineteen runs.

**$N = 16$, $M = 2$.** Now $\sin\theta = \sqrt{2/16} = 1/\sqrt8$, the *same*
$\theta$ as before, so $k_{\mathrm{opt}} = 2$ again and the total probability on
the marked set is again $121/128$. It is split evenly, so each of the two marked
strings carries $121/256 = 0.47265625$ and each amplitude is $11/16 = 0.6875$.
This is the graph-colouring lab of T2.M2.L7, and the coincidence is not one:
$\theta$ depends on $M/N$ alone, so doubling both the space and the solution count
leaves the iteration count unchanged.

## What the floor guarantees

$k_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is within half a step of $k^\star$, and one step of the
rotation is $2\theta$, so the final angle is within $\theta$ of $\pi/2$:

$$
\left| (2k_{\mathrm{opt}}+1)\theta - \frac{\pi}{2} \right| \le \theta .
$$

Therefore

$$
P(k_{\mathrm{opt}}) \ge \cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac{M}{N} .
$$

For $N = 8$ that promises at least $0.875$ and delivers $0.9453125$. For a real
search with $N = 2^{40}$ the promise is $1 - 2^{-40}$, so for large instances the
integer rounding is free. The bound only bites when the search space is tiny,
which is exactly the regime the labs in this module run in.

## Overshooting is not a small error

At $N = 4$ the ideal count is exactly 1 and the success probability is exactly 1.
Run a second iteration and the state rotates from $90$ degrees to $150$ degrees,
where $\sin^2 = 1/4$.

<Callout tone="warning" title="You need M to choose k, and often you do not know M">
Every formula above needs $M$. When $M$ is unknown, the standard fix is the
Boyer, Brassard, Hoyer and Tapp schedule: pick $k$ uniformly from
$\{0, \dots, \lceil m \rceil - 1\}$, run, check the answer with one extra query,
and on failure multiply $m$ by $6/5$ and repeat. The expected total is still
$O(\sqrt{N/M})$ queries, with a worse constant. Never guess $M = 1$ when it might
be larger: over-rotation is the failure mode, and it is silent.
</Callout>

<Check question="You have $N = 2^{20}$ and are told $M$ is either 1 or 4. You run $k = \lfloor \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N} \rfloor = 804$ iterations. What happens in each case?">
With $M = 1$ this is right and succeeds with probability essentially 1. With
$M = 4$ the true angle is $\theta_4 = 2\theta_1$ to excellent accuracy, so the
state has rotated to about $2 \times \pi/2 = \pi$, and
$\sin^2(\pi) \approx 0$: the algorithm returns an unmarked string almost
certainly. The correct count for $M = 4$ is 402. This asymmetry is why the
unknown-$M$ schedule randomises $k$ rather than betting on a value, and why any
Grover implementation should be tested at the largest $M$ it might face, not the
smallest.
</Check>
