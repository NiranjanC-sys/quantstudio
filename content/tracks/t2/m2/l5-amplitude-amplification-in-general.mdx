Nothing in the two-reflection derivation used the fact that $\ket{s}$ was
uniform. Replace the Hadamard layer with any state-preparation unitary and the
geometry is unchanged, which turns Grover from an algorithm about search into a
primitive about *any* randomised procedure whose successes you can recognise.
That primitive, amplitude amplification, is the reason Grover shows up inside
algorithms that have nothing to do with databases.

## The general iterate

Let $A$ be any unitary with $A\ket{0} = \ket{\psi}$, and let $\Pi$ project onto a
subspace of outcomes you call **good**. Split the prepared state:

$$
\ket{\psi} = \sin\theta_a \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{good}}} + \cos\theta_a \ket{\psi_{\mathrm{bad}}},
\qquad
a = \sin^2\theta_a = \| \Pi \ket{\psi} \|^2 .
$$

$a$ is the probability that running $A$ once and measuring gives a good outcome.
Define two reflections exactly as before:

$$
S_\chi = I - 2\Pi , \qquad S_0 = I - 2\ketbra{0}{0} ,
$$

and set

$$
Q = -A\, S_0\, A\dg\, S_\chi .
$$

$S_\chi$ reflects about $\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{bad}}}$. The middle product
$A S_0 A\dg = I - 2\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$ reflects about the plane orthogonal to
$\ket{\psi}$, so with the leading minus sign it reflects *about* $\ket{\psi}$.
Two reflections at angle $\theta_a$, so $Q$ is a rotation by $2\theta_a$ in the
plane they span, and

$$
Q^k \ket{\psi} = \sin\big((2k+1)\theta_a\big)\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{good}}}
+ \cos\big((2k+1)\theta_a\big)\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{bad}}} .
$$

Grover is the case $A = H^{\otimes n}$, $\Pi = \sum_{x \in S}\ketbra{x}{x}$,
$a = M/N$. Everything else in Module 2 was a corollary of this.

<Margin label="What you must be able to do">
Amplitude amplification needs three things a classical randomised algorithm does
not provide: a *unitary* $A$ rather than a sampler, the ability to run $A\dg$,
and a reversible checker for goodness. Any classical randomised algorithm can be
made unitary by keeping its coin flips as qubits in superposition, which is what
makes the primitive so widely applicable, but the cost of that reversibility is
real and is where the constant factors go.
</Margin>

## Why the cost is $O(1/\sqrt a)$

The optimal count is $k = \lfloor \pi/(4\theta_a) \rfloor$, and for small $a$,
$\theta_a \approx \sqrt a$, so

$$
k \approx \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt a} .
$$

Classically you would run $A$ until it succeeded, which takes $1/a$ attempts in
expectation. Quantumly you run $A$ and $A\dg$ about $\pi/(4\sqrt a)$ times each.
That is a quadratic saving in the *number of runs of your own subroutine*, whatever
the subroutine is.

| $a$ | classical expected runs $1/a$ | amplifications $\lfloor \pi/(4\theta_a)\rfloor$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0.25$ | $4$ | $1$ |
| $0.01$ | $100$ | $7$ |
| $10^{-4}$ | $10^{4}$ | $78$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $10^{6}$ | $785$ |

The smallest exact instance takes one qubit. Let $A = R_y(\pi/3)$, so
$\ket{\psi} = \cos(\pi/6)\ket{0} + \sin(\pi/6)\ket{1}$ and the good outcome
$\ket{1}$ has $a = 1/4$. Then $\theta_a = \pi/6$, $\pi/(4\theta_a) = 1.5$, and one
amplification is exactly right.

Amplitude $-1$ on $\ket{1}$, probability 1. Here $S_\chi$ is a single $Z$, which
negates $\ket{1}$, and $S_0$ is $XZX$, which negates $\ket{0}$. Two calls to the
preparation and one to its inverse have turned a one-in-four procedure into a
certainty.

## Amplitude estimation

$Q$ is a rotation by $2\theta_a$ in a two-dimensional invariant subspace, so its
eigenvalues there are $e^{\pm 2i\theta_a}$. Feed $Q$ to phase estimation, which is
T2.M4, and you read $\theta_a$ off a counting register. With $t$ counting qubits
you learn $\theta_a$ to additive accuracy about $2^{-t}$, using $2^t - 1$
applications of $Q$. Writing $\varepsilon$ for the target accuracy in $a$ itself,

$$
\text{queries} = O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
\quad\text{versus classical}\quad
O\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
$$

from the Chernoff bound on $1/\varepsilon^2$ samples. That is the quadratic
speedup behind every "quantum Monte Carlo" claim: not a faster simulation, a
faster *estimator* of an expectation you could already sample.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The quadratic saving is fragile in practice">
Amplitude estimation needs coherent, controlled applications of $Q$, so the
circuit depth grows as $1/\varepsilon$ rather than the width growing as
$1/\varepsilon^2$. Under any realistic error rate the depth is the binding
constraint, and the crossover against a classical Monte Carlo on a modern CPU sits
at accuracies far beyond what near-term hardware can hold coherently. The
asymptotics are real; the break-even point is the thing to report.
</Callout>

<Check question="Your subroutine $A$ succeeds with probability $a = 0.4$, so $\theta_a = 0.684719$ and $\pi/(4\theta_a) = 1.147$. You run one amplification. What do you get, and would running zero have been better?">
One iterate gives $P(1) = \sin^2(3\theta_a) = \sin^2(2.054157) = 0.784$, so
amplification helps: $0.4$ goes to $0.784$. But notice how little room there is.
When $a$ is already large, $\theta_a$ is large, one step of $2\theta_a$ overshoots
easily, and the ideal count $k^\star = 0.647$ is not close to any integer. The
standard fix when $a$ is large and known is exact amplitude amplification: replace
the two $\pi$ phase flips by tuned angles so that a whole number of iterations
lands exactly on $\pi/2$. With $a$ unknown, randomise $k$ as in the unknown-$M$
schedule of the previous lesson.
</Check>
