Inversion about the mean sounds like a trick with averages. It is in fact a
reflection, the oracle is also a reflection, and two reflections in a plane
compose into a rotation. Once you see the iterate that way, everything about
Grover follows from plane geometry: how fast the algorithm converges, when to
stop, and why running it longer makes it worse.

## Two states span everything that happens

Let $S$ be the set of $M$ marked inputs out of $N$, and define

$$
\ket{w} = \frac{1}{\sqrt M}\sum_{x \in S}\ket{x},
\qquad
\ket{r} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N-M}}\sum_{x \notin S}\ket{x} .
$$

These are orthonormal, and the uniform superposition decomposes as

$$
\ket{s} = \sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}\,\ket{w} + \sqrt{\frac{N-M}{N}}\,\ket{r}
= \sin\theta\,\ket{w} + \cos\theta\,\ket{r},
\qquad
\sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{M}{N}} .
$$

Everything the algorithm does stays inside $\mathrm{span}\{\ket{w}, \ket{r}\}$,
because both operators below preserve it. A search over $2^{50}$ items is
therefore a problem in two real dimensions.

## Reflection one: the oracle

The marking oracle of T2.M1.L3 is
$O_f = I - 2\sum_{x \in S}\ketbra{x}{x}$, which in the two-dimensional basis is

$$
O_f = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\quad \text{on } (\ket{w}, \ket{r}) .
$$

That is a reflection about the $\ket{r}$ axis. It costs one query.

## Reflection two: the diffuser

Start from the reflection about $\ket{0^n}$, which negates every basis state
*except* the all-zeros one:

$$
R_0 = I - 2\ketbra{0^n}{0^n} .
$$

Conjugate it by Hadamards. Since $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \ket{s}$ and
$H^{\otimes n}$ is its own inverse,

$$
H^{\otimes n} R_0 H^{\otimes n}
= H^{\otimes n} H^{\otimes n} - 2\,H^{\otimes n}\ketbra{0^n}{0^n}H^{\otimes n}
= I - 2\ketbra{s}{s} .
$$

The **diffuser** is the negative of that, $D = 2\ketbra{s}{s} - I$, a reflection
*about* $\ket{s}$ rather than about its orthogonal complement. Acting on a state
with amplitudes $a_x$, it sends $a_x \mapsto 2\bar a - a_x$ with
$\bar a = \frac1N \sum_x a_x$, which is the inversion about the mean promised in
the last lesson.

<Margin label="A global sign nobody pays for">
The circuit below builds $I - 2\ketbra{s}{s} = -D$, not $D$. Grover is usually
written with $D$, so a $k$-iteration circuit differs from the textbook operator by
$(-1)^k$. That is a global phase, invisible to every measurement, and it is why
the simulated amplitudes here sometimes carry the opposite sign to the formula
$\sin((2k+1)\theta)$. Check magnitudes, not signs.
</Margin>

Gate by gate, $R_0$ is a masked multi-controlled $Z$ with the mask flipping *all*
$n$ bits, so the diffuser is

$$
H^{\otimes n} \; X^{\otimes n} \; \mathrm{MCZ} \; X^{\otimes n} \; H^{\otimes n} ,
$$

which is $2n$ Hadamards, $2n$ $X$ gates and one multi-controlled $Z$. It contains
no oracle call at all, which is exactly why the query count and the gate count of
Grover tell different stories.

## Two reflections make a rotation

In a real plane, reflecting about a line at angle $\alpha$ and then about a line
at angle $\beta$ is a rotation by $2(\beta - \alpha)$. Here the first line is the
$\ket{r}$ axis, at angle 0, and the second is $\ket{s}$, at angle $\theta$. So

$$
G = D \, O_f = \begin{pmatrix}
\cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ -\sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta
\end{pmatrix}
$$

is a rotation by $2\theta$ towards $\ket{w}$. Starting from $\ket{s}$, which sits
at angle $\theta$ from $\ket{r}$, $k$ iterations land at angle $(2k+1)\theta$:

$$
G^k \ket{s} = \sin\big((2k+1)\theta\big)\ket{w} + \cos\big((2k+1)\theta\big)\ket{r} .
$$

Measure and you read a marked item with probability $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$. Every
quantitative claim in this module is that one formula.

## The smallest complete example

Take $N = 4$ with one marked item, so $\sin\theta = 1/2$ and $\theta = \pi/6$. One
iteration rotates from $\theta = 30$ degrees to $3\theta = 90$ degrees, landing
exactly on $\ket{w}$. The success probability is $\sin^2(\pi/2) = 1$, with no
error at all.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The diffuser depends on the algorithm, not the problem">
$D$ contains no information about $f$. It is the same $2n$ Hadamards and one MCZ
whether you are cracking a hash or colouring a graph. Everything problem-specific
lives in the oracle, which is why a Grover implementation is judged almost
entirely on how cheaply it compiles its predicate. When someone reports a Grover
circuit with a plausible qubit count and an implausible depth, the oracle is
where the depth went.
</Callout>

<Check question="What does the iterate do if you mistakenly reflect about $\ket{0^n}$ instead of about $\ket{s}$, that is, if you drop the Hadamard layers from the diffuser?">
It stops being a rotation in the $\{\ket{w}, \ket{r}\}$ plane, because $R_0$ does
not preserve that plane: it singles out $\ket{0^n}$, which is generally neither
marked nor aligned with $\ket{r}$. Concretely, $O_f$ and $R_0$ are both diagonal,
so they commute and their product is diagonal too. A diagonal operator can never
change any probability, so the circuit would return the uniform distribution for
every iteration count. The Hadamard layers are not cosmetic; they are the only
part of the diffuser that moves amplitude between basis states.
</Check>
