You have the formula $P(k) = \sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$. This lesson is the formula
with your hands on it. Work each figure the same way: read the iteration count,
predict the probability and the arrow, then look. The prediction is the exercise;
the figure is only the answer key.

## A rotation you can see whole

Two qubits, marked index $11$, so $\theta = \pi/6$ and each iterate turns the
state by $2\theta = 60$ degrees. Three iterates therefore turn it by $180$
degrees, which is why this instance closes on itself after exactly three steps.

At $k = 1$ the state is $\ket{11}$ up to a sign. Predict: what are the two Bloch
arrows?

Both arrows sit at $z = -1$ with purity 1, because $\ket{11}$ is a product state
and each qubit is separately in $\ket{1}$. This is the only iteration count at
which this instance is solved.

Both arrows have length zero and purity zero. The two qubits are now maximally
entangled: the state is $(-\ket{00}-\ket{01}-\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2$, which no
product of single-qubit states can reproduce. Uniform probabilities on the left,
no arrow on the right. Over-rotation is not a small degradation, it has taken you
from certainty to a maximally uninformative distribution while spending a second
query to do it.

<Margin label="Why the arrows vanish and the first lesson's did not">
A single-qubit Bloch arrow only has length 1 when that qubit is unentangled from
the rest. Grover's state is a product exactly at the two ends of the swing, at
$\ket{s}$ and at $\ket{w}$, and entangled everywhere in between. The Bloch view
is therefore an entanglement meter here, not a state display: watch the purity
number, not the direction.
</Margin>

Both arrows are back at $x = +1$ with purity 1, and every amplitude is $+0.5$.
Three iterations of Grover on $N = 4$ are the identity on $\ket{s}$, so the
success probability is periodic in $k$ with period 3:

$$
P(k) = 0.25,\; 1,\; 0.25,\; 0.25,\; 1,\; 0.25, \dots
$$

## Now with a smaller angle

Eight items and one marked, so $\theta = 0.361367$ and each step turns by
$2\theta = 0.722734$ radians. The state no longer lands on $\ket{w}$ exactly, and
the period $\pi/\theta = 8.6936$ is no longer an integer, so the sequence never
repeats exactly.

| $k$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ | $6$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P(k)$ | $0.125$ | $0.78125$ | $0.9453125$ | $0.330078$ | $0.012207$ | $0.547974$ | $0.999786$ |

Two things to notice. The minimum at $k = 4$ is $0.012207$, *below* the $0.125$
you would have got by not running the algorithm at all: four queries have made
the answer less likely than a coin. And the second peak at $k = 6$ reaches
$0.999786$, better than the first peak at $k = 2$, because $13\theta = 4.6978$ is
closer to $3\pi/2$ than $5\theta$ was to $\pi/2$. Running longer is not monotonically
worse, it is *sinusoidally* worse, and three times the work buys you a fraction of
a percent.

<Check question="For $N = 2^{30}$ with one marked item, roughly how many iterations does it take to come back near the uniform superposition, and why is that number never useful?">
Here $\theta \approx 1/\sqrt N = 2^{-15}$, so the period in $k$ is
$\pi/\theta \approx \pi \cdot 2^{15} = 102943.7$ iterations, almost exactly four
times the optimal count $\lfloor \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N \rfloor = 25735$. It is
never useful because you would stop at the first peak; the revival only matters as
a diagnostic. If you measure a Grover circuit and get the uniform distribution,
either your oracle marks nothing, or it marks everything, or your iteration count
landed on a multiple of the period. All three are bugs, and they look identical
from the histogram.
</Check>
