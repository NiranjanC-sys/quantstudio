A unitary's eigenvalues all sit on the unit circle, so each one is a single real
number $\varphi$ with $e^{2\pi i \varphi}$ as the eigenvalue. Phase estimation is
the circuit that writes $\varphi$ into a register in binary. Order finding is one
instance of it, quantum chemistry another, linear-systems solvers a third; if you
learn one subroutine in this track, learn this one.

## The setup

You are given a unitary $U$ as a circuit you can control, and an eigenvector
$\ket{u}$ with

$$
U\ket{u} = e^{2\pi i \varphi}\ket{u}, \qquad 0 \le \varphi < 1 .
$$

You want $\varphi$ to $t$ bits. Use $t$ **counting** qubits, indices 0 to $t-1$
with qubit 0 the most significant, plus whatever register holds $\ket{u}$.

## The phase ramp

Hadamard every counting qubit, then apply $U^{2^{\,t-1-q}}$ controlled on counting
qubit $q$. Kickback does the rest. Since $\ket{u}$ is an eigenvector, a controlled
$U^m$ leaves it alone and puts $e^{2\pi i \varphi m}$ on the control's $\ket 1$
branch:

$$
\frac{\ket0 + \ket1}{\sqrt2}\ket{u}
\;\longmapsto\;
\frac{\ket0 + e^{2\pi i \varphi m}\ket1}{\sqrt2}\ket{u} .
$$

Do that for every counting qubit with $m = 2^{\,t-1-q}$ and multiply out the
tensor product. A basis state $\ket{j}$ of the counting register has
$j = \sum_q j_q 2^{\,t-1-q}$, so the accumulated phases combine into
$e^{2\pi i \varphi j}$ and

$$
\bigotimes_{q=0}^{t-1}\frac{\ket0 + e^{2\pi i \varphi 2^{t-1-q}}\ket1}{\sqrt2}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{t}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{t}-1} e^{2\pi i \varphi j}\ket{j} .
$$

That is the phase ramp of T2.M3.L3. Compare it with the product form of the QFT
and you see the point: this state is exactly $\mathrm{QFT}\ket{2^t \varphi}$, so

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\dg \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{t}}}\sum_j e^{2\pi i \varphi j}\ket{j} \right)
= \ket{2^{t}\varphi}
$$

whenever $2^t \varphi$ is an integer. The whole algorithm is: make the ramp,
invert the transform, read the register.

<Margin label="Exponentiate the angle, do not repeat the gate">
Written naively the controlled powers cost $1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{t-1} = 2^t - 1$
applications of $U$, which is exponential in $t$. For a phase gate you instead
multiply the angle, which is free. For a general $U$ you cannot, and $2^t$
applications is the honest cost; this is why phase estimation is efficient only
when $U^{2^k}$ has a short circuit, as modular exponentiation does.
</Margin>

## The exact case

Take $U = P(2\pi \cdot 5/8)$, the phase gate, whose eigenvector $\ket1$ has
$\varphi = 5/8 = 0.101$ in binary: exactly three bits. Three counting qubits should
therefore give a deterministic answer.

## The eigenstate register never moves

Look again at the kickback step. Controlled $U^m$ acting on $\ket{j}\ket{u}$ gives
a phase times $\ket{j}\ket{u}$: the second register is an eigenvector, so it comes
back unchanged, every time. The inverse QFT touches only the counting register. So
$\ket u$ enters and leaves untouched, and by linearity, feeding a *superposition*
of eigenvectors gives a superposition of answers, each entangled with its own
eigenvector:

$$
\sum_i c_i \ket{0^t}\ket{u_i}
\;\longmapsto\;
\sum_i c_i \ket{2^t \varphi_i}\ket{u_i} .
$$

Put the eigenstate qubit of the circuit above in $\ket{+} = (\ket0 + \ket1)/\sqrt2$,
a superposition of the two eigenvectors of $P$, whose phases are $\varphi = 0$ and
$\varphi = 5/8$.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is what makes phase estimation an algorithm rather than a measurement">
You almost never have an eigenvector to hand. In order finding nobody can prepare
the eigenvectors of the modular multiplication operator, and in chemistry nobody
can prepare the ground state. What you can prepare is a state with decent overlap
onto the one you want. Phase estimation then returns that eigenphase with
probability equal to the overlap squared, and the eigenvector for free. The
overlap is the resource, and estimating it honestly is the difference between a
chemistry proposal and a chemistry result.
</Callout>

<Check question="You run phase estimation with $t = 3$ on a unitary whose eigenphase is $\varphi = 1$, that is, eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i} = 1$. What does the counting register read?">
$000$, with probability 1. The phase is defined modulo 1, and $\varphi = 1$ is the
same as $\varphi = 0$: every controlled power contributes $e^{2\pi i m} = 1$, the
ramp is flat, and the inverse QFT sends the uniform superposition back to
$\ket{0^t}$. This is the reason phase estimation cannot distinguish an eigenvalue
of $1$ from an eigenvalue of $1$, which sounds trivial but is the source of a real
failure in order finding: the trivial eigenvector contributes an uninformative
$000\cdots0$ outcome, and T2.M5.L3 has to detect and discard it.
</Check>
