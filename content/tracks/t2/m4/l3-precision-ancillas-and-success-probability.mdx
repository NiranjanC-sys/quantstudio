Real eigenphases are irrational. The circuit of T2.M4.L1 was deterministic only
because $5/8$ happened to fit in three bits, and nothing in nature fits in three
bits. So the working question is: how many counting qubits do you need to get $n$
correct bits, with what confidence, and what does adding a qubit actually buy?

## The distribution when the phase does not fit

Let $k$ be a measurement outcome and $\delta = \varphi - k/2^t$ the offset. Running
the inverse QFT on the phase ramp and reading off the amplitude of $\ket k$ gives a
geometric sum, and its modulus squared is the Dirichlet kernel

$$
\Pr[k] = \frac{1}{2^{2t}}\left| \frac{\sin(\pi 2^{t}\delta)}{\sin(\pi \delta)} \right|^{2} ,
$$

which is $1$ when $\delta = 0$ and decays like $1/(2^t\delta)^2$ away from the
peak. Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

**The peak is always tall.** The best outcome, the $k$ nearest to $2^t\varphi$,
always carries at least

$$
\frac{4}{\pi^2} = 0.405285 ,
$$

with equality only in the worst case, $\varphi$ exactly halfway between two
representable values.

**The peak never gets taller.** The height depends on the fractional part of
$2^t \varphi$, not on $t$. Adding counting qubits moves the grid, it does not
sharpen the kernel.

<Margin label="Precision and confidence are different resources">
Counting qubits buy *precision*: the spacing $2^{-t}$ between representable
answers. Repetitions buy *confidence*: the chance that a particular run landed on
the peak rather than in the skirt. The two are bought separately, and conflating
them is why resource estimates for quantum chemistry vary by orders of magnitude
between papers.
</Margin>

## The sizing rule

Fix a target: $n$ correct bits, that is an answer within $2^{-n}$ of $\varphi$,
with failure probability at most $\varepsilon$. Summing the Dirichlet tails
outside the tolerance window gives a failure bound of $1/(2(2^{p}-2))$ when
$t = n + p$, and setting that below $\varepsilon$ yields the standard rule

$$
t = n + \left\lceil \log_2\!\left(2 + \frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\right) \right\rceil .
$$

| target | extra qubits | total $t$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $n=3$, $\varepsilon=0.25$ | $2$ | $5$ |
| $n=5$, $\varepsilon=0.05$ | $4$ | $9$ |
| $n=5$, $\varepsilon=0.01$ | $6$ | $11$ |
| $n=10$, $\varepsilon=0.01$ | $6$ | $16$ |

The extra term grows like $\log(1/\varepsilon)$, so confidence is cheap in qubits.
Check the first row against the table above: $t = 5$ gives $0.964107$ of the weight
within $2^{-3}$, comfortably better than the $0.75$ the rule promises.

## Repetition, and its limit

The alternative to extra qubits is extra shots. Run $r$ times and take the most
frequent outcome. Because the peak carries at least $4/\pi^2$ and every other
single outcome carries less, the modal outcome converges to the peak, and a
Chernoff bound gives a failure probability falling exponentially in $r$.

The subtlety is that $4/\pi^2 = 0.405$ is *below* one half, so a naive bitwise
majority vote is not guaranteed to work: an individual bit can be wrong more often
than right in the worst case. Two safe alternatives:

- take the modal *string* rather than a bitwise majority, which is sound because
  the peak beats every competitor individually;
- buy the confidence in qubits instead, using the sizing rule above, which is
  exactly the mechanism that turns $4/\pi^2$ into $1 - \varepsilon$.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Extra counting qubits are not free at scale">
Under error correction each logical counting qubit costs thousands of physical
qubits, and each extra counting qubit also doubles the number of applications of
$U$, so it doubles the runtime. Adding four qubits for confidence therefore
multiplies the runtime by sixteen. When a resource estimate reports "$t = n +
\log(1/\varepsilon)$" as a small additive cost, check whether it also propagated
the multiplicative $2^t$ in the controlled powers. That is usually where the
missing factor of a thousand lives.
</Callout>

<Check question="You need the eigenphase of a molecular Hamiltonian to 8 bits with 99% confidence. How many counting qubits, and how many applications of $U$?">
The rule gives $t = 8 + \lceil \log_2(2 + 50) \rceil = 8 + 6 = 14$ counting
qubits. The controlled powers use $U^{2^{k}}$ for $k = 0$ to $13$, so
$2^{14} - 1 = 16383$ applications of $U$ in total if $U$ has no shortcut. The
qubit count sounds mild and the runtime does not: this is the calculation that
motivates every alternative in T2.M8.L3, where the same eigenphase is read from a
qubitised walk operator whose powers are cheaper.
</Check>
