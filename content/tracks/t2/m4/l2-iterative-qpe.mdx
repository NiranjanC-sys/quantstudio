The textbook circuit spends $t$ qubits to hold $t$ bits of answer, then throws
$t-1$ of them away the moment the register is measured. On a machine with 127
qubits and a shallow coherence budget that is an unaffordable luxury. Iterative
phase estimation extracts the same $t$ bits with one ancilla, by measuring each
bit as soon as it is ready and feeding the result forward as a rotation.

## Reading the bits from the bottom up

Write the phase in binary, $\varphi = 0.b_1 b_2 \cdots b_t$. The trick is that the
*last* bit is the easiest one to get. Apply $U^{2^{t-1}}$ once, controlled on a
single ancilla in $\ket+$. The phase kicked back is

$$
2\pi \varphi 2^{t-1} = 2\pi \big( b_1b_2\cdots b_{t-1}.b_t \big) ,
$$

and phases are defined modulo $2\pi$, so everything left of the point vanishes and
the ancilla ends in $(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.b_t}\ket1)/\sqrt2$. That is
$\ket+$ if $b_t = 0$ and $\ket-$ if $b_t = 1$. A Hadamard and a measurement read
$b_t$ with certainty.

Now go up one bit. Apply $U^{2^{t-2}}$, which kicks back $2\pi \cdot 0.b_{t-1}b_t$.
The known $b_t$ contributes a known $2\pi \cdot 0.0b_t = \pi b_t / 2$, so rotate it
away with a phase gate of angle $-\pi b_t/2$ on the ancilla before the final
Hadamard. What is left is $2\pi \cdot 0.b_{t-1}$, and the measurement reads
$b_{t-1}$ with certainty. Repeat: in the round that determines bit $b_m$, the
correction is

$$
-2\pi \times 0.0\,b_{m+1}b_{m+2}\cdots b_t ,
$$

which is a single phase gate whose angle depends on the bits already measured. It
is zero in the first round, since no bits are known yet.

<Margin label="Why the correction is a phase and not a conditional gate">
The feed-forward is one $R_z$ angle computed classically from the measured bits.
It does not need a conditional application of a two-qubit gate, only a
classically-parameterised single-qubit rotation, which every control stack can do
between shots. That is why iterative QPE was implementable on hardware years
before mid-circuit measurement was reliable enough for error correction.
</Margin>

## The trade

| | textbook QPE | iterative QPE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| counting qubits | $t$ | $1$ |
| applications of $U$ | $2^t - 1$ | $2^t - 1$ |
| inverse QFT | $t(t-1)/2$ rotations | none |
| mid-circuit measurement | no | $t-1$ times |
| classical latency in the loop | none | $t-1$ round trips |

The oracle cost is identical, because the controlled powers are the same powers.
What iterative QPE buys is $t-1$ qubits and the entire inverse QFT; what it pays
is $t-1$ measure-and-decide round trips, each of which must complete inside the
coherence time of the ancilla and the eigenstate register.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The eigenstate register still has to survive">
Only the counting register shrinks. The register holding $\ket u$ is present for
every round and must stay coherent through all $2^t - 1$ applications of $U$, so
the total coherence requirement is unchanged. Iterative QPE is a width
optimisation, not a depth one, and reporting it as "phase estimation on 2 qubits"
without saying how many qubits $\ket u$ occupies is the standard way to make a
resource estimate look better than it is.
</Callout>

<Check question="Your hardware needs 4 microseconds to measure, decide and apply the correction, and the ancilla has $T_2 = 80$ microseconds. How many bits can you extract iteratively before the feedback latency alone exhausts the coherence?">
Twenty rounds of 4 microseconds each fill the whole $80$ microsecond budget, so
even ignoring the time spent applying $U$ you cannot exceed about 20 bits, and in
practice you want the total well under $T_2$, so a dozen is more honest. That is
the constraint that decides between iterative and textbook QPE on a given device:
if classical latency times $t$ is comparable to $T_2$, spend the qubits instead and
run the textbook version, whose entire counting register is measured once at the
end.
</Check>
