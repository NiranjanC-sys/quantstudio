Simon hides an XOR mask, order finding hides a period, discrete log hides an
exponent. Written out they look like three different tricks. Written in the right
language they are one theorem with three groups substituted in, and once you can
see the pattern you can also see exactly where it stops: the boundary of the
abelian case is the boundary of everything the subject knows how to do
exponentially fast.

## The problem

Let $G$ be a finite abelian group and let $f: G \to S$ be a function into some set,
promised to satisfy

$$
f(g_1) = f(g_2) \iff g_1 g_2^{-1} \in H
$$

for some unknown subgroup $H \le G$. In words: $f$ is constant on each coset of
$H$ and takes different values on different cosets. Given oracle access to $f$,
output a generating set for $H$.

The promise is the entire content. $f$ is otherwise arbitrary, so no classical
algorithm can do anything but hunt for collisions, and collisions among $|G|/|H|$
distinct values arrive after about $\sqrt{|G|/|H|}$ queries.

<Margin label="Structure theorem">
Every finite abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups,
$G \cong \Z_{N_1} \times \cdots \times \Z_{N_k}$. The Fourier transform of a
product is the tensor product of the transforms, so
$F_G = F_{\Z_{N_1}} \otimes \cdots \otimes F_{\Z_{N_k}}$ and everything reduces to
the cyclic case of T2.M3.L3. That is why "abelian" and "efficient QFT" mean the
same thing here.
</Margin>

## The algorithm, in four lines

**Prepare** $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g \in G}\ket{g}\ket{0}$.

**Query** $f$ to get $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g}\ket{g}\ket{f(g)}$, and measure
the second register. The outcome is some value $f(g_0)$, and the first register
collapses onto the uniform superposition over that value's preimage, which by the
promise is exactly one coset:

$$
\ket{g_0 H} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|H|}}\sum_{h \in H}\ket{g_0 h} .
$$

The offset $g_0$ is uniformly random and you never learn it.

**Transform** with $F_G$. The characters of an abelian $G$ are the homomorphisms
$\chi_y : G \to \C^\times$, indexed by $y \in \hat G \cong G$, and
$F_G \ket{g} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_y \chi_y(g)\ket{y}$. So

$$
F_G\ket{g_0 H}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{|G||H|}}\sum_{y}\chi_y(g_0)\left(\sum_{h \in H}\chi_y(h)\right)\ket{y} .
$$

The inner sum is a character sum over a subgroup: it is $|H|$ when $\chi_y$ is
trivial on all of $H$, and 0 otherwise, because a non-trivial character sums to
zero over any subgroup it does not kill.

**Measure.** Writing the **annihilator**

$$
H^{\perp} = \{\, y \in \hat G : \chi_y(h) = 1 \text{ for all } h \in H \,\} ,
$$

the state is $\sqrt{|H|/|G|}\sum_{y \in H^\perp}\chi_y(g_0)\ket{y}$, so the outcome
is a uniform element of $H^\perp$ and the unknown offset survives only as an
unmeasurable phase.

$H^\perp$ is a subgroup of order $|G|/|H|$, and $(H^\perp)^\perp = H$. So collect
enough uniform samples from $H^\perp$ to generate it, then dualise. Each sample
costs one query.

## The smallest cyclic instance

Take $G = \Z_4$, held in two qubits with qubit 0 the most significant, and
$f(x) = x \bmod 2$, which is constant on the cosets of $H = \{0, 2\}$. Since
$\chi_y(h) = e^{2\pi i y h/4}$, the annihilator is
$\{y : 2y \equiv 0 \bmod 4\} = \{0, 2\}$: predict outcomes $0$ and $2$, each with
probability $0.5$, and nothing else.

Four strings at $0.25$ each. The first two bits are $00$ in two of them and $10$ in
the other two, so $y = 0$ and $y = 2$ each carry $0.5$, exactly $H^\perp$. One
non-zero sample, $y = 2$, generates $H^\perp$, and dualising gives
$H = \{x : 2x \equiv 0\} = \{0, 2\}$.

## The three algorithms you already know

| algorithm | $G$ | $H$ | $H^\perp$ | what a sample is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simon | $\Z_2^{\,n}$ | $\{0, s\}$ | $\{y : y \cdot s = 0\}$ | one linear equation on $s$ |
| order finding | $\Z_{2^t}$ approximating $\Z$ | $r\Z$ | multiples of $2^t/r$ | one approximate multiple of $1/r$ |
| discrete log | $\Z_r \times \Z_r$ | $\langle (m,1)\rangle$ | $\{(\alpha,\beta): \alpha m + \beta = 0\}$ | one congruence on $m$ |

Deutsch-Jozsa and Bernstein-Vazirani fit too, with $G = \Z_2^n$ and $H$ the kernel
of a linear form. Every exponential speedup in this track other than the quantum
walks of T2.M7 is a row of that table.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Order finding is the row that does not quite fit">
$\Z$ is infinite, so there is no finite uniform superposition over it and no exact
QFT. Order finding truncates to $\Z_{2^t}$, which is not a subgroup of $\Z$ in any
useful sense, and pays for the mismatch with the leakage of T2.M3.L3 and the
continued-fraction repair of T2.M5.L3. The framework is exact for finite $G$ and
approximate for the infinite case, and every complication in Shor comes from that
one substitution.
</Callout>

<Check question="Suppose $H = G$, so $f$ is constant. What does the algorithm return, and how many queries does it take to be sure?">
$H^\perp$ is trivial, containing only the identity character, so every sample
returns $y = 0$. That is the correct answer and it is indistinguishable from a
broken oracle after one query. You become confident by counting: a proper subgroup
$H < G$ has $|H^\perp| \ge 2$, so a uniform sample from it is non-zero with
probability at least $1/2$, and $k$ consecutive zero samples make
$H \ne G$ at most $2^{-k}$ likely. Twenty samples settle it, which is the same
"the all-zeros outcome is uninformative" phenomenon as in T2.M3.L5.
</Check>
