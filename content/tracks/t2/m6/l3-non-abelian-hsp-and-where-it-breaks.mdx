Drop the word "abelian" from the previous two lessons and almost everything
survives. You can still prepare the coset state with one query. The state still
determines $H$. What you cannot do, and what nobody has done in thirty years, is
get $H$ back out of it efficiently. This lesson is about that gap, because it is
simultaneously the most-attacked open problem in quantum algorithms and the reason
lattice cryptography is believed to be quantum-safe.

## What still works

For any finite group $G$, abelian or not, one query to a function constant on left
cosets of $H$ leaves the register in

$$
\ket{gH} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|H|}}\sum_{h \in H}\ket{gh}
$$

with $g$ uniformly random. Averaging over the unknown $g$, the state you actually
hold is the mixed **coset state**

$$
\rho_H = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \ketbra{gH}{gH} .
$$

Ettinger, Hoyer and Knill proved that $O(\log|G|)$ copies of $\rho_H$ determine $H$
*information-theoretically*: no two distinct subgroups produce coset states that
are close in trace distance once you hold enough copies. So the query complexity of
the non-abelian hidden subgroup problem is polynomial, for every $G$. The problem is
entirely one of *time*.

<Margin label="Query complexity is not the obstruction">
This is the cleanest example in the track of the gap T2.M1.L4 warned about. A
polynomial query complexity with no known polynomial-time algorithm means the free
unitaries $U_j$ of the query model are hiding the whole difficulty. The measurement
that separates the candidate subgroups exists; describing it takes exponentially
many parameters, and nobody knows how to implement it with a small circuit.
</Margin>

## Why the abelian proof stops

The abelian argument used one fact: the characters $\chi_y$ are one-dimensional, so
the Fourier transform sends the coset state to a distribution over $\hat G$ and
the unknown offset $g_0$ becomes a phase $\chi_y(g_0)$ that measurement ignores.

For non-abelian $G$ the irreducible representations $\rho$ have dimension
$d_\rho > 1$, and the Fourier transform sends $\ket{gH}$ to a superposition over
*matrix entries* $(\rho, i, j)$. The unknown offset now acts by matrix
multiplication on the row index, which is not a phase and does not go away. Three
standard measurement strategies, in increasing order of power:

**Weak Fourier sampling** measures only the representation name $\rho$, discarding
the matrix indices. The resulting distribution is offset-independent, but for the
symmetric group it is provably almost the same whether $H$ is trivial or an order-2
subgroup, so it cannot distinguish the two cases you care about.

**Strong Fourier sampling** measures $\rho$ and then the row and column in a basis
of your choosing. Moore, Russell and Schulman showed that for $S_n$ *no* choice of
basis works: the information in a single coset state is exponentially small.

**Entangled measurements across many copies** are therefore necessary. For $S_n$
they are known to be sufficient information-theoretically, and no efficient
implementation is known. For the dihedral group, Kuperberg's sieve gives a genuine
algorithm at $2^{O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ time and queries: sub-exponential, super-
polynomial, and the best known.

Both give $000$, $001$, $110$ and $111$ at $0.25$ each. The offset moved two
amplitudes from $+0.5$ to $-0.5$ and changed no probability. That is the whole
abelian miracle in one figure: the nuisance parameter lands in a phase. In a
non-abelian group it lands in a unitary conjugation of a $d_\rho$-dimensional
block instead, and no measurement of the block label alone can undo it.

## What is at stake

| $G$ | HSP instance solves | status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S_n$, symmetric group | graph isomorphism | no efficient algorithm; strong Fourier sampling proved insufficient |
| $D_N$, dihedral group | unique shortest vector in a lattice | Kuperberg sieve at $2^{O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ |
| any abelian $G$ | factoring, discrete log, Simon | polynomial time |

Graph isomorphism was the historical prize, and it has been partly deflated:
Babai's 2015 classical algorithm runs in quasipolynomial
$\exp((\log n)^{O(1)})$ time, so a quantum polynomial algorithm would improve on
it but no longer break a wall.

The dihedral case is the one with live stakes. Regev showed that a polynomial-time
algorithm for the dihedral hidden subgroup problem would give a quantum algorithm
for the unique shortest vector problem, and lattice problems of that family are
what the post-quantum standards ML-KEM and ML-DSA rest on. The security argument
for post-quantum cryptography is therefore partly an argument that this specific
open problem stays open.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Sub-exponential is not safe, it is a parameter choice">
Kuperberg's $2^{O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ is super-polynomial, so it does not break
lattice schemes, but it is fast enough that parameter selection has to account for
it, and its space requirement is also sub-exponential. Any claim that a
post-quantum scheme has "no known quantum attack" should be read as "no known
attack better than the sieve, at these parameters". The distinction has already
moved recommended key sizes once.
</Callout>

<Check question="If someone found an efficient measurement for the symmetric-group coset state, would that put graph isomorphism in BQP?">
It would put it in BQP provided the reduction is also efficient, which it is: two
graphs on $n$ vertices are isomorphic exactly when the automorphism group of their
disjoint union contains a permutation swapping the two halves, and that group is a
hidden subgroup of $S_{2n}$ under the natural function. So yes, with the caveat
that the promise required is the *hidden subgroup* promise, and the automorphism
group is an arbitrary subgroup rather than the order-2 case usually analysed. The
hard case really is the order-2 one, which is precisely the case Moore, Russell and
Schulman showed single-copy Fourier sampling cannot see.
</Check>
