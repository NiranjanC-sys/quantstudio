The framework of the last lesson returns one uniform sample from $H^\perp$ per
query. Turning samples into a generating set is linear algebra over a finite
abelian group, and the accounting is short. What is worth more care is the second
question: what does the resulting speedup actually claim, and against what.

## Counting the queries

Each query yields a uniform element of $H^\perp$, a group of order $|G|/|H|$.
Having accumulated a subgroup $K \le H^\perp$ generated by the samples so far, the
next sample fails to enlarge $K$ exactly when it lands inside $K$, which has
probability $|K|/|H^\perp| \le 1/2$ whenever $K$ is proper. A proper subgroup has
index at least 2, so every sample has at least an even chance of at least doubling
$|K|$, and $|H^\perp| \le |G|$ means at most $\log_2|G|$ doublings are needed.

A Chernoff bound on that process gives: $O(\log|G|)$ queries suffice to generate
$H^\perp$ with probability $1 - 1/\mathrm{poly}$. For the concrete case $G =
\Z_2^{\,n}$ and $|H| = 2$, the exact expectation was computed in T2.M3.L6 and comes
to $n - 1 + 1.6067$ samples: the constant overhead never grows.

<Margin label="Two costs, not one">
Query count is $O(\log|G|)$. Gate count is that many oracle calls plus $O(\log^2|G|)$
per QFT, so also polynomial in the input length $\log|G|$, provided the oracle
itself is cheap. Order finding is the case where the oracle is *not* cheap: its
modular exponentiation is $\Theta(n^3)$ and dominates everything.
</Margin>

## Watching the annihilator shrink

Take $G = \Z_8$ on three qubits with $H = \{0, 4\}$, hidden by $f(x) = x \bmod 4$,
which is exactly the low two bits. Then $|H^\perp| = 8/2 = 4$, and

$$
H^{\perp} = \{\, y : e^{2\pi i \cdot 4y/8} = 1 \,\} = \{0, 2, 4, 6\} .
$$

Sixteen strings at $0.0625$ each; summing over the two oracle qubits, the counting
register reads $000$, $010$, $100$ or $110$ with probability $0.25$ apiece, which
is $y \in \{0, 2, 4, 6\}$. Half the samples, $y = 2$ or $y = 6$, generate the whole
annihilator on their own. $y = 4$ generates only $\{0, 4\}$ and needs a partner,
and $y = 0$ is useless. Two samples fail only when neither is a generator, which
happens with probability $(2/4)^2 = 1/4$, so two queries succeed three times in
four and three queries succeed seven times in eight.

## What the speedup claims

In the **query model** the separation is unconditional and exponential. A
classical algorithm learns nothing about $H$ until it sees a collision, and
collisions among $|G|/|H|$ distinct values require $\Omega(\sqrt{|G|/|H|})$
queries by the birthday bound. So $O(\log|G|)$ against $\Omega(\sqrt{|G|/|H|})$ is
a genuine, proven, exponential gap.

Outside the query model the claim weakens sharply, and it is important to say how.
When the "oracle" is an explicitly given function, such as $a^x \bmod N$, a
classical algorithm may exploit the formula rather than treating it as a black
box, and for factoring it does: the number field sieve runs in
$\exp(O(n^{1/3}\log^{2/3} n))$, sub-exponential rather than exponential. Nobody has
proved factoring is outside $\mathrm{P}$. So the honest statement is:

> Shor is exponentially faster than the best *known* classical algorithm, and
> unconditionally exponentially faster than any classical algorithm *in the query
> model*. Neither statement implies $\mathrm{BQP} \ne \mathrm{BPP}$.

T2.M9.L3 returns to exactly this distinction.

## Two applications that are not cryptography

**Pell's equation and the principal ideal problem.** Solving $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$ over
the integers reduces to finding the regulator of a real quadratic field, which is
a hidden subgroup problem over $\R$, the infinite abelian case. Hallgren gave a
polynomial-time quantum algorithm; the best classical algorithms are
sub-exponential. This is the cleanest example of an abelian HSP speedup with no
cryptographic motive, though solving Pell also breaks the Buchmann-Williams key
exchange.

**Decomposing a black-box abelian group.** Given a group by a black box for its
operation, find its order and its decomposition into cyclic factors. Cheung and
Mosca solve this in polynomial time by repeated order finding plus abelian HSP;
classically it needs $\Omega(\sqrt{|G|})$ group operations. Grigoriev's
self-shift-equivalence problem for multivariate polynomials is a third instance in
the same family.

<Callout tone="note" title="The list is short, and that is the point">
Thirty years after Shor, the abelian hidden subgroup problem is still where nearly
every known exponential speedup lives, and the applications outside number theory
are a handful. This is not pessimism, it is the shape of the field: an exponential
quantum speedup requires structure so specific that finding a new one is a research
result in itself. T2.M7 covers the main family that is *not* HSP-based.
</Callout>

<Check question="You are told $|G| = 2^{100}$ and $|H| = 2^{60}$. How many queries, roughly, and what is the classical cost?">
$|H^\perp| = 2^{40}$, so at most 40 doublings and $O(40)$ queries: a few hundred at
worst, and about 42 in expectation by the Simon-style accounting. Classically the
number of distinct values is $|G|/|H| = 2^{40}$, so collisions appear after about
$2^{20}$ queries, a million: not out of reach, which is a reminder that the
exponential gap is in the *exponent* and small instances stay classically easy.
Scale to $|G| = 2^{2048}$ with $|H|$ trivial and the classical cost is $2^{1024}$
while the quantum cost is a couple of thousand queries.
</Check>
