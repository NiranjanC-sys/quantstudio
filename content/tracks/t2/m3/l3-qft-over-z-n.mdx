The circuit of the last lesson factorised because $N$ was a power of two. Order
finding needs a transform over $\Z_r$ for an $r$ nobody knows in advance, and $r$
is almost never a power of two. What happens when you use the wrong group is not
a catastrophe and not a detail: it is a specific, bounded loss of probability, and
the whole of Shor's post-processing exists to clean it up.

## The transform over a cyclic group

For any finite abelian group $G$ the Fourier transform is defined by the
characters of $G$, the homomorphisms $\chi: G \to \C^\times$. For the cyclic group
$\Z_N$ the characters are $\chi_k(j) = e^{2\pi i jk/N}$, indexed by $k \in \Z_N$,
and the transform is

$$
F_{\Z_N}\ket{j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k \in \Z_N} e^{2\pi i jk/N}\ket{k} .
$$

This is the same formula as before with no assumption that $N = 2^n$. When $N$ *is*
a power of two, $\Z_N \cong \Z_2^{\,n}$ as a set of bitstrings and the exponent
splits across bits, which is what gave the product form. For other $N$ the group
does not decompose that way and the seven-gate circuit does not exist. Efficient
exact circuits for $F_{\Z_N}$ do exist, by Kitaev's construction and its
refinements, but they are considerably more work and almost nobody builds them.

<Margin label="Why $\Z_2^n$ is the easy case">
$\Z_2^{\,n}$ is a product of $n$ copies of $\Z_2$, and the Fourier transform of a
product group is the tensor product of the transforms. The transform over $\Z_2$
is the Hadamard. So Fourier sampling over $\Z_2^{\,n}$, which is Bernstein-Vazirani
and Simon, needs nothing but $H^{\otimes n}$: no rotations, no swaps, depth 1.
Everything expensive about the QFT comes from $\Z_{2^n}$ being a *cyclic* group of
order $2^n$ rather than a product of small ones.
</Margin>

## What order finding actually needs

Order finding produces a register in a superposition over an arithmetic
progression: the values $x_0, x_0 + r, x_0 + 2r, \dots$ modulo the register size.
The period $r$ is the answer. If you had a register of size exactly $r$, or any
multiple of $r$, the transform would put all the weight on exact multiples of
$2^t/r$ and reading a peak would give $r$ immediately.

You do not. You have $t$ qubits, so a register of size $2^t$, and $r$ divides
$2^t$ only if $r$ is itself a power of two. What comes out of the QFT is a
distribution peaked *near* the multiples of $2^t/r$ rather than on them, and the
peaks have skirts.

## The cost of the mismatch, exactly

Consider the cleanest form of the problem. Feed the transform a state whose
amplitudes are a pure phase ramp at frequency $\varphi$:

$$
\ket{\Psi_\varphi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^t-1} e^{2\pi i \varphi j}\ket{j} ,
$$

which is a product state, one qubit per binary place, and which the inverse QFT
maps back to $\ket{2^t \varphi}$ when $2^t\varphi$ is an integer. Take $t = 3$ and
$\varphi = 5/8$, exactly representable in three bits.

The probability of the nearest outcome $\ket{011}$ is $0.687838$, and the second
nearest $\ket{010}$ takes $0.174940$; the remaining six outcomes share $0.137222$
between them. In closed form, writing $\delta = \varphi - k/2^t$ for the offset
from the outcome $k$,

$$
\Pr[k] = \frac{1}{2^{2t}}\left|\frac{\sin(\pi 2^t \delta)}{\sin(\pi \delta)}\right|^2 ,
$$

a Dirichlet kernel: a central lobe with $1/\delta^2$ tails.

## The bound worth remembering

For any $\varphi$, the outcome nearest to $2^t\varphi$ has probability at least

$$
\frac{4}{\pi^2} = 0.405285 .
$$

The worst case is $\varphi$ exactly halfway between two representable values, and
even then the best outcome carries more than two fifths of the weight. Since
$4/\pi^2$ is a constant, a constant number of repetitions gives the nearest value
with any confidence you like. Combined with a register of $t \ge 2n$ bits, that is
enough for the continued-fraction step of T2.M5.L3 to recover $r$ exactly.

<Callout tone="note" title="Never use the wrong group deliberately">
Approximating $\Z_r$ by $\Z_{2^t}$ is forced on you because $r$ is unknown. When
the group *is* known and is not a power of two, use the right transform. The
dihedral hidden subgroup problem of T2.M6.L3 is the standard cautionary tale:
substituting a nearby abelian group there does not merely lose a constant, it
loses the structure the algorithm was going to exploit.
</Callout>

<Check question="You run the leaky circuit above once and read $\ket{010}$, which is $2/8 = 0.25$ rather than the true $1/3$. Have you failed?">
Not necessarily. A single sample is a draw from the Dirichlet distribution, and
$0.174940$ of the time it lands on the second-nearest value. With three bits you
cannot tell $0.25$ from $0.333$ anyway, which is the real problem: the register is
too short. The fix is more bits, not more shots. Adding bits narrows the spacing
$2^{-t}$ so that the nearest value is genuinely close to $\varphi$, and the
$4/\pi^2$ bound then guarantees that a handful of shots pins it down. This is the
precision-versus-confidence split that T2.M4.L3 makes precise.
</Check>
