Deutsch-Jozsa, Bernstein-Vazirani, Simon, order finding and discrete log look like
five different algorithms. They are one algorithm with five oracles. This lesson
writes down the shared template, checks it against the case you already know, and
then does the case that matters: what comes out when the hidden structure is a
whole subgroup rather than a single string.

## The template

Four steps, always in this order.

1. **Prepare** the uniform superposition over the group $G$:
   $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{x \in G}\ket{x}$.
2. **Query** the oracle once, entangling a second register with $f(x)$.
3. **Transform** the first register with the Fourier transform of $G$.
4. **Measure** the first register, and post-process classically.

The whole design question is which group $G$ makes step 3 cheap and step 4
informative. For $G = \Z_2^{\,n}$ the transform is $H^{\otimes n}$, depth 1. For
$G = \Z_{2^t}$ it is the QFT circuit of T2.M3.L2.

<Margin label="Why the second register is discarded">
Step 2 entangles the input register with $f$, and step 3 only touches the input
register. Whether you measure the output register or throw it away is
mathematically irrelevant, by the principle of deferred measurement from T1.M8.L2.
Analyses usually say "measure it" because collapsing onto a definite $f$ value
leaves the input register in a clean coset state, which is easier to reason about
than the entangled whole.
</Margin>

## Bernstein-Vazirani is the one-string case

Take $f(x) = s \cdot x \bmod 2$ for a hidden $s \in \{0,1\}^n$. Use the phase form
of the oracle, so step 2 produces

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x (-1)^{s\cdot x}\ket{x} .
$$

That state is exactly $H^{\otimes n}\ket{s}$, because
$H^{\otimes n}\ket{s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x (-1)^{s \cdot x}\ket{x}$. Step 3
applies $H^{\otimes n}$ again, and $H^{\otimes n}$ is its own inverse, so the
register is $\ket{s}$ and step 4 reads $s$ with certainty from one query. A
classical algorithm needs $n$ queries, one per bit.

## The coset case

Now suppose $f$ is not a linear form but is **constant on cosets** of a subgroup
$H \le \Z_2^{\,n}$, and distinct on distinct cosets. Use the bit-flip oracle.
After step 2 and a measurement of the output register, the input register collapses
onto a uniform superposition over one coset:

$$
\ket{x_0 + H} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|H|}}\sum_{h \in H}\ket{x_0 + h} ,
$$

with the offset $x_0$ uniformly random and unknown. Apply $H^{\otimes n}$:

$$
H^{\otimes n}\ket{x_0 + H}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n |H|}}\sum_{y}(-1)^{x_0 \cdot y}
\left(\sum_{h \in H}(-1)^{h \cdot y}\right)\ket{y} .
$$

The inner sum is the character sum over a subgroup: it equals $|H|$ when
$h \cdot y = 0$ for every $h \in H$, and 0 otherwise, because otherwise exactly
half the terms are $+1$ and half $-1$. Define the **annihilator**

$$
H^{\perp} = \{\, y : h\cdot y = 0 \text{ for all } h \in H \,\} ,
$$

a subgroup of size $2^n/|H|$. Then the state is

$$
\sqrt{\frac{|H|}{2^n}}\sum_{y \in H^\perp}(-1)^{x_0 \cdot y}\ket{y} ,
$$

and measuring gives a **uniform random element of $H^\perp$**, with the unknown
offset $x_0$ pushed entirely into signs that measurement cannot see.

That is the payoff line of the whole module. One query, one Hadamard layer, and
you get a free uniform sample from the orthogonal complement of the hidden
subgroup. Collect enough samples and linear algebra gives you $H$.

## An instance you can check

Let $n = 3$ and $f(x) = x_0 \oplus x_1 \oplus x_2$, the parity. Parity is constant
on cosets of the even-weight subgroup

$$
H = \{000,\, 011,\, 101,\, 110\}, \qquad |H| = 4 ,
$$

so $H^\perp$ has size $8/4 = 2$. Solving $y \cdot h = 0$ for the generators $011$
and $101$ gives $y_1 = y_2$ and $y_0 = y_2$, so $H^\perp = \{000, 111\}$. The
prediction is therefore: the query register reads $000$ or $111$, each with
probability $0.5$, and nothing else ever.

Four surviving strings at $0.25$ each: $\ket{0000}$, $\ket{0001}$, $\ket{1110}$ and

the two annihilator elements has probability $0.5$. The sign on $\ket{1111}$ is the
$(-1)^{x_0 \cdot y}$ factor, and it carries no information you can measure.

<Callout tone="note" title="The bigger the hidden subgroup, the smaller the sample space">
$|H^\perp| = 2^n/|H|$. A one-element $H$ leaves $H^\perp$ everything, and you learn
nothing. A large $H$ leaves few candidates, and each sample is worth a lot. Simon's
case, $H = \{0, s\}$, sits at the informative end of the useless end: $H^\perp$ has
$2^{n-1}$ elements, and each sample is one linear equation on $s$.
</Callout>

<Check question="Run the parity circuit and you read $000$. How much have you learned about $H$?">
Nothing. $000$ lies in every annihilator, so the equation $h \cdot 000 = 0$ is
satisfied by every $h$ and constrains nothing. This is the wasted-sample problem
that every Fourier-sampling algorithm has: the all-zeros outcome occurs with
probability $|H|/2^n$ and is always uninformative. In Simon's case that is
$2/2^n$, negligible; in this parity instance it is $1/2$, which is why a subgroup
that large is useless to sample. The general rule is that you need $\log_2|H^\perp|$
independent non-zero samples, and the expected cost of collecting them is the
subject of the next lesson.
</Check>
