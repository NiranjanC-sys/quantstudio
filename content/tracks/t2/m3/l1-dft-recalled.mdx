Every exponential quantum speedup that is not Simon's algorithm runs through one
matrix. Before building it out of gates it is worth being precise about what it
does classically, because the quantum version computes exactly the same transform
and the entire difficulty is that you cannot read the answer out.

## The matrix

Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i / N}$, a primitive $N$-th root of unity, so $\omega^N = 1$
and $\omega^m \ne 1$ for $0 < m < N$. The **discrete Fourier transform** on $N$
points is the matrix

$$
F_{jk} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\, \omega^{jk}, \qquad 0 \le j, k < N ,
$$

which sends a vector $x$ to $\hat x_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_j x_j \omega^{jk}$.
The $1/\sqrt N$ is the normalisation that makes it unitary; engineering
conventions often put $1/N$ on one side and nothing on the other, which is fine
for signal processing and useless here.

Unitarity is one geometric series. The $(j, j')$ entry of $F F\dg$ is

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{jk}\overline{\omega^{j'k}}
= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{(j-j')k} .
$$

If $j = j'$ every term is 1 and the sum is $N$, giving 1. If $j \ne j'$ then
$\omega^{j-j'} \ne 1$ and the geometric series closes:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} z^k = \frac{z^N - 1}{z - 1} = 0
\quad \text{with } z = \omega^{j-j'} ,
$$

since $z^N = \omega^{N(j-j')} = 1$. So $F F\dg = I$: the roots of unity cancel
each other exactly, which is the only fact about them this module ever needs.

<Margin label="One symbol, two readings">
$F$ is symmetric, $F^{T} = F$, so there is no ambiguity about which index is
"time" and which is "frequency": the transform looks the same both ways. The
inverse is $F\dg$, which replaces $\omega$ by $\overline\omega = \omega^{-1}$.
Getting the sign of that exponent wrong is the single most common bug in a
hand-written QFT, and it is invisible on real-valued inputs.
</Margin>

## A periodic sequence and its peaks

Take $N = 8$ and the sequence supported at $j = 0$ and $j = 4$, normalised:

$$
x = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(e_0 + e_4\big) .
$$

It is periodic with period 4. Its transform is

$$
\hat x_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt8}\left(\omega^{0} + \omega^{4k}\right)
= \frac14\left(1 + (-1)^k\right)
$$

because $\omega^4 = e^{i\pi} = -1$. So $\hat x_k = 1/2$ for even $k$ and $0$ for
odd $k$: four equal peaks at $k = 0, 2, 4, 6$, spaced $N/4 = 2$ apart.

That spacing is the rule. **A period of $r$ in $j$ becomes peaks spaced $N/r$
apart in $k$**, whenever $r$ divides $N$. Period 4 gives 4 peaks two apart; period
2 would give 2 peaks four apart. This reciprocity is the entire mechanism of
order finding: hide a period in a register, transform, and read a peak whose
position tells you the period.

The quantum version of the same computation, on three qubits, prepares
$(\ket{000} + \ket{100})/\sqrt2$ with a single Hadamard on the most significant
qubit and then applies the QFT.

## What the quantum version changes, and what it does not

The naive matrix-vector product costs $O(N^2)$ multiplications. The fast Fourier
transform exploits the factorisation of $\omega^{jk}$ into even and odd index
halves, recursing to

$$
T(N) = 2T(N/2) + O(N) = O(N \log N) .
$$

The quantum Fourier transform on $n = \log_2 N$ qubits does the *same* linear
algebra with $O(n^2) = O(\log^2 N)$ gates, exponentially fewer operations than
even the FFT. That sounds like an exponential speedup for signal processing. It is
not, for two reasons that you should memorise now and re-derive in the next
lesson.

First, the input has to already be a quantum state. Loading $N$ classical numbers
into amplitudes costs $\Omega(N)$ unless the vector has structure a circuit can
generate. Second, and fatally, the output is a quantum state. You cannot read
$\hat x_k$; you can only measure, which returns one index $k$ drawn with
probability $|\hat x_k|^2$, and destroys the state.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="The QFT is not a subroutine for computing Fourier transforms">
If you want the coefficients of a signal, the QFT gives you nothing: extracting
all $N$ of them to reasonable accuracy needs $\Omega(N)$ measurements, wiping out
the advantage. The QFT is useful only when the answer you want is a *single
property* of the spectrum, robust enough to survive being sampled. In practice
that means one thing: the spectrum has a small number of sharp peaks and you want
their positions. Order finding, phase estimation and Simon are all instances of
that one pattern.
</Callout>

<Check question="You transform a length-8 sequence and find peaks at $k = 0$ and $k = 4$ only. What was the period, and how would you distinguish that from a sequence with peaks at every $k$?">
Peaks spaced 4 apart mean $N/r = 4$, so $r = 2$: the sequence repeats every two
positions. A spectrum with weight at every $k$ means the opposite extreme, a
sequence concentrated at a single $j$, since a delta in one domain is flat in the
other. The two extremes are the reason sampling works at all: a hidden period
produces a spectrum with $r$ sharp spikes, so a single measurement lands on one of
them, whereas a spectrum spread over all $N$ points tells you almost nothing per
sample.
</Check>
