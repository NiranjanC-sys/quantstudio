Simon's algorithm is the smallest exponential separation that survives
randomisation, and it is the historical seed of Shor: reading it, Shor asked what
the same trick would do over $\Z_N$ instead of $\Z_2^n$. Seen through the previous
lesson it needs no new ideas at all. It is coset Fourier sampling with the
smallest interesting subgroup.

## The problem and its subgroup

$f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n$ is promised to be two-to-one with a hidden mask
$s \ne 0$:

$$
f(x) = f(y) \iff y = x \oplus s .
$$

Find $s$. In the language of T2.M3.L5, $f$ is constant on the cosets of the
subgroup $H = \{0, s\}$, which has $|H| = 2$. So one query of the coset-sampling
routine returns a uniform element of

$$
H^\perp = \{\, y : y \cdot s = 0 \,\} ,
$$

a subspace of dimension $n-1$ containing $2^{n-1}$ strings. Each sample is one
linear equation $y \cdot s = 0$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, and $n-1$ independent
equations pin $s$ down to a one-dimensional solution space whose only non-zero
element is $s$ itself.

<Margin label="Why classical algorithms are stuck">
Classically you learn nothing until you see a collision, and collisions among
$2^{n-1}$ distinct output values arrive only after about $2^{n/2}$ queries by the
birthday bound. The lower bound is $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ and it holds for randomised
algorithms with bounded error, which is what makes Simon a genuine exponential
separation rather than an exact-computation curiosity like Deutsch-Jozsa.
</Margin>

## A three-bit instance

Take $s = 110$ and define $f(x) = x \oplus (x_0 \cdot s)$, that is, XOR $s$ into
the output whenever the leading bit of $x$ is 1.

| $x$ | $000$ | $001$ | $010$ | $011$ | $100$ | $101$ | $110$ | $111$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f(x)$ | $000$ | $001$ | $010$ | $011$ | $010$ | $011$ | $000$ | $001$ |

Four values, each hit twice, and the two preimages of every value differ by $110$.
The circuit copies $x$ into the output register with three CNOTs and then XORs $s$
into it controlled on $x_0$, which is two more CNOTs. The copy and the conditional
XOR both touch qubit 3, so those two gates cancel; leaving both in makes the
construction legible.

Sixteen surviving strings at $0.0625$ each. Read the first three bits of each and
you get only $000$, $001$, $110$ and $111$, four values with four output strings
apiece, so $0.25$ each. Check against the prediction: $y \cdot 110 = y_0 \oplus y_1$,
which is 0 exactly for those four. The other four strings, $010$, $011$, $100$ and
$101$, never appear.

## Solving the linear system

Suppose two runs return $y^{(1)} = 001$ and $y^{(2)} = 111$. Write the equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0\cdot s_0 + 0 \cdot s_1 + 1 \cdot s_2 &= 0 \\
1\cdot s_0 + 1 \cdot s_1 + 1 \cdot s_2 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The first gives $s_2 = 0$. Substituting into the second gives $s_0 \oplus s_1 = 0$,
so $s_0 = s_1$. The solution space is $\{000, 110\}$, and the promise says
$s \ne 0$, so $s = 110$. Two samples sufficed here; had the second been $000$ or a
repeat of the first, it would have added no rank and another run would have been
needed.

## How many samples

Samples are uniform on an $(n-1)$-dimensional space. Having collected $k$
independent vectors, the next one is dependent exactly when it lands in their span,
which has $2^k$ of the $2^{n-1}$ elements. So the expected number of runs to reach
rank $n-1$ is

$$
E_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} \frac{2^{n-1}}{2^{n-1} - 2^{k}} .
$$

| $n$ | $E_n$ | overhead $E_n - (n-1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $3$ | $3.3333$ | $1.3333$ |
| $4$ | $4.4762$ | $1.4762$ |
| $8$ | $8.5989$ | $1.5989$ |
| $64$ | $64.6067$ | $1.6067$ |

The overhead converges to about $1.6067$ and never grows, so $n + 2$ runs are
comfortably enough in practice. Total cost: $O(n)$ queries and one $O(n^3)$
Gaussian elimination, against $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ classically.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Independence is over GF(2), not over the reals">
The vectors $001$, $110$ and $111$ are linearly *dependent* over
$\mathrm{GF}(2)$, since $001 \oplus 110 = 111$, even though as real vectors they
are independent. Rank must be computed with XOR arithmetic throughout. Using a
floating-point rank routine on Simon samples is a real bug that produces a
confident wrong $s$, and it passes every test in which $n$ is small enough that
the sample happened to be independent both ways.
</Callout>

<Check question="You run the circuit above four times and get $000$, $111$, $111$, $001$. Can you determine $s$, and how many of the four runs were useful?">
Yes. Discard $000$, which is always a solution and constrains nothing, and discard
the repeat of $111$. What remains is $\{111, 001\}$, rank 2, which is $n - 1 = 2$,
and the elimination above gives $s = 110$. Two of the four runs were useful, which
is close to the expected $E_3 = 3.3333$ runs for rank 2. Note that you cannot tell
in advance which runs will be useful, so the algorithm's cost is a random variable;
the guarantee is on its expectation and, via a Chernoff bound, on its tail.
</Check>
