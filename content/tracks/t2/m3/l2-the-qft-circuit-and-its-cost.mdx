An $N \times N$ matrix normally needs $N^2$ numbers to describe and $N^2$
multiplications to apply. The QFT needs $O(\log^2 N)$ gates. That collapse is not
magic and it is not compression: it is the observation that $\omega^{jk}$
factorises across the bits of $k$, so the transform is a *product* of
single-qubit states and therefore a product of single-qubit gates.

## The factorisation

Work on $n$ qubits, $N = 2^n$, with qubit 0 the most significant bit. Write the
output index in binary as $k = k_1 k_2 \cdots k_n$, so that

$$
\frac{k}{N} = \sum_{l=1}^{n} k_l\, 2^{-l} .
$$

Substitute into the definition:

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{j}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k} e^{2\pi i j k / N}\ket{k}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k_1 \cdots k_n} \prod_{l=1}^{n} e^{2\pi i j k_l 2^{-l}} \ket{k_1 \cdots k_n} .
$$

The sum over all $k$ is a sum over each bit independently, and the summand is a
product with one factor per bit, so the sum factorises:

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{j}
= \bigotimes_{l=1}^{n} \frac{\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i j / 2^{l}}\ket{1}}{\sqrt2} .
$$

**The transform of a basis state is a product state.** No entanglement is created
at all, which is exactly why $n$ separate qubits can hold the answer.

Now note that $e^{2\pi i j / 2^l}$ only depends on $j \bmod 2^l$, that is, on the
*last* $l$ bits of $j$. Writing $0.j_a j_{a+1}\cdots$ for the binary fraction,
factor $l$ is

$$
\frac{\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i\, 0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n}\ket{1}}{\sqrt2} .
$$

<Margin label="Where the reversal comes from">
Factor 1 depends on one bit of $j$, the last; factor $n$ depends on all $n$. The
circuit naturally builds the all-bits factor on qubit 0 and the one-bit factor on
qubit $n-1$, which is the reverse of the order the formula wants. Hence the swap
layer at the end. Some implementations drop the swaps and relabel the wires
instead, which is free on paper and free in a simulator, and costs real routing on
hardware.
</Margin>

## The circuit

Apply $H$ to qubit 0. That turns $\ket{j_1}$ into
$(\ket 0 + e^{2\pi i\, 0.j_1}\ket 1)/\sqrt2$, because $e^{2\pi i \cdot 0.j_1}$ is
$+1$ when $j_1 = 0$ and $-1$ when $j_1 = 1$. Then, for each later qubit $m$, apply
a controlled phase $R_{m+1}$ with angle $2\pi/2^{m+1}$, controlled by qubit $m$
and targeting qubit 0. Each one appends the next binary digit, until qubit 0 holds
$0.j_1 j_2 \cdots j_n$. Repeat starting at qubit 1, then qubit 2, and finish with
the swap layer.

Read the amplitudes as $\frac{1}{\sqrt8}e^{2\pi i k / 8}$: $0.353553$ at $k = 0$,
$0.25 + 0.25i$ at $k = 1$, pure $0.353553 i$ at $k = 2$, $-0.353553$ at $k = 4$,
and so on around the circle. That phase ramp is the entire content of the
transform, and it is entirely invisible in the probability column.

## The gate count

| ingredient | count |
| --- | --- |
| Hadamards | $n$ |
| controlled phases | $n(n-1)/2$ |
| swaps | $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ |

For $n = 3$ that is 3, 3 and 1, which is the seven-gate circuit above. For
$n = 10$ it is 10, 45 and 5. The controlled-phase term dominates, so the QFT is
$\Theta(n^2)$ gates, or $\Theta(\log^2 N)$ in the dimension. Depth is also
$\Theta(n)$ if you schedule the rotations greedily, since disjoint pairs commute.

The trouble arrives at scale. Factoring a 2048-bit modulus needs a QFT on roughly
$2n = 4096$ qubits, hence about $8.4$ million controlled-phase gates, and the
smallest of those rotations is by $2\pi/2^{4096}$, an angle no physical device can
distinguish from zero. Both problems are solved by the same observation, which is
the subject of T2.M3.L4.

## Why this is not yet an algorithm

The QFT computes the transform of whatever amplitude vector you hand it. It does
not hand you the answer. Two obstructions, both fundamental:

**Input.** Preparing an arbitrary $N$-amplitude state costs $\Omega(N)$ gates in
general. The states the QFT is used on in practice are never arbitrary: they are
periodic superpositions produced by an oracle, or phase ramps produced by
controlled unitaries, both of which a small circuit generates.

**Output.** The result is a state, and measuring it samples one index $k$ with
probability $|\hat x_k|^2$. To learn all the coefficients you would need
$\Omega(N)$ samples and full tomography. So the QFT is only ever the *second to
last* step of an algorithm, and the last step is always a measurement whose single
outcome is already the answer.

<Callout tone="note" title="The pattern to look for">
Every QFT-based algorithm has the same shape: get a state whose amplitudes carry a
periodicity or a phase, transform, measure once, post-process classically. Simon,
Bernstein-Vazirani, order finding, discrete log and phase estimation are all that
shape with different first steps. When you meet a new "quantum Fourier" proposal,
find those four stages; if the third one needs many samples of many different
coefficients, the speedup is not there.
</Callout>

<Check question="You apply the QFT to $\ket{000}$ on three qubits. What comes out, and how many gates did the circuit waste?">
$\mathrm{QFT}\ket{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt8}\sum_k \ket{k}$, the uniform superposition,
identical to what three Hadamards produce. Every controlled phase acted trivially
because the control qubit was $\ket 0$, so all three rotations and the swap were
wasted: four of the seven gates did nothing. This is not a curiosity but the base
case of the whole construction, and it is a useful test: if your QFT
implementation does not reduce to $H^{\otimes n}$ on the all-zeros input, the
controls or the angles are wrong.
</Check>
