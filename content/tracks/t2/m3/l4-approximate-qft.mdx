A QFT on 4096 qubits contains a controlled rotation by $2\pi/2^{4096}$. No device
will ever apply that gate: the angle is smaller than the numerical precision of
the control electronics by thousands of orders of magnitude, and smaller than the
gate error by more still. The honest response is not to apply it, and it turns out
that throwing away all the small rotations costs almost nothing while removing
almost all of the gates.

## The cutoff

In the circuit of T2.M3.L2, the controlled phase between qubits $a$ and $b$ has
angle $2\pi/2^{\,b-a+1}$. Call $l = b-a+1$ the **level** of the rotation. Level 2
is a quarter turn, level 3 an eighth, and level $l$ shrinks geometrically. The
approximate QFT with cutoff $m$, due to Coppersmith, keeps every rotation of level
at most $m$ and deletes the rest.

How many survive? Qubit $a$ controls rotations at levels $2$ through $n-a$, of
which at most $m-1$ are kept, so

$$
\text{kept} = \sum_{a=0}^{n-1} \min(n-1-a,\; m-1) \approx n(m-1) - \frac{m(m-1)}{2} = O(nm) .
$$

Choosing $m = O(\log n)$ leaves $O(n \log n)$ rotations instead of
$n(n-1)/2 = \Theta(n^2)$.

## The error, bounded and measured

Coppersmith's bound on the operator norm of the difference is

$$
\big\| \mathrm{QFT}_n - \mathrm{AQFT}_{n,m} \big\| \;\le\; \frac{2\pi n}{2^{m}} .
$$

The reasoning is a triangle inequality: each deleted gate is a rotation by
$\theta_l = 2\pi/2^{l}$, so it differs from the identity by at most $\theta_l$ in
norm, and summing $\theta_l$ over the at most $n$ deleted rotations per qubit and
$n$ qubits gives $2\pi n / 2^m$ after the geometric series is summed.

Measured against the true transform, on eight qubits:

| cutoff $m$ | rotations kept | measured $\|\Delta\|$ | Coppersmith bound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $4$ | $18$ of $28$ | $1.131464$ | $3.141593$ |
| $5$ | $22$ of $28$ | $0.414223$ | $1.570796$ |
| $6$ | $25$ of $28$ | $0.122641$ | $0.785398$ |
| $7$ | $27$ of $28$ | $0.024543$ | $0.392699$ |

The bound is loose by a factor of three or so, and it is a bound, which is what
matters. Note how fast the error falls: each extra level of cutoff roughly
quarters it while costing $n$ more gates.

<Margin label="Inverse-polynomial is enough">
Shor needs the transform accurate to within an inverse polynomial in $n$, because
the whole algorithm already succeeds only with constant probability and is
repeated. Setting $2^m = n^{c+1}$, so $m = (c+1)\log_2 n$, gives error
$2\pi/n^{c}$ with $O(n\log n)$ rotations. The asymptotic cost of Shor's Fourier
step is therefore $\tilde O(n)$, not $O(n^2)$, and the modular exponentiation
dominates by a wide margin.
</Margin>

## Seeing the cutoff bite

Three qubits is small enough that a cutoff of $m = 2$, which keeps only the
quarter turns and deletes the eighth turn, is a visible mutilation. Applied to
$\ket{001}$, the exact transform walks the amplitudes around the circle in steps
of 45 degrees. The approximate one walks in steps of 90 degrees and visits each
twice.

$0.853553$ instead of $1$, which is $(2+\sqrt2)/4$. On three qubits a cutoff of 2

probability, recoverable by repetition, not a wrong answer.

## Why hardware prefers the approximation

Three arguments, all of them practical.

A rotation by an angle far below the device's control resolution is not a small
gate, it is a *random* gate: what the hardware actually applies is dominated by
calibration error. Deleting it replaces a random perturbation with the identity,
which is strictly closer to the intended operation.

Every gate you apply costs decoherence time. Removing $\Theta(n^2)$ gates and
keeping $O(n\log n)$ shortens the circuit by orders of magnitude at scale. For
$n = 4096$ the exact QFT has $8{,}386{,}560$ controlled phases; at cutoff
$m = 25$ it has $98{,}004$, a factor of 86 fewer, with an error bound of
$7.67 \times 10^{-4}$.

And the depth improves as well, since the surviving rotations on a given qubit
form a short window that schedules in $O(m)$ layers rather than $O(n)$.

<Callout tone="note" title="Approximation before, not after">
Compile the approximation into the algorithm rather than letting a transpiler
discover it. A transpiler that drops small rotations is applying an unbounded
heuristic; Coppersmith's cutoff comes with the norm bound above, so you can carry
the error through the rest of the analysis and state the final success probability
honestly. The habit generalises: an approximation you chose is auditable, one your
toolchain chose is not.
</Callout>

<Check question="You are told a 20-qubit QFT must be accurate to operator norm $10^{-3}$. What cutoff does the Coppersmith bound demand, and how many rotations does that leave?">
Solve $2\pi \cdot 20 / 2^m \le 10^{-3}$, so $2^m \ge 125664$ and $m = 17$. With
$n = 20$ and $m = 17$ the kept count is
$\sum_a \min(19-a, 16) = 184$ out of $190$, barely a saving. The lesson is that
the approximation only pays at scale: the cutoff needed grows like $\log n$ plus
$\log(1/\varepsilon)$, and when $n$ is 20 that is already most of $n$. At
$n = 4096$ the same accuracy target costs a cutoff of 25 and saves 98.8% of the
gates.
</Check>
