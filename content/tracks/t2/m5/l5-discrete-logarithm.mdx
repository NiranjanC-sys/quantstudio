Factoring is not the only public-key assumption Shor broke. The same paper
disposed of the discrete logarithm, which underpins Diffie-Hellman, DSA and every
elliptic-curve signature scheme in use today. The circuit is order finding with
one extra register, and the reason it also kills elliptic curves is that it never
looks inside the group.

## The problem

Let $G$ be a cyclic group of known order $r$, generated by $g$, written
multiplicatively. Given $x = g^{m}$, find $m$. Classically the best general
algorithms are the baby-step giant-step and Pollard rho methods at
$O(\sqrt r)$ operations, and for the multiplicative group modulo a prime, index
calculus at sub-exponential cost. Both are what current key sizes are chosen to
defeat.

## Two registers, one function

Define a function of two variables:

$$
f(a, b) = g^{a} x^{-b} = g^{\,a - mb} .
$$

$f$ is constant exactly when $a - mb$ is constant modulo $r$, so it is constant on
cosets of the subgroup

$$
H = \{\,(mb,\, b) : b \in \Z_r\,\} = \langle (m, 1) \rangle \;\le\; \Z_r \times \Z_r .
$$

Finding $m$ is finding a generator of $H$. That is a hidden subgroup problem over
$\Z_r \times \Z_r$, and T2.M3.L5 already tells you the answer: Fourier sampling
returns a uniform element of the annihilator

$$
H^{\perp} = \{\,(\alpha, \beta) : \alpha m + \beta \equiv 0 \pmod r \,\} .
$$

So the circuit is: two counting registers over $\Z_r$, both in uniform
superposition, a third register computing $g^a x^{-b}$ by controlled
multiplications, an inverse QFT over $\Z_r$ on *each* counting register, and a
measurement returning the pair $(\alpha, \beta)$.

<Margin label="One equation, one unknown">
Each sample is the single linear congruence $\alpha m + \beta \equiv 0
\pmod r$. If $\gcd(\alpha, r) = 1$ then $\alpha$ is invertible and
$m \equiv -\beta\,\alpha^{-1} \pmod r$ from *one* sample. That happens with
probability $\varphi(r)/r$, which for a prime-order group is $1 - 1/r$: almost
always. Compare Simon, where you needed $n-1$ independent samples; here the
unknown is a single number, not a bit vector.
</Margin>

## A four-element instance

Take $G = \langle 7 \rangle$ inside the units modulo 15, whose order is $r = 4$,
and let $x = 4$. Since $7^2 \equiv 4$, the answer is $m = 2$. Because $r = 4$ is a
power of two, the QFT over $\Z_4$ is the two-qubit circuit you already have.

Qubits 0 and 1 hold $a$, qubits 2 and 3 hold $b$, qubits 4 to 7 hold the group
element, loaded with 1. The controlled multipliers are precomputed classically:
$g^{2} \equiv 4$ and $g^{1} \equiv 7$ for the $a$ register, and
$x^{-1} \equiv 4$ and $x^{-2} \equiv 1$ for the $b$ register, so the most
significant qubit of the $b$ register controls the identity and drops out.

Read the first two bits as $\alpha$ and the next two as $\beta$. The four
surviving pairs are

$$
(0,0), \quad (1,2), \quad (2,0), \quad (3,2) ,
$$

each with marginal probability $0.25$. Check them against
$\alpha m + \beta \equiv 0 \pmod 4$ with $m = 2$: $2\cdot0+0 = 0$,
$2\cdot1+2 = 4 \equiv 0$, $2\cdot2+0 = 4 \equiv 0$, $2\cdot3+2 = 8 \equiv 0$. All
four satisfy it and no other pair does.

Now solve. From $(1, 2)$: $\alpha = 1$ is invertible modulo 4, so
$m \equiv -2 \cdot 1^{-1} \equiv 2$. From $(3, 2)$: $3^{-1} \equiv 3$ modulo 4, so
$m \equiv -2 \cdot 3 = -6 \equiv 2$. The pairs with $\alpha \in \{0, 2\}$ are
useless because $\alpha$ is not invertible modulo 4; here that is half the
outcomes, which is the price of a composite group order. For a prime-order group,
which is what real cryptography uses, essentially every sample works.

## Why elliptic curves fall too

Nothing in the derivation used what the group elements *are*. It used only that
you can multiply by a classical constant reversibly, that the group is cyclic of
known order, and that you can Fourier transform over $\Z_r$. Replace
multiplication modulo $p$ by point addition on an elliptic curve and every step
survives verbatim.

| target | classical best | quantum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| factoring, $n$-bit modulus | sub-exponential | $O(n^3)$ gates |
| finite-field discrete log | sub-exponential | $O(n^3)$ gates |
| elliptic-curve discrete log | $O(2^{n/2})$ | $O(n^3)$ gates |

Elliptic curves are the *worst* affected, because they were chosen precisely for
having no sub-exponential classical attack, so their key sizes are small. A
256-bit curve gives 128 bits of classical security against Pollard rho; against
Shor it is a 256-bit group like any other. Roetteler, Naehrig, Svore and Lauter
estimated a 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete log at roughly $2330$ logical qubits
and $1.26 \times 10^{11}$ Toffoli gates, which is *cheaper* than factoring
RSA-2048.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Small keys are not safer, they are sooner">
The instinct that a 256-bit key is a smaller target than a 2048-bit key is exactly
backwards under Shor, whose cost is polynomial in the key length. Elliptic-curve
signatures will fall before RSA does, not after. This is the whole argument for
post-quantum migration timelines, and it is also why "harvest now, decrypt later"
is a real threat model for anything signed or encrypted today that must stay
confidential into the 2040s.
</Callout>

<Check question="Your group has order $r = 2^{k}$ and you sample $(\alpha, \beta)$ with $\alpha$ even. What can you still extract?">
A partial answer. $\alpha m \equiv -\beta \pmod{2^k}$ with $\alpha = 2^{j}u$ and
$u$ odd determines $m$ only modulo $2^{k-j}$, and requires $2^j \mid \beta$ for a
solution to exist at all. So an even $\alpha$ pins down the low bits of $m$ and
leaves the high ones free. Collect a few samples and combine them: the one with the
smallest $j$ determines the most. In a prime-order group this never arises, which
is one more reason cryptographic groups are chosen with prime order.
</Check>
