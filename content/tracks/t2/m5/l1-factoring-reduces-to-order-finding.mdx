There is no quantum factoring algorithm. There is a quantum *order-finding*
algorithm, and a classical reduction, known since the 1970s and entirely
independent of quantum mechanics, that turns order finding into factoring. Shor's
contribution was to notice that the quantum part of the problem was the part
phase estimation already solves. Getting the reduction straight first is what
makes the quantum circuit in T2.M5.L4 legible.

## The reduction

To factor an odd composite $N$ that is not a prime power:

1. Pick $a$ uniformly at random in $\{2, \dots, N-1\}$.
2. Compute $g = \gcd(a, N)$. If $g > 1$ you got lucky and $g$ is a factor; stop.
3. Otherwise find the **multiplicative order** $r$ of $a$ modulo $N$, the least
   $r \ge 1$ with $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$.
4. If $r$ is odd, go back to step 1.
5. If $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod N$, go back to step 1.
6. Return $\gcd(a^{r/2} - 1,\, N)$ and $\gcd(a^{r/2} + 1,\, N)$.

Every step except 3 is cheap classically: greatest common divisors take Euclid's
algorithm, modular exponentiation takes repeated squaring. Step 3 is the hard one,
and it is the only quantum step.

<Margin label="The excluded cases are easy anyway">
Even $N$ is divisible by 2. A prime power $N = p^k$ is found by testing
$\sqrt[k]{N}$ for $k$ up to $\log_2 N$. A prime $N$ is detected in polynomial time
by a primality test, which does not factor it but tells you there is nothing to
find. So the reduction only needs to handle odd composites with at least two
distinct prime factors, which is exactly the RSA case.
</Margin>

## Why step 6 works

Suppose $r$ is even and $a^{r/2} \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod N$. Set $x = a^{r/2}$.
Then $x^2 = a^r \equiv 1$, so

$$
N \mid x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1) .
$$

$N$ divides the product. Does it divide either factor? If $N \mid x - 1$ then
$x \equiv 1$, contradicting the order being $r$ rather than $r/2$. If
$N \mid x + 1$ then $x \equiv -1$, excluded by step 5. So $N$ divides neither
factor but divides their product, which means its prime factors are *split*
between them. Therefore

$$
1 < \gcd(x - 1, N) < N ,
$$

a non-trivial factor, and likewise for $x+1$. Notice what did the work: finding a
square root of 1 modulo $N$ other than $\pm 1$. Such square roots exist only when
$N$ is composite with at least two distinct prime factors, which is precisely the
case the reduction handles.

## $N = 15$, worked

Take $a = 7$. The powers of 7 modulo 15 are

$$
7^1 = 7, \quad 7^2 = 49 \equiv 4, \quad 7^3 \equiv 28 \equiv 13, \quad 7^4 \equiv 91 \equiv 1 ,
$$

so $r = 4$. It is even, and $a^{r/2} = 7^2 \equiv 4$, which is neither $1$ nor
$14 \equiv -1$. So

$$
\gcd(4 - 1, 15) = \gcd(3, 15) = 3, \qquad \gcd(4 + 1, 15) = \gcd(5, 15) = 5 .
$$

$15 = 3 \times 5$. The orbit $1 \to 7 \to 4 \to 13 \to 1$ is what the quantum
circuit will encode: multiplication by 7 modulo 15 is a permutation of the
residues, and $r$ is the length of the cycle containing 1. On four qubits that
permutation is a cyclic bit shift followed by a bitwise complement, which is the
subject of the next lesson; applied four times it returns the register to its
starting value.

## How often does a random $a$ work

Steps 4 and 5 send you back to the start, so the reduction is only useful if they
fail rarely. They do. For an odd $N$ with $m \ge 2$ distinct prime factors and
$a$ chosen uniformly from the units modulo $N$,

$$
\Pr\big[\, r \text{ even and } a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \,\big] \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{1}{2^{\,m-1}} \;\ge\; \frac12 .
$$

So at least half of all candidates work, and the expected number of restarts is at
most 2. The proof runs through the Chinese remainder theorem: modulo each prime
power factor the order of $a$ has some power of 2 in it, and the bad cases require
all $m$ of those powers to agree, which happens for at most half the units.

Check it on $N = 15$, whose units are $\{1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14\}$:

| $a$ | $2$ | $4$ | $7$ | $8$ | $11$ | $13$ | $14$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $r$ | $4$ | $2$ | $4$ | $4$ | $2$ | $4$ | $2$ |
| $a^{r/2} \bmod 15$ | $4$ | $4$ | $4$ | $4$ | $11$ | $4$ | $14$ |
| usable | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | no |

Only $a = 14 \equiv -1$ fails, along with the trivial $a = 1$. Six of the eight
units work, which is $75\%$, comfortably above the guaranteed $50\%$.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="Verify the order before you trust it">
The quantum step returns a candidate $r$, and it can return a *divisor* of the
true order rather than the order itself, because the post-processing of T2.M5.L3
recovers a fraction in lowest terms. Always check $a^{r} \equiv 1 \pmod N$
classically before using $r$, and if it fails, try small multiples of the
candidate. This check costs one modular exponentiation and catches the most common
failure of an otherwise correct implementation.
</Callout>

<Check question="You run the algorithm on $N = 21$ with $a = 8$ and find $r = 2$. What factors do you get, and why?">
$8^2 = 64 \equiv 1 \pmod{21}$, so $r = 2$, which is even, and
$a^{r/2} = 8 \not\equiv \pm 1$. Then $\gcd(8-1, 21) = \gcd(7, 21) = 7$ and
$\gcd(8+1, 21) = \gcd(9, 21) = 3$, giving $21 = 3 \times 7$. Notice how little
quantum work this needed: $r = 2$ means one bit of order. The hard instances are
the ones where $r$ is close to $N$, which is the generic case for RSA moduli and
the reason the counting register needs $2n$ qubits rather than a handful.
</Check>
