The quantum circuit does not return $r$. It returns an integer $s$ from which you
must extract $r$, and the extraction is pure number theory: a two-hundred-year-old
algorithm for finding the simplest fraction near a given real number. This is the
step that turns an approximate phase into an exact integer, and it is the reason
order finding tolerates a counting register whose readings are never quite right.

## What the measurement means

The order-finding circuit puts the counting register into a state peaked near the
multiples of $2^t/r$. A measurement returns $s$ with

$$
\frac{s}{2^{t}} \approx \frac{k}{r}
$$

for some integer $k$ in $\{0, 1, \dots, r-1\}$, uniformly random. Both $k$ and $r$
are unknown, and the approximation is good to about half a grid spacing:

$$
\left| \frac{s}{2^{t}} - \frac{k}{r} \right| \le \frac{1}{2^{t+1}} .
$$

Choose $t$ so that $2^{t} \ge N^{2}$, which since $r < N$ gives
$2^{t+1} > 2r^{2}$ and therefore

$$
\left| \frac{s}{2^{t}} - \frac{k}{r} \right| < \frac{1}{2r^{2}} .
$$

That inequality is exactly the hypothesis of a classical theorem.

<Margin label="Legendre's theorem">
If $|x - k/r| < 1/(2r^2)$ with $\gcd(k, r) = 1$, then $k/r$ appears among the
convergents of the continued-fraction expansion of $x$. There are at most
$O(\log r)$ convergents, so you can simply try them all. The theorem is what makes
$2n+1$ counting qubits the right number: fewer, and the bound fails; more, and you
are paying for precision the post-processing cannot use.
</Margin>

## The expansion

The continued fraction of $x$ is produced by repeatedly taking the integer part
and inverting the remainder:

$$
x = a_0 + \cfrac{1}{a_1 + \cfrac{1}{a_2 + \cdots}} ,
\qquad
[a_0; a_1, a_2, \dots] .
$$

Truncating after $a_i$ and collapsing the fraction gives the $i$-th **convergent**
$p_i/q_i$, always in lowest terms, and the $q_i$ increase strictly. The recipe for
order finding is: expand $s/2^t$, list the convergents, and take the one with the
largest denominator $q_i < N$. That $q_i$ is your candidate $r$.

## The four-bit case

Run order finding for $a = 7$, $N = 15$, with $t = 4$. The order is 4, which
divides $16$ exactly, so the peaks are exact and the counting register takes only
the values $0$, $4$, $8$ and $12$, each with probability $0.25$. The distribution
is the Fourier transform of a period-4 comb, which you can see directly.

Now post-process each outcome.

| $s$ | $s/16$ | continued fraction | convergents | candidate $r$ | $7^r \bmod 15$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $[0]$ | $0/1$ | $1$ | $7$, fails |
| $4$ | $1/4$ | $[0; 4]$ | $0/1,\; 1/4$ | $4$ | $1$, correct |
| $8$ | $1/2$ | $[0; 2]$ | $0/1,\; 1/2$ | $2$ | $4$, fails |
| $12$ | $3/4$ | $[0; 1, 3]$ | $0/1,\; 1/1,\; 3/4$ | $4$ | $1$, correct |

Two of the four outcomes give $r = 4$ immediately. $s = 8$ gives $r = 2$, which is
a *divisor* of the true order: the fraction $2/4$ reduced to $1/2$ and lost the
factor. $s = 0$ gives nothing at all. So a single run succeeds with probability
$0.5$ here, and two runs push that to $0.75$.

## A case where the fractions do work

Order finding is more convincing when the answer is not a power of two. Take
$N = 21$, $a = 2$, whose order is $r = 6$, with $t = 9$ so that $2^t = 512 \ge
21^2$. The six peaks sit near $512k/6$.

| $s$ | $s/512$ | continued fraction | convergents | largest $q_i < 21$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $85$ | $0.166016$ | $[0; 6, 42, 2]$ | $0/1,\; 1/6,\; 42/253,\; 85/512$ | $6$ |
| $171$ | $0.333984$ | $[0; 2, 1, 170]$ | $0/1,\; 1/2,\; 1/3,\; 171/512$ | $3$ |
| $256$ | $0.500000$ | $[0; 2]$ | $0/1,\; 1/2$ | $2$ |
| $341$ | $0.666016$ | $[0; 1, 1, 1, 170]$ | $0/1,\; 1/1,\; 1/2,\; 2/3,\; 341/512$ | $3$ |
| $427$ | $0.833984$ | $[0; 1, 5, 42, 2]$ | $0/1,\; 1/1,\; 5/6,\; 211/253,\; 427/512$ | $6$ |

Look at $s = 85$: the true ratio is $1/6 = 0.166667$ and the measurement is
$0.166016$, off by $0.00065$. The continued fraction snaps it back to exactly
$1/6$, because $1/6$ is the only fraction with a small denominator that close. The
later convergents $42/253$ and $85/512$ are better approximations to the
*measurement* and worse answers, which is why the rule is "largest denominator
below $N$", not "best approximation".

Outcomes $171$ and $341$ return $3$, a proper divisor of $6$, because $k$ and $r$
shared a factor. The standard repair is to test small multiples: $2^3 = 8
\not\equiv 1 \pmod{21}$, but $2^6 = 64 \equiv 1$, so doubling the candidate works.

<Callout tone="note" title="Always verify, never assume">
Post-processing produces a *candidate*. Check $a^{r} \equiv 1 \pmod N$ with one
classical modular exponentiation before doing anything else, and if it fails, try
$2r$, $3r$ and so on up to a small bound, then take another sample. This costs
microseconds and turns an algorithm that is right half the time into one that is
right or silent, which is the only kind you can build a factoring service on.
</Callout>

<Check question="With $t = 9$ and $N = 21$, you measure $s = 0$. What do you learn, and how often should you expect it?">
Nothing: $0/512 = 0$, whose only convergent is $0/1$, giving the useless candidate
$r = 1$. You should expect it with probability about $1/r = 1/6$, since the $r$
peaks are equally likely and one of them sits at $k = 0$. The all-zeros outcome is
the phase-estimation signature of the eigenvalue 1, promised at the end of
T2.M4.L1, and it is unavoidable: every order-finding run has a $1/r$ chance of
learning nothing. Discard it and sample again.
</Check>
