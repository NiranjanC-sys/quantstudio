Phase estimation needs controlled powers of a unitary. For order finding that
unitary is $U_a\ket{y} = \ket{ay \bmod N}$, and it is not handed to you by
nature: somebody has to compile modular arithmetic into reversible gates. This is
where all of Shor's actual cost lives, and it is the reason a 2048-bit factoring
run is measured in days rather than seconds.

## The stack of reductions

Four layers, each built from the one below.

**Modular exponentiation** $\ket{x}\ket{1} \mapsto \ket{x}\ket{a^x \bmod N}$.
Since $a^x = \prod_j (a^{2^j})^{x_j}$, this is $t$ controlled modular
multiplications, one per bit of $x$, where the multiplier $a^{2^j} \bmod N$ is
computed *classically* in advance by repeated squaring. The quantum circuit never
computes an exponent.

**Controlled modular multiplication** $\ket{y} \mapsto \ket{cy \bmod N}$ for a
classical constant $c$. Write $cy = \sum_j c\,2^j y_j$ and accumulate: $n$
controlled modular additions of the classical constants $c\,2^j \bmod N$. The
result lands in a fresh register, and a final swap plus a multiplication by
$c^{-1} \bmod N$ clears the input, which is Bennett's uncompute again.

**Modular addition** $\ket{y} \mapsto \ket{y + b \bmod N}$. Add $b$, subtract $N$,
look at the sign bit: if it went negative, add $N$ back. The comparison writes one
ancilla bit, and that bit has to be uncomputed by a second comparison against the
result, or it stays entangled and destroys the interference exactly as in
T2.M1.L1.

**Addition** $\ket{y} \mapsto \ket{y+b}$. A ripple-carry adder in Toffolis, or
Draper's adder which does the whole thing with phase rotations in the Fourier
basis and needs no carry ancillas at all.

<Margin label="Why the QFT shows up twice">
Draper's adder transforms the register with a QFT, applies single-qubit phase
rotations proportional to $b$, and transforms back. Inside Shor that means the
Fourier transform appears in two entirely different roles: once per addition as an
arithmetic device, and once at the very end as the periodicity detector. They are
the same circuit doing unrelated jobs, and confusing them is a common source of
misreading a Shor diagram.
</Margin>

## The instance that fits on four qubits

$N = 15$ is small enough that the arithmetic collapses into permutations you can
read off. Since $16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$, multiplying by 2 is a *cyclic* left shift
of the four bits: no carry ever escapes, it wraps. So

$$
\times 2 = \text{rotate left by 1},
\qquad
\times 4 = \text{rotate left by 2},
\qquad
\times 8 = \text{rotate left by 3} .
$$

And $7 \equiv -8 \pmod{15}$, while negating modulo 15 is the bitwise complement,
because $15 - y$ flips every bit of a four-bit $y$. Therefore

$$
\times 7 = \text{complement} \circ \text{rotate left by 3} ,
$$

three SWAP gates and four $X$ gates, with no ancillas and no adders. Multiplication
by 4 is two SWAPs.

Amplitude $0.707107$ on $\ket{00001}$ and on $\ket{10111}$: the control bit and the
work value are now correlated, which is the entanglement phase estimation feeds on.

<Callout tone="gotcha" title="This circuit is wrong on two of its sixteen inputs">
The complement trick maps $y \mapsto 15 - 8y$, which is $7y$ modulo 15 for
$y \ne 0$. On $y = 0$ it gives $1111 = 15$, and on $y = 15$ it gives $0000$. Both
should be fixed points of multiplication modulo 15, and they are not. It does not
matter here because the work register is loaded with 1 and the orbit
$1 \to 7 \to 4 \to 13 \to 1$ never touches $0$ or $15$. It would matter
immediately if you reused the gadget somewhere the register can reach those
values, and this shortcut is the reason small-$N$ Shor demonstrations do not scale
to a general circuit.
</Callout>

## The cost at scale

For an $n$-bit modulus, count upward through the stack:

| layer | count | cost each | subtotal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| addition | | | $O(n)$ gates |
| modular addition | $2$ adds plus a comparison | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| modular multiplication | $n$ modular additions | $O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ |
| modular exponentiation | $2n$ multiplications | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^3)$ |

So controlled modular exponentiation is $\Theta(n^3)$ elementary gates, and it
dwarfs the $O(n^2)$ of the QFT and the $O(n\log n)$ of its approximate version.
Beauregard's construction gets the *width* down to $2n+3$ qubits by reusing
registers aggressively, at the cost of $O(n^3 \log n)$ gates and a serial depth
that is hard to parallelise.

Put numbers on it. For $n = 2048$, $n^3 \approx 8.6 \times 10^{9}$ elementary
gates, every one of which must be error corrected. That figure, not the qubit
count, is why credible estimates for breaking RSA-2048 run to hours or days on a
machine with millions of physical qubits.

<Check question="Why does the algorithm precompute $a^{2^j} \bmod N$ classically instead of squaring inside the circuit?">
Because squaring inside the circuit would mean multiplying a quantum register by
another quantum register, which needs a full multiplier rather than a
multiply-by-constant, roughly squaring the gate count and requiring twice the
width. The exponents $2^j$ are known before the circuit runs and do not depend on
the superposition, so every multiplier is a *classical constant* and each
controlled multiplication is a fixed permutation. Recognising which parts of an
algorithm are data-independent is most of what makes quantum arithmetic
affordable.
</Check>
