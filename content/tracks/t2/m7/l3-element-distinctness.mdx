Are any two entries of a list equal? Classically this needs to look at everything,
$\Theta(N)$ queries. Grover applied naively makes it *worse*. The right answer,
$\Theta(N^{2/3})$, came from Ambainis in 2003 and is the flagship result of the
quantum-walk framework: not a new interference trick, but a systematic way of
turning a random walk over partial solutions into a quantum algorithm.

## The problem and the obvious attacks

Given oracle access to $f: [N] \to S$, decide whether there exist $i \ne j$ with
$f(i) = f(j)$.

**Classically** it takes $\Theta(N)$ queries: sort, or hash, and any algorithm
that misses one index can be defeated by putting the collision there.

**Grover over pairs** looks promising and is not. There are $\binom N2 \approx
N^2/2$ pairs, and Grover over $N^2$ items costs $O(\sqrt{N^2}) = O(N)$ queries.
Exactly the classical bound, with a large constant and a quantum computer.

The reason Grover fails is that it treats each pair as an independent test,
throwing away every query as soon as it is made. Checking whether the pair
$(i, j)$ collides costs two queries even though you may have looked at $f(i)$ a
moment ago while testing $(i, k)$. The walk fixes exactly this.

<Margin label="Walking over partial solutions">
The state of the walk is not a candidate answer, it is a *stored subset* of the
data. Moving one step changes the subset a little and costs only the queries needed
for the change. That is the general Szegedy and Ambainis pattern: pay a large
setup cost once, then amortise it over many cheap updates, and use amplitude
amplification to reach a marked state.
</Margin>

## The Johnson graph

Fix $r$. The vertices of the Johnson graph $J(N, r)$ are the $\binom Nr$ subsets of
$[N]$ of size exactly $r$, and two subsets are adjacent when they differ in a
single element, one swapped out for one swapped in.

The walk's state at vertex $R$ carries the stored data $\{(i, f(i)) : i \in R\}$.
A vertex is **marked** when $R$ contains both indices of a collision. The algorithm
is amplitude amplification with the walk in place of the diffuser: walk, check,
amplify.

## The cost formula

Szegedy's framework gives the query cost of a walk-based search as

$$
S + \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\,U + C \right)
$$

with

- $S$ the **setup** cost, to build the initial uniform superposition over vertices
  with their data;
- $U$ the **update** cost, one step of the walk;
- $C$ the **checking** cost, testing whether a vertex is marked;
- $\varepsilon$ the fraction of marked vertices;
- $\delta$ the spectral gap of the walk.

Fill in the four quantities for element distinctness.

$S = r$: loading $r$ values takes $r$ queries.

$U = O(1)$: a step removes one index and inserts another, so two queries.

$C = 0$: the data is already stored, so checking for an internal collision is
computation, not queries. Concretely it is an equality test on stored pairs, which
in circuit form is a comparator into an ancilla:

$\varepsilon = \Theta\big((r/N)^2\big)$: a random $r$-subset contains a given pair
of indices with probability about $(r/N)^2$.

$\delta = \Omega(1/r)$: the spectral gap of $J(N, r)$ for $r \ll N$.

Substituting:

$$
r + \frac{N}{r}\left( \sqrt{r} \cdot 1 + 0 \right) = r + \frac{N}{\sqrt r} .
$$

Minimise over $r$. The two terms balance at $r^{3/2} = N$, giving
$r = N^{2/3}$ and total cost

$$
O\!\left(N^{2/3}\right) .
$$

| $N$ | classical $N$ | Grover over pairs $N$ | walk $N^{2/3}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{3}$ | $1000$ | $1000$ | $100$ |
| $10^{6}$ | $10^{6}$ | $10^{6}$ | $10^{4}$ |
| $10^{9}$ | $10^{9}$ | $10^{9}$ | $10^{6}$ |

## Why the exponent is exactly $2/3$

The upper bound is Ambainis. The matching lower bound is Aaronson and Shi, who
proved $\Omega(N^{2/3})$ for element distinctness using the polynomial method of
T2.M9.L1. So the exponent $2/3$ is not an artefact of the Johnson-graph choice; it
is the truth, and no cleverer walk or different graph can beat it.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The memory is the real cost">
The walk stores $r = N^{2/3}$ input values *in superposition*, which means
$N^{2/3}$ qubits of quantum-accessible memory, coherent for the whole run. For
$N = 10^{9}$ that is a million qubits holding data, on top of the logic. Under any
plausible error-correction overhead the walk is far more expensive than the
classical linear scan it beats asymptotically. Element distinctness is the
standard example of a real quantum speedup whose resource profile makes it
unusable, and it is a useful check on any claim that a quadratic-or-better speedup
implies practical value.
</Callout>

<Check question="Suppose the checking cost were $C = r$ instead of 0, because the data had to be re-read. What would the optimal $r$ and total cost become?">
The formula becomes

$$
r + \frac{N}{r}\left(\sqrt r + r\right) = r + \frac{N}{\sqrt r} + N .
$$

The additive $N$ term now dominates everything, so the total is $\Theta(N)$
and the walk gives no speedup at all. This is why storing the data at the vertices
is the essential idea rather than an optimisation: the entire $N^{2/3}$ comes from
$C = 0$, and a version of the algorithm that cannot hold the subset coherently is
just a classical scan.
</Check>
