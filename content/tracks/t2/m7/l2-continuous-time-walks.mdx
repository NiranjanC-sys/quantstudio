The coined walk needed an extra register because a discrete step that merges
paths is not unitary. Take the step size to zero and the problem disappears: a
continuous-time evolution generated by a Hermitian matrix is automatically
unitary, no coin required. The price is that you now have to *simulate* a
Hamiltonian, which is Module 8, and the reward is the only proven exponential
black-box speedup on a natural graph problem.

## The definition

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a graph on $N$ vertices, so $A_{uv} = 1$ when
$u$ and $v$ are joined and 0 otherwise. $A$ is real symmetric, hence Hermitian, so

$$
U(t) = e^{-iAt}
$$

is unitary for every real $t$. That is the **continuous-time quantum walk**. The
Hilbert space is spanned by the vertices alone: $\dim = N$, with no coin factor.

Compare the classical continuous-time walk, whose distribution obeys
$\frac{dp}{dt} = -L p$ with $L = D - A$ the Laplacian. Same generator up to the
degree term, entirely different equation: one is first-order in $p$ with real
exponential decay, the other is first-order in the *amplitude* with imaginary
exponentials, so classical walks equilibrate and quantum walks oscillate forever.

<Margin label="Why no coin is needed">
The coined walk had to remember where the walker came from so the step could be
inverted. In continuous time the generator $A$ is applied for an infinitesimal
interval, and $e^{-iA\,dt} \approx I - iA\,dt$ is invertible by construction: there
is no step to undo, only a flow to run backwards. Reversibility comes free from
Hermiticity.
</Margin>

## The four-cycle, exactly

The two-dimensional hypercube is a four-cycle: vertices $00$, $01$, $11$, $10$
with edges joining strings that differ in one bit. Its adjacency matrix is

$$
A = X \otimes I + I \otimes X ,
$$

and the two terms commute, so the walk factorises:

$$
e^{-iAt} = e^{-iXt} \otimes e^{-iXt} = R_x(2t) \otimes R_x(2t) .
$$

Two single-qubit rotations, and the walk is exact for every $t$ with no Trotter
error. Start at vertex $00$ and evolve to $t = \pi/4$.

Probability 1 on $\ket{11}$. This is **perfect state transfer**: on the
$n$-dimensional hypercube, $e^{-iAt}$ at $t = \pi/2$ maps any vertex exactly onto
its antipode, because each factor $R_x(\pi)$ flips its qubit. A classical walk on
the hypercube converges to the uniform distribution, $2^{-n}$ per vertex, and never
concentrates anywhere. The quantum walk arrives with certainty, in time $\pi/2$
independent of $n$.

## Hitting time and the spectral gap

For search and traversal what matters is how fast the walk moves probability from
a start state to a target. Expand the start state in eigenvectors of $A$ with
eigenvalues $\lambda_j$:

$$
e^{-iAt}\ket{\psi_0} = \sum_j e^{-i\lambda_j t} c_j \ket{\lambda_j} .
$$

The state changes appreciably on the timescale set by the *differences*
$\lambda_j - \lambda_k$, so the relevant quantity is the spectrum's gap structure.
Two regimes:

- **A large gap** between the relevant eigenvalues means fast, clean oscillation
  between start and target, as in the hypercube where the two states involved are
  separated by $\Delta\lambda = 2$ per dimension.
- **A small gap** $\delta$ means the walk needs time $\Theta(1/\delta)$ to
  distinguish the eigenvectors, which sets the hitting time.

Classically the analogous statement uses the *Markov chain* gap and gives mixing
time $\Theta(1/\delta)$ as well, but the classical hitting time is
$\Theta(1/\delta)$ while the quantum one is often $\Theta(1/\sqrt\delta)$. That
square root is the general quantum-walk speedup, and it is the engine behind the
element-distinctness algorithm of the next lesson.

<Callout tone="note" title="Continuous time is not free">
$e^{-iAt}$ is a mathematical object, not a gate. Turning it into a circuit means
Hamiltonian simulation: Trotter for sparse $A$, or the block-encoding methods of
T2.M8.L3. The four-cycle above was exact only because its adjacency matrix is a sum
of commuting one-local terms. For a general sparse graph the simulation cost enters
every complexity statement, which is why quantum-walk papers quote query complexity
in calls to a neighbour oracle rather than gate counts.
</Callout>

<Check question="On the $n$-dimensional hypercube, the walk transfers a vertex to its antipode at $t = \pi/2$ regardless of $n$. Does that give an exponentially fast search?">
No, and seeing why matters. Perfect transfer requires knowing the target: you must
start at the antipode of where you want to arrive, so the walk delivers you
somewhere you could already compute. Search means finding a vertex marked by an
oracle, which breaks the hypercube's symmetry and destroys the exact factorisation;
the marked-vertex walk on the hypercube takes $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ time, the same as
Grover. Fast transport on a symmetric graph is a statement about the graph, not
about search. The welded tree of T2.M7.L4 is the case where symmetry and hardness
coexist.
</Check>
