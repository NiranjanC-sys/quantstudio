Phase estimation is the engine underneath Shor, underneath quantum chemistry,
and underneath most of what people mean by a quantum advantage. Its textbook
form contains one step that looks like an implementation detail and is actually
the whole cost model: counting qubit $k$ applies $U$ raised to a power of two,
and whether you build that as repeated queries or as a single scaled rotation
decides whether the circuit is affordable.

## Task

Let $U = P(2\pi\varphi)$ be the single-qubit phase gate with
$\varphi = 3/8$, so $U\ket{1} = e^{2\pi i \varphi}\ket{1}$ and $\ket{1}$ is the
eigenvector.

Return a four-qubit circuit, three counting qubits at indices 0, 1 and 2 and the
eigenvector qubit at index 3, that starts from $\ket{0000}$, prepares the
eigenvector, runs phase estimation, and ends with the counting register holding
$\ket{011}$ with probability 1. That is the binary expansion of
$3/8 = 0.011_2$, read most significant bit first on qubit 0.

The budget is seven two-qubit gates in total.

## Input and output contract

Implement one function, taking no arguments:

```python
def solve() -> dict:
    """Return a RedQuant circuit IR, version 1."""
```

Ops are applied in list order. A controlled phase is the `cp` gate, with the
control first in `targets` and the angle in `params`:

```python
{"id": "cu0", "gate": "cp", "targets": [0, 3], "params": [9.42477796076938]}
```

That angle is $2\pi \cdot \tfrac38 \cdot 4 = 3\pi$, the power of two belonging to
counting qubit 0. `cp` is symmetric in its two wires, so the order only matters
for readability.
The inverse QFT on the counting register is part of your circuit, not something
the judge appends.

Qubit 0 is the most significant bit of the basis index and also the most
significant bit of the counting register, so the register reads $\ket{011}$ as
qubit 0 holding 0, qubit 1 holding 1 and qubit 2 holding 1.

## Worked example

The reference solution prepares $\ket{1}$ on qubit 3 with an `x`, Hadamards the
counting register, then applies one `cp` per counting qubit with angle
$2\pi\varphi\,2^{\,n-1-k}$, that is $2\pi \cdot \tfrac38 \cdot 4$, then
$\cdot\, 2$, then $\cdot\, 1$, and finishes with the three-qubit inverse QFT.
Running it gives:

```text
outcome 0111 probability 1.0
counting register reads 011 = phi * 8
```

The full four-qubit outcome is $\ket{0111}$: counting register $011$, eigenvector
qubit still in $\ket{1}$ where it started, as it must be, since it is an
eigenvector and the algorithm only ever reads its phase. Because $3/8$ is exact
in three binary digits, the probability is 1 rather than the $\ge 4/\pi^2$ the
general bound promises. The circuit has 14 ops, depth 10 and seven two-qubit
gates.

## Constraints

- `qubits` is exactly 4 and `clbits` is exactly 0.
- At most **seven** two-qubit gates in total, counting the controlled unitaries and everything inside the inverse QFT.
- The eigenvector qubit must come back in $\ket{1}$, unentangled from the counting register.
- Allowed gates: `h`, `x`, `p`, `cp`, `swap`, `rz`.
- No `measure`, no `reset`, no classical conditions.

## Scoring

200 points for the first accepted submission. The judge runs
`statevector_equals` against $\ket{0111}$ with `up_to_global_phase` set to true
and a tolerance of $10^{-9}$, then `resource_bound` with
`max_two_qubit_gates` of 7, then `gate_set_subset` over the list above. A
circuit that repeats the unit controlled unitary $2^{\,n-1-k}$ times is correct
and spends eleven two-qubit gates, so it fails the resource bound rather than the
state check. No partial credit, one penalty minute per rejected submission.
