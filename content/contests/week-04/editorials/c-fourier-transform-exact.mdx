## The intended solution

Seven ops in the standard pattern, most significant qubit first:

```text
h  0
cp(pi/2)  0, 1
cp(pi/4)  0, 2
h  1
cp(pi/2)  1, 2
h  2
swap 0, 2
```

Depth 6, four two-qubit gates, and the resulting operator is the discrete
Fourier matrix exactly, with no leftover global phase.

The recursive structure is worth stating once. Qubit $q$ gets a Hadamard, which
writes the bit $x_q$ into a relative phase of $e^{i\pi x_q}$, and then one
controlled phase from each less significant qubit, contributing
$e^{2\pi i x_r / 2^{\,r-q+1}}$. Summing the contributions across all qubits gives
the phase $2\pi i jk/8$ that the matrix demands, but assembled in the wrong wire
order: the transform naturally writes the most significant output bit onto the
least significant wire.

## Why the swap is not a convention

That last sentence is the problem. Without the final `swap`, the circuit computes
a perfectly good unitary $\tilde F$ satisfying $\tilde F = R F$, where $R$ is the
bit-reversal permutation on basis indices. $R$ is not a global phase and it is
not a relabelling of the basis you are free to adopt later, because the QFT is
almost never the last thing in a circuit. Feed $\tilde F$ into a phase estimation
routine and the counting register reads its answer backwards; feed it into an
arithmetic circuit and the carries go the wrong way.

There are exactly two defensible positions: emit the swap, or track the reversal
explicitly everywhere downstream. This problem takes the first, and
`up_to_global_phase` is set to false so that neither the phase nor the
permutation can be waved away.

On three qubits the bit reversal is a single `swap` of qubits 0 and 2, since
qubit 1 is its own mirror image. On $n$ qubits it is $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ swaps.

## Why the naive answer is too expensive

The naive route to a $2^n \times 2^n$ unitary is generic synthesis: run a
Householder or cosine-sine decomposition on the matrix and emit whatever comes
out. For $n = 3$ that is dozens of two-qubit gates against a ceiling of four,
and the count grows as $O(4^n)$ rather than the QFT's $O(n^2)$. The whole reason
the Fourier transform is useful in a quantum algorithm is that it is one of the
few large unitaries with a small circuit, and finding that circuit by search is
not how anyone gets it.

The other overspend is decomposing the `swap` into three CNOTs. That is the
right move when the hardware has no swap, and here it costs three two-qubit
gates where the budget has room for one.

## Common wrong answers

Using $\pi/4$ where $\pi/2$ belongs, or applying the controlled phases in the
opposite direction along the register, produces a unitary whose entries all have
the right modulus $0.353553$ and the wrong phases. The failure message names an
amplitude with the right magnitude and a wrong argument, which is the signature
of an angle error rather than a structural one.

Reaching for `cz` instead of `cp(pi)` is a different error with the same look:
`cz` is `cp` at $\lambda = \pi$, and the QFT on three qubits never needs
$\lambda = \pi$.
