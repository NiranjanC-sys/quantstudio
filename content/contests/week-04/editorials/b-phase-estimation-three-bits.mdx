## The intended solution

Four steps, fourteen ops, seven two-qubit gates.

1. `x` on qubit 3, preparing the eigenvector $\ket 1$ of $U = P(2\pi\varphi)$.
2. `h` on each of the three counting qubits.
3. One controlled phase per counting qubit, with counting qubit $k$ carrying
   angle $2\pi\varphi\,2^{\,n-1-k}$. For $\varphi = 3/8$ and $n = 3$ that is
   $3\pi$, then $3\pi/2$, then $3\pi/4$.
4. The three-qubit inverse QFT on the counting register: a swap of qubits 0 and
   2, then `h` and the conjugate controlled phases in reverse order.

The outcome is $\ket{0111}$ with probability 1: counting register $011 = 3$, and
$3/8$ is the phase.

Step 3 is the whole problem. The textbook writes $U^{2^{\,n-1-k}}$ and draws
$2^{\,n-1-k}$ copies of the controlled-$U$ box, because in general you cannot
raise a black-box unitary to a power more cheaply than by applying it repeatedly.
When $U$ is a phase gate you can: $P(\lambda)^m = P(m\lambda)$, so the whole tower
collapses into one gate with a scaled angle. Three controlled phases instead of
$1 + 2 + 4 = 7$.

## Why the naive answer blows the budget

Count the naive circuit. Seven controlled-$U$ gates for step 3, plus three
controlled phases and one swap inside the inverse QFT, is eleven two-qubit gates
against a ceiling of seven. The circuit is correct and produces exactly the same
state; it fails `resource_bound`, which is the honest failure mode, because on
hardware the difference is real. In Shor's algorithm the same distinction is the
difference between $O(n^3)$ and $O(n^4)$ modular multiplications.

The other place people overspend is the inverse QFT. Writing it as the forward
QFT followed by an inversion, or building it from a generic circuit inverter that
does not simplify, easily doubles the four two-qubit gates it needs.

## Why the answer is exact

The general phase estimation bound promises the nearest $n$-bit approximation
with probability at least $4/\pi^2 \approx 0.405$, and better with more counting
qubits. That bound is about phases which are *not* exactly representable. Here
$\varphi = 3/8 = 0.011_2$ terminates in three binary digits, the state before the
inverse QFT is exactly the Fourier basis state for $j = 3$, and the inverse QFT
maps it to $\ket{011}$ with amplitude 1. Probability 1, not 0.405 and not 0.95.

That is also why the eigenvector qubit must come back unentangled. If your
circuit leaves qubit 3 correlated with the counting register, you have applied
something other than a controlled power of $U$, and the state check will catch it
even though the counting marginal may still look right.

## Common wrong answers

Reversing the angle assignment, giving counting qubit 0 the smallest power
instead of the largest, and omitting the swap at the head of the inverse QFT are
the same mistake wearing two hats, and they produce the identical wrong state.
The probability stops being deterministic and spreads over five outcomes:

| outcome | probability |
| --- | --- |
| $\ket{0111}$ | $0.728553$ |
| $\ket{1111}$ | $0.125$ |
| $\ket{1001}$ | $0.073223$ |
| $\ket{0101}$ | $0.036612$ |
| $\ket{1101}$ | $0.036612$ |

The peak is still on the right answer, which is precisely why a check that only
looks at the most likely outcome would accept this and an amplitude check does
not. Because qubit 0 is the most significant bit here, the power assignment has
to be checked rather than assumed.
