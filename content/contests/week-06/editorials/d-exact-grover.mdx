## The intended solution

Keep the shape of Grover and change the two reflections into two rotations of a
smaller, matched angle.

Ordinary Grover's oracle multiplies the marked amplitude by $-1$ and its
diffusion multiplies the $\ket{000}$ amplitude by $-1$: both are phase rotations
of angle $\pi$. Long's variant replaces both with $e^{i\varphi}$, the same
$\varphi$ in each, chosen so that $J$ iterations rotate through exactly the right
total. For $J$ iterations with $\sin\beta = \sqrt{M/N}$,

$$
\varphi = 2\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\sin\big(\pi/(4J+2)\big)}{\sin\beta}\right),
$$

which for $J = 2$, $M = 1$, $N = 8$ evaluates to $2.1268800471555034$ radians,
or $121.86$ degrees. Each iteration then turns the state slightly less far than
an ordinary iteration would, and after two of them the state is exactly
$\ket{101}$.

The circuit is a Hadamard layer, then twice over: phase $\varphi$ on the marked
state, Hadamard layer, phase $\varphi$ on $\ket{000}$, Hadamard layer. Each phase
is a doubly controlled `p` gate conjugated by $X$ gates on the wires that read 0
in the string being phased. Thirty-five ops, depth 17, four gates on three wires,
and the printed probability on $\ket{101}$ is $1.0$ at twelve decimal places.

## Why the naive answer stalls

Run ordinary Grover and evaluate the success probability rather than quoting
$\tfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N$:

| iterations | probability on $\ket{101}$ |
| --- | --- |
| $1$ | $0.78125$ |
| $2$ | $0.9453125$ |
| $3$ | $0.330078125$ |

Two is the best integer and it is $0.945$, not 1. The reason is arithmetic, not
implementation: the rotation angle is $2\arcsin(1/\sqrt8) = 0.7227$ radians, the
continuous optimum number of iterations is $\pi/(2\theta) - 1/2 = 1.6734$, and
$1.6734$ is not an integer. You cannot round your way to certainty.

The usual response is to accept $0.945$ and repeat on failure, which costs an
expected $1/0.945$ runs and, worse, requires a way to check the answer. When the
oracle is expensive or the check is not free, exactness is worth real money. It
is also the difference between a subroutine you can call inside a larger
amplitude amplification and one you cannot, because a $5.5$ percent failure
probability compounds.

## Why phase matching, specifically

The naive fix is to rotate one of the two reflections by $\varphi$ and leave the
other at $\pi$. That does not work, and the reason has a name: the phase-matching
condition. The oracle rotation and the diffusion rotation must be equal, and
equal in sign, or the two reflections stop composing into a rotation of the
two-dimensional search subspace and the state leaks out of it. Long's derivation
starts from that condition and solves for the angle that makes $J$ iterations
land exactly.

## Common wrong answers

Using $\varphi$ on the oracle and $\pi$ on the diffusion, in either arrangement,
breaks phase matching and drops the success probability to $0.48538$, far below
the $0.945312$ that plain Grover would have given. Using $+\varphi$ on one and
$-\varphi$ on the other is worse still, at $0.002039$: the two rotations very
nearly cancel.

Changing $J$ without changing $\varphi$ is the other trap, and it fails harder
than it looks. The angle formula needs
$\sin(\pi/(4J+2)) \le \sin\beta$ to have a real solution at all. At $J = 1$ that
ratio is $\sin(\pi/6)/\sqrt{1/8} = 1.41421$, so there is no real $\varphi$:
one iteration cannot reach certainty on eight items no matter what phase you
choose. At $J = 2$ the ratio is $\sin(\pi/10)/\sqrt{1/8} = 0.874$ and the
solution exists. The angle and the iteration count are a matched pair.
