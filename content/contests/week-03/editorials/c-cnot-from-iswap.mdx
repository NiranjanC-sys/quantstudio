## The intended solution

Five ops:

```text
iswap 0, 1
h     0
iswap 0, 1
sdg   0
sx    1
```

Materialised and divided by $e^{i\pi/4}$, that is CNOT exactly. Two entangling
gates, three single-qubit gates, depth 4.

The shape is forced and the details are not. Any two-iSWAP solution has to be
iSWAP, some single-qubit layer, iSWAP, some single-qubit layer, with an optional
layer at the front. What varies between solutions is which single-qubit gates go
in those layers, and the reference circuit's particular choice, an `h` in the
middle and `sdg` with `sx` at the end, came out of an exhaustive search over
Clifford-plus-$\sqrt X$ layers. A hand-derived answer using $R_z$ and $R_x$
rotations with the same iSWAP placement is equally acceptable and will score the
same.

## Why one iSWAP is impossible

This is the part worth understanding, because it explains why the exercise is
not "find the right single-qubit gates and you can do it in one".

Strip the local gates from any two-qubit unitary and what is left is a point
$(c_1, c_2, c_3)$ in the Weyl chamber, the canonical coordinates of
$\exp(-i(c_1 XX + c_2 YY + c_3 ZZ))$. Local gates before and after cannot move
that point. CNOT sits at $(\pi/4, 0, 0)$. iSWAP sits at $(\pi/4, \pi/4, 0)$.
They are different points, so no amount of single-qubit dressing turns one
application of iSWAP into a CNOT. Two applications give you access to a whole
region of the chamber, CNOT included, and that is why the answer is exactly two.

The same argument run the other way says a CZ costs two iSWAPs as well, and that
a SWAP, sitting at $(\pi/4, \pi/4, \pi/4)$, costs three.

## Why the naive answer is too expensive

The reflex is to reach for the identity $\mathrm{iSWAP} = \mathrm{SWAP}$ times a
phase on the exchanged subspace, then to write SWAP as three CNOTs and invert.
That gives a CNOT built from three iSWAPs plus a pile of single-qubit corrections,
and it fails `max_two_qubit_gates` of 2. It is the right instinct applied one
level too coarsely: the exchange that iSWAP performs is not something you have to
undo with a third entangler, because the second iSWAP already undoes it.

The other expensive route is to call a generic KAK routine and let it emit
whatever entangler count it likes. Generic routines target CNOT or CZ as their
basis gate; asking one for an iSWAP-based circuit without telling it the basis
usually produces a CNOT circuit with each CNOT then expanded into two iSWAPs, for
a total of four.

## Common wrong answers

Omitting the middle single-qubit gate gives $\mathrm{iSWAP}^2$, which is
$\mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, 1)$ on the exchanged subspace, a diagonal Clifford, not
a CNOT. It is a common near miss because the circuit looks symmetric and
plausible.

Getting the final layer wrong shows up as a matrix that is CNOT with two entries
carrying a factor of $\pm i$. Since `up_to_global_phase` divides out a single
common phase, entries that disagree with each other by a phase are still a
failure, and the message names the first mismatching entry.
