## The intended solution

Three CNOTs, alternating direction:

```text
CNOT 0 -> 1
CNOT 1 -> 0
CNOT 0 -> 1
```

On basis states, write the register as an ordered pair $(a, b)$. A CNOT adds its
control into its target modulo 2, so the three steps give

$$
(a, b) \;\to\; (a, a \oplus b) \;\to\; (b, a \oplus b) \;\to\; (b, a),
$$

because $a \oplus (a \oplus b) = b$ and then $(a \oplus b) \oplus b = a$. The
contents have changed places using no scratch space, which is the classical XOR
swap trick verbatim. Since the map is correct on all four basis states and CNOT
is linear, it is correct on every superposition, and the resulting operator is
the SWAP matrix exactly, not up to a phase.

The reverse order, 1 to 0 then 0 to 1 then 1 to 0, works equally well. Both are
depth 3.

## Why three, and why not two

Three is not merely what this construction costs, it is the minimum. In the
canonical picture, every two-qubit gate reduces to a point
$\exp(-i(a\,XX + b\,YY + c\,ZZ)/2)$ in the Weyl chamber, and the CNOT count is a
function of that point: one CNOT reaches the points with two coordinates zero,
two CNOTs reach the points with one coordinate zero, and three are needed for
everything else. SWAP sits at $a = b = c = \pi/2$, the far corner, with all three
coordinates maximal. It is as non-local as a two-qubit gate can be, so it costs
the maximum.

That is also the reason routing dominates compiled circuit sizes. A single
logical two-qubit gate between distant wires costs one CNOT for the gate plus six
for the SWAP there and back, and the SWAP count grows with the distance across
the coupling map.

## Why the naive answer is rejected

Writing `{"gate": "swap", "targets": [0, 1]}` produces exactly the right matrix
and fails `gate_set_subset`, whose `allowed` list is `["x"]`. That is not
pedantry: the problem exists because hardware has no SWAP, so a solution that
assumes one has answered a different question.

The other rejected shortcut is three CNOTs in the same direction. They compose to
the identity on the target and leave the control alone, and the resulting
operator is the identity, not SWAP. Direction is the whole content of the trick.

## Common wrong answers

Two CNOTs in opposite directions give $(a, b) \to (b, a \oplus b)$, which is a
legitimate reversible map, is entangling, and is not SWAP. It is easy to convince
yourself otherwise by checking only $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{10}$: those two do
exchange. Check $\ket{11}$ as well and it goes to $\ket{10}$, which SWAP fixes.
