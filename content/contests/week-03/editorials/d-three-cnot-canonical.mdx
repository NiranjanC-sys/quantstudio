## The intended solution

Use the Vatan and Williams circuit, which is the standard three-CNOT template
for an arbitrary point of the Weyl chamber, and just fill in its three angles.

Write the target as $\exp(i(A\,XX + B\,YY + C\,ZZ))$, so that
$A = a/2 = \pi/6$, $B = b/2 = \pi/8$ and $C = c/2 = \pi/10$. The template is

```text
rz(-pi/2)  on qubit 1
CNOT 1 -> 0
rz(2C - pi/2)  on qubit 0
ry(pi/2 - 2A)  on qubit 1
CNOT 0 -> 1
ry(2B - pi/2)  on qubit 1
CNOT 1 -> 0
rz(pi/2)   on qubit 0
```

with the three free angles evaluating to $-0.9424777960769379$,
$0.5235987755982989$ and $-0.7853981633974483$. Eight ops, depth 7, three CNOTs,
and the operator matches the target up to a global phase of $e^{i\pi/4}$.

Two structural facts make the template work. The CNOT directions alternate, which
is what lets the three interaction terms be produced independently rather than
compounding. And the two $R_z(\pm\pi/2)$ frame changes at the ends are fixed,
independent of $(a, b, c)$: only the middle three angles carry the parameters.

A useful sanity check while debugging: the canonical gate is block diagonal in
the parity basis. It never moves weight between the even subspace, spanned by
$\ket{00}$ and $\ket{11}$, and the odd one, spanned by $\ket{01}$ and $\ket{10}$.
Applied to $\ket{00}$ the reference circuit gives $0.982963$ on $\ket{00}$ and
$0.017037$ on $\ket{11}$, and exactly zero on the other two. If your circuit puts
any weight on $\ket{01}$ or $\ket{10}$ from that input, the error is structural,
not a wrong angle.

## Why the naive answer is rejected

The naive answer is three two-qubit rotations: `rxx(pi/3)`, `ryy(pi/4)`,
`rzz(pi/5)`. The three terms commute, so the product is exactly the target, and
it is also exactly a restatement of the question. The gate-set check rejects
`rxx`, `ryy` and `rzz` for that reason.

The interesting cost question is what those three rotations become once compiled.
Each $R_{PP}(\theta)$ is a CNOT, a single-qubit rotation, a CNOT, with a basis
change on both wires for $XX$ and $YY$. That is six CNOTs for the three terms,
exactly double the optimum, and the excess is not removable by peephole
simplification: the middle CNOT pair of adjacent terms does cancel, taking the
count from six to four, and four is still one more than three. Reaching three
requires the interleaved template, where the single-qubit rotations sit between
CNOTs rather than inside their own conjugation pairs.

## Why three is the lower bound

The CNOT cost of a two-qubit unitary is decided by its canonical coordinates.
Zero CNOTs reach only the local gates. One reaches the points with two
coordinates zero. Two reach the points with one coordinate zero. Everything else
needs three, and three always suffice. Here $a$, $b$ and $c$ are $\pi/3$,
$\pi/4$ and $\pi/5$, all nonzero and pairwise different, so the point is generic
and the bound is tight.

## Common wrong answers

Using $A = a$ rather than $A = a/2$ in the angle formulas is the most common
slip, and it produces a valid canonical gate at the wrong point of the chamber.
Applied to $\ket{00}$ it gives $0.933013$ and $0.066987$ where the target gives
$0.982963$ and $0.017037$: the parity structure is intact and the numbers are
off in the second decimal place, which is the signature of a factor of two in
the angles rather than a broken circuit.

Reversing the CNOT directions while keeping the angles is the other one. The
template is not symmetric under that swap, and the result is not the canonical
gate at any point of the chamber: it is a different unitary that no relabelling
of $a$, $b$ and $c$ repairs. If your operator is close but not equal, check the
angles; if it is nowhere near, check the directions.
