## The intended solution

Think of the single excitation as a unit of water poured onto qubit 0 and then
split down a chain.

1. `x` on qubit 0. The state is $\ket{100}$, all the excitation on the first wire.
2. Controlled $R_y(\theta_1)$ on qubit 1, controlled by qubit 0, with
   $\theta_1 = 2\arccos(1/\sqrt3) = 1.9106332362490184$. This writes
   $\cos(\theta_1/2)\ket{10} + \sin(\theta_1/2)\ket{11}$ on the first two wires,
   and $\cos(\theta_1/2) = 1/\sqrt3$ exactly.
3. CNOT from qubit 1 onto qubit 0. Wherever qubit 1 took the excitation, qubit 0
   gives its own up, turning $\ket{11}$ into $\ket{01}$. The state is now
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\ket{100} + \sqrt{\tfrac23}\ket{010}$.
4. Controlled $R_y(\pi/2)$ on qubit 2, controlled by qubit 1, splitting the
   remaining $\sqrt{2/3}$ into two equal halves of $1/\sqrt3$.
5. CNOT from qubit 2 onto qubit 1, moving the second half down to
   $\ket{001}$.

Four two-qubit gates, no ancilla, no three-wire gate, and every amplitude comes
out at $0.57735$.

The angle rule generalises: to leave a fraction $p$ of the probability behind,
use $\theta = 2\arccos\sqrt p$. Here the first split leaves $1/3$ and the second
leaves $1/2$ of what remains.

## Why the naive answer is too deep

The textbook route to an arbitrary three-qubit state is a full state-preparation
tree: a uniformly controlled rotation on each level, which for three qubits means
one $R_y$, two singly controlled $R_y$s and four doubly controlled $R_y$s. Each
doubly controlled rotation decomposes into two CNOTs and two singly controlled
rotations, so the total lands near a dozen two-qubit gates and depth in the
teens. It produces the right state and blows the budget of four.

The other tempting shortcut is to prepare $\ket{+++}$ and try to project onto the
weight-one subspace. There is no unitary that does this. Post-selection would
work and is not available: the judge simulates a closed circuit with no
measurement.

## Common wrong answers

The single most common failure is $\theta_1 = 2\arcsin(1/\sqrt3) = 1.2309594$,
which leaves $2/3$ on qubit 0 instead of $1/3$ and produces amplitudes
$0.8165, 0.4082, 0.4082$. It is still a legal state, it is still supported on
weight-one strings, and it is not the W state. Using $\pi/3$ for either angle
fails the same way. Because all three target amplitudes are equal, a plot of the
probabilities is the fastest possible check: if the three bars are not level,
the arccosine is wrong.
